# Options

An option is a financial derivative that gives its holder an asymmetrical exposure to the performance of an underlying asset (the underlier). Specifically, an option is contract that grants its holder the right, but not the obligation, to trade the underlier at a predetermined *strike price* $K$. For a European-style option, this right may by exercised only at the expiration time $T$, whereas for an American-style option, it may be exercised at any time up to and including $T$. In exchange for this right, the option holder pays the seller (or writer) a premium.

There are many types of options, but call and put options are the fundamental ones. A call option gives its holder the right, but not the obligation to buy the underlier at the strike price $K$. At expiration $T$, its payoff is

$$
  \Pi_T^\text{call} = N (S_T - K)^+
$$

where $S_T$ denotes the price of the underlier at $T$, and $N$ denotes the number of units of the underlier covered by the option.

Similarly, a put option gives it holder the right, but not the obligation to sell the underlier at the strike price $K$. Its payoff at expiration $T$

$$
  \Pi_T^\text{put} = N (K - S_T)^+
$$

A general distinction goes between vanilla and exotic options. Vanilla options are standard option contracts with conventional payoff structures. The fundamental vanilla options are calls and puts. Options with non-standard or more complex payoff structures are called exotic options.

Options have a wide range of applications, including:
- **Hedging:** Options can be used to protect existing exposures in the underlier. For example, an investor with a long position in the underlier can purchase a put option to establish a floor on the value of that position. Conversely, a short position in the underlier can be hedged by purchasing a call option, which limits the loss that can arise from an increase in the underlier's price.
- **Leverage:** A call option can provide exposure to increases in the underlier's price while requiring an initial cash outlay that is typically smaller than the cost of purchasing the underlier outright. For example, rather than investing $N S_0$ to acquire $N$ units of an asset, an investor may purchase a call on $N$ units by paying a premium that may be substantially smaller than $N S_0$. This creates leveraged exposure, but the two investments are not equivalent: the call has a nonlinea
- **Directional exposure with limited downside:** Suppose an investor has a bearish view on the underlier over a given horizon. A short position in the underlier or a short forward position generates a payoff that decreases as $S_T$ increases and therefore carries potentially substantial losses if the market moves against the position. By contrast, purchasing a put option provides bearish exposure while limiting the holder's maximum loss to the premium paid (ignoring financing and transaction costs). The put therefore combines directional exposure with explicit downside protection.
- **Expressing specific market view:** Because options have nonlinear payoffs, combinations of calls, puts, and positions in the underlier can be constructed to express particular views about the direction, magnitude, or distribution of future price movements. Such combinations, commonly referred to as option strategies or option spreads, allow an investor to tailor the payoff profile to a specific market scenario and investment horizon.

## Option value

Let $S_t$ denote the price of the underlier at time $t$. An the market-to-market (MtM) value of an option, or simply option price, is the current market value at which the option can be bought or sold prior to its expiration. At inception, the option's market price is called the option premium. 

For a standard vanilla option, its market price can be conceptually decomposed into two components

$$
  \text{option price} = \text{intrinsic value} + \text{time value}
$$

**Intrinsic value**
The intrinsic value of an option at time $t$ is the value that would be obtained by exercising the option immediately, assuming immediate exercise is permitted. For a call and put, respectively, the intrinsic value is given by

$$
\begin{align*}
  I_t^\text{call} =& (S_t - K)^+ \\
  I_t^\text{put} =& (K - S_t)^+
\end{align*}
$$

For a European-style option, which cannot be exercised before its expiration, intrinsic value should be interpreted as the immediate-exercise value rather than as an amount that can actually be realized at time $t$.

**Time value**
The time value (or extrinsic value) of an option is the portion of its market price that exceeds its intrinsic value. Denoting the market price of the optiona at time $t$ by $V_t$, its time value is

$$
  \mathrm{TV}_t = V_t - I_t
$$

Time value reflects, among other factors, the possibility that the option may become more valuable before expiration as the underlier price, volatility, interest rates, or other relevant market variables evolve. For a standard option in a frictionless setting with no arbitrage, the time value vanishes at expiration, so that

$$
\begin{align*}
  V_T^\text{call} =& (S_T - K)^+ \\
  V_T^\text{pu} =& (K - S_T)^+
\end{align*}
$$

### Option sensitivities

The dynamics of an option's value can be described either in terms of calendar time $t$ or time to maturity $\tau = T - t$, where $T$ is the expiration time. Since $T$ is fixed, time to maturity decreases one-for-one with the passage of calendar time, i.e. $\d\tau = -\d t$.

The delta of an option measures the sensitivity of its value to changes in the price of the underlier. Let $V(S, t)$ denote the value of an option as a function of the underlier price $S$ and calendar time $t$. The delta at time $t$ is defined as 

$$
\begin{equation*}
  \Delta_t = \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} (S_t, t)
\tag{\label{equation-3}}
\end{equation*}
$$

Thus, for a sufficiently small change $\Delta S$ in the underlier price, and all other model inputs fixed, the corresponding first-order change in the option value is $\Delta V \approx \Delta_t \Delta S$.

The theta of an option measures the sensitivity of its value to the passage of time, holding the other model inputs fixed. Let $V(S,\tau)$ denoted the value of an option as a function of the underlier price $S$ and time to maturity $\tau$. The sensitivity to time maturity is given by

$$
  \frac{\partial V}{\partial\tau} (S_t, \tau_t)
$$

Because $\d\tau = -\d t$, the corresponding sensitivity to calendar time is

$$
  \frac{\partial V}{\partial\tau} (S_t, \tau_t) = -\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} (S_t, t)
$$

If we define theta with respect to calendar time as

$$
  \theta_t = \frac{\partial V}{\partial t} (S_t, t) = -\frac{\partial V}{\partial\tau} (S_t, \tau_t)
$$

then a small increment $\Delta t$ in calendar time produces the first-order change $\Delta V \approx \Theta_t \Delta t$, provided all other model inputs are held fixed.

An alternative convention is to define theta in terms of time to maturity

$$
  \theta_t = \frac{\partial V}{\partial \tau} (S_t, \tau_t) = -\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} (S_t, t)
$$

which differ only by a minus sign.

### Moneyness

The moneyness of an options is classified as follows
- **In-the-money (ITM):** The option has strictly positive intrinsic value, i.e. $S_t > K$ for a call and $S_t < K$ for a put.
- **At-the-money (ATM):** The option has zero intrinsic value and the underlier price equals the strike, $K = S_t$.
- **Out-of-the-money (OTM):** The option has zero intrinsic value and is not AMT, i.e. $S_t < K$ for a call and $S_t > K$ for a put.

Moneyness is therefore time-dependent: an option can move between ITM, ATM and OTM as the priceof the underlier changes.

At inception, it is also common to classify the strike relative to a reference price. Two commonly used conventions are at-the-money spot (ATMS) and at-the-money forward (ATMF).
- an option is ATMS if $K = S_0$
- an option is ATMF if $K = F_{0,T}$, where $F_{0,T}$ denotes the forward price of the underlier for maturity $T$, observed at inception

In general $F_{0,T} \neq S_0$, because the forward price incorporates relevant financing, carry, income, and other market-specific effects of the period from $0$ to $T$. Consequently, an option may be ATMF without being ATMS, and vice versa.

The terms ITM and OTM can also be used to describe the option's moneyness at inception. Specifically, an option is ITM at inception if 
- $K < S_0$ for a call
- $K > S_0$ for a put

and OTM at inception if
- $K > S_0$ for a call
- $K < S_0$ for a put

When $K = S_0$, the option is ATM spot at inception.

### Notional amount

The notional amount of an option determines the scale of its payoff. The precise convention depends on the type of option and the market in which it is traded.

A common convention is to specify the notional amount as a number of units of the underlier. If $N$ denotes the number of units, the terminal payoff is

$$
\begin{align*}
  \Pi_T^\text{call} =& N (S_T - K)^+ \\
  \Pi_T^\text{put} =& N (K - S_T)^+ \\
\end{align*}
$$

Alternatively, particularly in structured products, the notional may be specified as a monetary amount. In this convention, the payoff is often expressed in terms of the return of the underlier rather than in units of the underlying asset. For example, a monetary notional $N$ applied to an ATMS call may give the terminal payoff

$$
  \Pi_T^\text{call} = N \left(\frac{S_T}{S_0} - 1 \right)^+
$$

Here, $N$ has units of currency, whereas in the first convention $N$ represents a number of units of the underlier. The two conventions therefore generate different monetary payoffs unless the respective notionals are appropriately related.

## Vanilla options

Vanilla options are standard, relatively simple option contracts with conventional payoff structures. The fundamental vanilla options are calls and puts, typically written on equities, equity indices, FX rates, commodities, or interest rates. The term vanilla is not, however, a strict synonym for liquid: an option may have a standard payoff structure without being particularly liquid.

For many actively traded vanilla options, market prices or implied volatilities are quoted using conventions based on a standard pricing framework. In the case of equity and some other asset classes, the Black–Scholes–Merton model provides the classical reference framework. In practice, however, market-standard pricing models and volatility conventions differ across asset classes; for example, FX and interest-rate options commonly use conventions and models adapted to their respective markets.

### Equity options

Vanilla equity options are call and put options of either European or American style written on a single stock or an equity index. Equity index options are actively traded on many exchanges and generally have substantial trading volumes. Single-stock options are also exchange-listed in several market, although trading activity varies considerably across stocks and regions.

Settlement conventions depend on the particular contract. For example, many exchange-listed single-stock options in the United Stated are American-style and physically settled, meaning that exercise results in the delivery of the underlying shares. Equity index optoins are typically cash-settled, since an index itself cannot be physically delivered. The precise exercise and settlement conventions are contract-specific.

Equity options are generally quoted by specifying the strike and expiration. Depending on the market convention, the strike may be expressed in absolute price terms or relative to a reference level. For index options and structured equity products, it is also common to describe strikes in terms of their moneyness, for example as a percentage of the spot or forward level.

### Foreign exchange (FX) options

A vanilla foreign exchange option is a call or put option on an exchange rate. Let $X_t$ denote the number of units of currency $2$ ($\text{ccy}2$) per unit of currency $1$ ($\text{ccy}1$), so that the FX quotation is expressed as 

$$
\begin{equation*}
  X_t = \frac{\text{ccy}1}{\text{ccy}2}
\tag{\label{equation-1}}
\end{equation*}
$$

A call on $\text{ccy}1$ gives the holder the right to buy currency $1$ and pay for it in currency $2$ at the strike exchange rate $K$. If the option has a notional $N$ units of currency $1$, its terminal payoff, expressed in currency $2$, is

$$
  \Pi_T^\text{call, ccy1} = N (X_T - K)^+
$$

Similarly, a put on currency $1$ gives the holder the right to sell $N$ units of currency $1$ in exchange for currency $2$ at strike $K$, with terminal payoff

$$
  \Pi_T^\text{put, ccy2} = N (K - X_T)^+
$$

**FX option duality**

FX options exhibit a quotation duality arising from the fact that an exchange rate can be quoted in either direction. The inverse exchange rate of $\eqref{equation-1}$ is

$$
\begin{equation*}
  Y_t = \frac{1}{X_t} = \frac{\text{ccy}1}{\text{ccy}2}
\tag{\label{equation-2}}
\end{equation*}
$$

Consider an $N$-unit call on $\text{ccy}1$, with strike $K$ in units of $\text{ccy}2$ per unit of $\text{ccy}1$. Its payoff in currency $2$ is $N ( X_T - K)^+$. Using $\eqref{equation-2}$, the terminal payoff can be expressed as

$$
  N (X_T - K)^+ = N K X_T \left(\frac{1}{K} - Y_T \right)
$$

so, after accounting for the appropriate currency conversion, the same economic exposure can be represented as a put on the inverse FX rate with strike $1/K$. Specifically, the corresponding option is a put on $\text{ccy}2$, with notional $KN$ in currency $2$ and strike $1/K$ in units of $\text{ccy}1$ per unit of $\text{ccy}2$.

Thus, an option to buy $N$ units of $\text{ccy}1$ against $KN$ units of $\text{ccy}2$ at exchange rate $K$ can equivalently be described, under the inverse quotation, as an option to sell $KN$ units of $\text{ccy}2$ against $N$ units of $\text{ccy}1$ at exchange rate $1/K$.

**FX option quotation**

In the FX options market, vanilla options are commonly quoted in terms of delta rather than directly by strike. Delta is calculated using the relevant FX option pricing convention, typically based on a Black–Scholes/Garman–Kohlhagen framework. Standard market quotations often include:

- ATMF (at-the-money forward);
- $25\%$-delta options; and
- $10\%$-delta options.

The precise definition of FX delta—such as spot delta, forward delta, or premium-adjusted delta—is market- and currency-pair-dependent, so the quotation convention must be specified when interpreting an FX volatility quote.

### Commodity options

Depending on the commodity and market convention, a vanilla commodity option may be written directly on the spot price of the commodity or, more commonly in exchange-traded markets, on a futures contract. The precise underlying, exercise style, and settlement mechanism are contract-specific.

For an option written with notional $N$ on a futures price $F_t$, the terminal payoff for strike $K$ is

$$
  \Pi_T^\text{call} = N (F_T - K)^+
$$

### Interes rate options

Two fundamental classes of vanilla interest-rate options are caps/floors and swaptions.

#### Caps and floors

Consider an accrual period $(T_{i-1}, T_i)$ with year fraction $\tau_i$ and a reference interest rate $R_i$ applying to that period. A caplet is a call option on the reference interest rate. With notional $N$ and strike $K$, its payoff, typically paid at $T_i$ is

$$
  \Pi_{T_i}^\text{caplet} = N\tau_i (R_i - K)^+
$$

Thus, the caplet holder receives a payment when the reference rate exceeds the strike $K$. The exact definition of $R_i$, its fixing date, and the payment date depend on the underlying rate and market convention.

An interest-rate cap is a portfolio of caplets covering a sequence of accrual periods. If all caplets have the same strike $K$, its cash flow in period $i$ is

$$
  \Pi_{T_i}^\text{cap} = N\tau_i (R_i - K)
$$

and the collection cash flows is given by

$$
  \Pi^\text{cap} = N \sum_{i=1}^m \tau_i (R_i - K)^+
$$

Similarly, a floorlet is a put option on the reference interest rate. It payoff at $T_i$ is

$$
  \Pi_{T_i}^\text{floorlet} = N \tau_i (K - R_i)^+
$$

An interest-rate floor is a portfolio of floorlets with cash flow at $T_i$ given by

$$
  \Pi_{T_i}^\text{floor} = N \tau_i (K - R_i)^+
$$

The colloction of cash flows is the sum

$$
  \Pi^\text{floor} = N \sum_{i=1}^m \tau_i (K - R_i)^+
$$

Hence, the cap and floor can be viewed as collections of individual options on the relevant reference rates, each associated with a particular accrual period. Market quotations for caps and floors are commonly expressed in terms of strike and maturity, although conventions can vary by currency and reference rate.

#### Swaption

A swaption is an option that gives the holder the right, but not the obligation to enter into and interest-rate swap (IRS) at a specified future date. Consider a swaption expiring at $T$, where the underlying interest-rate swap begins at $T$ and matures at $T_m$. The holder has the right to enter into this swap at the predermined fixed rate $K$.

Let $s(T, T_m)$ denote the par swap rate at the option epiration date $T$, and let $A(T, T_m)$ denote the present value at $T$ of one unit of the fixed-leg annuity of the underlying swap. For a payer swaption, the value at expiration is

$$
  \Pi_T^\text{payer} = N A(T, T_m) (s(T, T_m) - K)^+
$$

For a receiver swaption, the corresponding payoff is

$$
  \Pi_T^\text{receiver} = N A(T, T_m) (K - s(T, T_m))^+
$$

The factor $A(T, T_m)$ converts the difference between the market swap rate and the strike into the present value, at $T$, of the corresponding stream of fixed-versus-floating interest payments.

Here $N$ is the notional of the underlying interest-rate swap. The option tenor is the period from inception to the expiration date $T$, while the underlying swap tenor is the period from $T$ to $T_m$. Thus, it is useful to distinguish the swaptions' option maturity from the maturity of the underlying swap.

Swaptions are typically quoted by specifying the option expiry, the underlying swap tenor, and the relevant strike or volatility measure. For example, a quotation may refer to a swaption with a 5-year option expiry into a 10-year underlying swap. The precise quotation convention—strike, premium, or implied volatility—depends on the market and currency.

### Option pricing and hedging

**Option hedging**

In an idealized complete, frictionless market, the no-arbitrage value of a contingent claim can be interpreted as the cost of dynamically replicating its payoff. Thus, the option's price is equal to the initial cost of a self-financing trading strategy that reproduces the option's terminal payoff.

A delta-hedging strategy dynamically adjusts the position in the underlier according to the option's delta $\eqref{equation-3}$. For example, if the option has delta $\Delta_t$, a position of $-\Delta_t$ units of the underlier, together with the appropriate position in the risk-free asset, locally offsets the option's first-order exposure to movement in the underlier price.

It is important to distinguish the option price from its hedging cost in a general market. The equality between the two is a consequence of the no-arbitrage and completeness assumptions underlying the replication argument; in real markets, transaction costs, discrete rebalancing, liquidity constraints, model risk, and imperfect hedging mean that an option's market price need not equal the realized cost of hedging it ex post.

**Vanilla option pricing**

The Black-Scholes-Merton (BSM) model is the classical framework for the pricing and delta hedging of European options. Under this model, the underlier price follows a geometric Browian motion 

$$
  \frac{d S_t}{S_t} = \mu \d t + \sigma \d W_t
$$

where $\mu$ is the drift of the underlier under the chosen probability measure, $\sigma > 0$ is its constant volatility, and $W_t$ is a standard Wiener process. Under the risk-neutral measure used for pricing, and in the absence of dividends, the drift becomes the continuously compounded risk-free rate $r$

$$
  \frac{d S_t}{S_t} = r \d t + \sigma \d W_t^\mathbb{Q}
$$

The BSM model applies to vanilla equity options, but has been adapted to other asset classes. In particular, the Garman-Kohlhagen model is the standard extension for FX options, while the Black model is widely used for options on forwards and futures, including many interest-rate derivatives.

For a European vanilla, the classical Black-Scholes (BS) formulas give the no-arbitrage call and put prices under the assumptions of the BSM model. For a dividend-free underlier, with current spot price $S_0$, strike $K$ and time to maturity $T$, the call and put prices are

$$
\begin{equation*}
\begin{split}
  c =& S_0 N(d_1) - K e^{-rT} N(d_2) \\
  p =& K e^{-rT} N (-d_2) - S_0 N(-d_1)
\end{split}
\tag{\label{equation-4}}
\end{equation*}
$$

where

$$
  d_1 = \frac{\ln(S_0/K) + (r + \sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}
$$

and

$$
  d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T} = \frac{\ln(S_0/K) + (r - \sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}
$$

Here $N(\cdot)$ denotes the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal distribution

$$
  N(z) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \int_{-\infty}^z \exp\left(-\frac{x^2}{2} \right) \;\d x
$$

The formulas $\eqref{equation-4}$ assumes zero dividends or other carry on the underlier. If the underlier pays a continuous dividend yield $q$, the corresponding formulas become

$$
\begin{equation*}
\begin{split}
  c =& S_0 e^{-qT} N(d_1) - K e^{-rT} N(d_2) \\
  p =& K e^{-rT} N (-d_2) - S_0 e^{-qT} N(-d_1)
\end{split}
\tag{\label{equation-4}}
\end{equation*}
$$

with 

$$
  d_1 = \frac{\ln(S_0/K) + (r - q + \sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}
$$

and $d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T}$. Thus, in the BSM framework, the option value depends on the spot price $S_0$, strike $K$, time to maturity $T$, interest rate $r$, and volatility $\sigma$, together with any relevant carry such as a dividend yield $q$.

Among these parameters, volatility is particularly important for option traders and risk managers, because it is not directly observable in the same way as spot or interest rates and has a substantial effect on option values. For fixed $S_0$, $K$, $T$, and $r$, the price of a standard European call or put is a strictly increasing function of volatility. Consequently, there is a one-to-one relationship between the option price and its implied volatility. The implied volatility is the value of $\sigma$ that, when substituted into the relevant pricing model, reproduces the observed market price.

### Implied volatility

Implied volatility is the value of the volatility parameters $\sigma$ that, with all other relevant model inputs held fixed, makes a pricing model reproduce the observed market price of an option. In the Black-Scholes-Merton framework, the implied volatility $\sigma_\text{i}$ is defined implicitly by

$$
  V^\text{BSM} (S_0, K, T, r, \sigma_\text{i}) = V^\text{market}
$$

where $V^\text{market}$ is the observed market price.

It is important to distinguish realized volatility, which describes the historical or future variability of the underlier, from implied volatility, which is a model-dependent quantity inferred from option prices.

In the basic BSM model, a single constant volatility parameter $\sigma$ is assumed for a given underlier. Consequently, the model would imply the same volatility for all strikes and maturities. In observed markets, however, the implied volatility inferred from option prices generally varies with both strike and maturity. Thus, implied volatility should more generally be viewed as a function $\sigma_\text{i} (K, T)$.

For a fixed maturity, the dependence of implied volatility on strike is known as the volatility smile or, more generally, the volatility skew/smile. Across maturities, the corresponding dependence is referred to as the volatility term structure.

#### Volatility smile 

There are several complementary explanations for the observed strike dependence of implied volatility

- **Limitations of the BSM model.** The BSM model assumes that the underlier follows a geometric Brownian motion with constant volatility. Under this assumption, the terminal price is lognormally distributed. Empirical equity-return distributions, however, typically exhibit features that are inconsistent with this assumption, including heavier tails and, in many cases, significant skewness. A single constant volatility therefore cannot simultaneously reproduce market prices across all strikes. By allowing volatility to vary with strike, practitioners can reproduce a broader cross-section of observed option prices.

  This adjustment should not be interpreted as implying that the resulting volatility parameter represents the true future volatility of the underlier. Rather, it is a model-implied parameter that summarizes the market prices of options with different strikes. In particular, simply replacing the constant BSM volatility by a strike-dependent implied volatility does not, by itself, define a complete stochastic model for the dynamics of the underlier or its volatility. Consequently, such a procedure is generally insufficient for consistently pricing path-dependent derivatives. More sophisticated models, such as local-volatility or stochastic-volatility models, are typically required when the dynamics of the entire implied-volatility surface are relevant.

- **Supply and demand.** Option prices also reflect the balance of supply and demand across strikes. For example, institutional investors with substantial long equity exposure may demand OTM put options to protect against large downward moves in equity markets. Strong demand for such protection can increase the prices of OTM puts and therefore their implied volatilities. This mechanism is one contributing factor to the typically higher implied volatility observed for lower-strike equity index puts.

The shape of the smile is not universal, however. Single-stock options can exhibit different patterns from broad equity-index options, and the skew can vary substantially across stocks, maturities, and market conditions. In some markets or for some individual securities, implied volatility can even increase with strike over part of the relevant range.

#### Dynamics of the volatility smile

The dynamics of the implied-volatility surface describe how implied volatility changes as the underlier price and other market variables evolve. Two simplified descriptions are commonly used to characterize the movement of the smile when the underlier price changes while the overall level of expected volatility is assumed to remain approximately stable.
- **Sticky strike.** The implied volatility associated with each fixed strike remains approximately unchanged as the underlier price moves. In other words,

$$
  \sigma_\text{i} (K, S_t) \approx \sigma_\text{i} (K, S_0)
$$

for fixed $K$. The smile is therefore anchored to absolute strike levels. This assumption can be a useful approximation in certain market conditions, although it is not a fundamental property of option prices.

- **Sticky delta.** The implied volatility associated with a given option delta remains approximately unchanged as the underlier price moves. In this case, the smile effectively moves with the underlier, since the strike corresponding to a given delta changes as the spot or forward price changes. This convention is particularly natural in markets such as FX, where volatility surfaces are frequently quoted in delta space.

Neither assumption is universally correct. They are simplified rules for describing smile dynamics, and the actual evolution of the volatility surface is an empirical property of the market.

#### Forward volatility and variance additivity

Suppose $0 = t_0 < t_1 < t_2$. If $\sigma_1$ and $\sigma_2$ denote the corresponding constant-equivalent implied volatilities for maturities $t_1$ and $t_2$, then, under the assumption that total variance is additive across non-overlapping time intervals

$$
  \sigma_2^2 t_2 = \sigma_1^2 t_1 + \sigma^2 (t_1, t_2) (t_2 - t_1)
$$

where $\sigma(t_1, t_2)$ is the forward volatility associated with the interval $[t_1, t_2]$. Hence

$$
\begin{equation*}
  \sigma^2 (t_1, t_2) = \frac{\sigma_2^2 t_2 - \sigma_1^2 t_1}{t_2 - t_1}
\tag{\label{equation-5}}
\end{equation*}
$$

The key object being interpolated or extrapolated here is total variance, not volatility itself. This follows from the fact that, under the usual constant-volatility diffusion framework, variance accumulates linearly with time.

Forward volatility is useful, for example, in pricing forward-starting options, whose strike or moneyness is determined at a future date.

If $t_1 < t < t_2$, a simple piecewise-constant-forward-volatility interpolation assumes that the forward volatility over $[t_1, t]$ is equal to the forward volatility over the entire interval $[t_1, t_2]$. The total variance accumulated up to $t$ is then

$$
  \sigma^2 (t) t = \sigma_1^2 t_1 + \sigma^2 (t_1, t_2) (t - t_1)
$$

Substituting in $\eqref{equation-5}$ gives

$$
  \sigma^2 (t) t = \sigma_1^2 t_1 + \frac{t - t_1}{t_2 - t_1} (\sigma_2^2 t_2 - \sigma_1^2 t_1)
$$

Equivalently,

$$
  \sigma^2 (t) t = [1 - w(t)] \sigma_1^2 t_1 + w(t) \sigma_2^2 t_2
$$

where

$$
  w(t) = \frac{t - t_1}{t_2 - t_1}
$$

Thus, this interpolation scheme performs a linear interpolation of total variance between two maturities. The resulting implied volatility is then recovered as

$$
  \sigma(t) = \sqrt{\frac{[1 - w(t)] \sigma_1^2 t_1 + w(t) \sigma_2^2 t_2}{t}}
$$

This is only one possible interpolation method. In practice, volatility surfaces are typically interpolated using conventions designed to preserve desirable properties such as smoothness and, where required, absence of static arbitrage.

derived (with other parameters fixed) from an option price quoted in the market. For a given underlier, although its volatility has a unique value in the BSM model, its implied volatility may be different for different tenors and option strikes. For a given tenor, the implied volatilities from the options with different strike prices show a pattern known as volatility smile.

#### FX volatility quotations

In the FX options market, volatility is commonly quoted using delta-based conventions rather than directly specifying strikes. For a given maturity, a typical quotation includes the at-the-money-forward (ATMF) volatility together with risk reversals and butterflies.

The ATMF volatility, denoted $\sigma_\text{ATMF}$, corresponds to a strike equal to the forward ATM strike, subject to the relevant FX market convention.

A 25-delta risk reversal is defined as

$$
  25\Delta\text{RR} = \sigma_{25\Delta\text{call}} - \sigma_{25\Delta\text{put}}
$$

It measures the assymmetry of implied volatility between the 25-delta call and put.

A 25-delta butterfly (or 25-delta strangle) is defined by

$$
  25\Delta\text{BF} = \frac{1}{2} (\sigma_{25\Delta\text{call}} + \sigma_{25\Delta\text{put}}) - \sigma_\text{ATMF}
$$

Given these three market quotes, the two wing volatilities can be recovered as

$$
\begin{align*}
  \sigma_{25\Delta\text{call}} =& \sigma_\text{ATMF} + 25\Delta\text{BF} + \frac{1}{2} 25\Delta\text{RR} \\
  \sigma_{25\Delta\text{put}} =& \sigma_\text{ATMF} + 25\Delta\text{BF} - \frac{1}{2} 25\Delta\text{RR}
\end{align*}
$$

The corresponding implied volatilities at other delta levels can then be obtained by interpolation according to the market's specified volatility-surface convention.

For fixed spot, forward, maturity, interest rates, and other model inputs, there is a one-to-one relationship between an option's strike and its delta under the standard Black-type pricing framework. Consequently, a volatility quoted as a function of delta can be transformed into an implied-volatility representation as a function of strike.

### Put-call parity

Let $c_t (T, K)$ and $p_t (T-K)$ denote, respectively, the time-$t$ prices of a European call and put with common strike $K$ and maturity $T$. Assume that the two options have identical contractual terms other than the right to buy or sell the underlier.

In the case of a zero dividend underlier, the put-call parity states that

$$
  c_t (T, K) - p_t (T, K) = S_t^\text{fwd} (T) - Ke^{-r(T - t)}
$$

where $S_t^\text{fwd} (T)$ denotes the relevant forward price of the underlier for maturity $T$. Equivalently, if $F_t (T)$ denotes the forward price

$$
  c_t (T, K) - p_t (T, K) = e^{-r(T - t)} (F_t(T) - K)
$$

More generally, it is useful to define the value of a foward contract with delivery price $K$ by

$$
  f_t (T, K) = e^{-r(T - t)} (F_t (T) - K)
$$

Then put-call parity can be written compactly as

$$
\begin{equation*}
  c_t (T, K) - p_t (T, K) = f_t (T, K),\, t \leq T
\tag{\label{equation-25}}
\end{equation*}
$$

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>

The result follows directly from the terminal payoff. At maturity, we have

$$
\begin{equation*}
  (S_T - K)^+ - (K - S_T)^+ = S_T - K,\, \forall T > 0
\tag{\label{equation-7}}
\end{equation*}
$$

Thus, a long call combined with a short put has exactly the same terminal payoff as a forward contract to buy the underlier for $K$ at $T$. By the no-arbitrage principle, two portfolios with identical cash flows must have the same value at every earlier time $t$.
</details>

If put-call parity were violated, we could construct an arbitrage by taking a long position in the cheaper portfolio and a short position in the more expensive portfolio, with the initial cash flows offsetting and the terminal cash flows cancelling exactly.

For a non-dividend-paying stock, if the relevant repo or financing carry is zero, the forward price is $F_t = S_t - e^{-r(T-t)} K$, so that $f_t (T, K) = S_t - Ke^{-r(T - t)}$. In this case, the put-call parity becomes

$$
  c_t (T, K) - p_t (T, K) = S_t - K e^{-r(T - t)}
$$

or, equivalently

$$
  c_t (T, K) + Ke^{-r(T - t)} = p_t (T, K) + S_t
$$

This latter form has a direct replication interpretation: a call combined with a zero-coupn bond paying $K$ at $T$ has the same payoff as a put combined with one unit of the underlier. If the underlier pays a continuous dividend yield $q$, the corresponding relation becomes

$$
\begin{equation*}
  c_t - p_t = S_t e^{-q(T-t)} - Ke^{-r(T-t)}
\tag{\label{equation-6}}
\end{equation*}
$$

More generally, the appropriate carry and discounting conventions depend on the asset class.

**Implication for implied volatility**

Put–call parity imposes a strong consistency condition on the implied volatilities of European calls and puts. Under a given arbitrage-free pricing model, if a call and put have the same strike $K$, maturity $T$, and underlying, and are priced using the same market inputs for rates and carry, then they must have the same implied volatility. Indeed, the difference between their prices $\eqref{equation-6}$ contains no volatility-dependent component.

This also implies that the vega of the call and put must be identical. For example, in the Black-Scholes-Merton model with continuous dividend yield $q$ the call and put vega are

$$
\begin{align*}
  \text{Vega}_\text{call} =& \frac{\partial c}{\partial\sigma} = S_t e^{-q(T - t)} \phi(d_1) (T - t)^{1/2} \\
  \text{Vega}_\text{put} =& \frac{\partial p}{\partial\sigma} = S_t e^{-q(T - t)} \phi(d_1) (T - t)^{1/2}
\end{align*}
$$

so that $\text{Vega}_\text{call} = \text{Vega}_\text{put}$.

Put–call parity therefore provides a particularly clear example of option decomposition: although a call and a put are individually nonlinear contingent claims, their difference eliminates the optionality,

$$
  \text{call} - \text{put} = \text{forward}
$$

because their nonlinear payoff components cancel as shown in $\eqref{equation-7}$.

For interest-rate derivatives, the analogous relationship is given by

$$
  \text{cap} - \text{floor} = \text{payer swap}
$$

provided the cap and floor have matching notionals, accrual periods, strikes, and reference rates. More precisely, this is an equality of the corresponding streams of cash flows: each caplet minus the corresponding floorlet produces the cash flow of a payer swap for that accrual period

$$
  N\tau_i (R_i - K)^+ - N \tau_i (K - R_i)^+ = N\tau_i (R_i - K)
$$

Summing across all periods gives the cash flows of the corresponding payer interest-rate swap.

### European option strategies

European options can be used to express specific views on the future price of an underlier. More generally any sufficiently regular European payoff at a fixed maturity can be represented, or approximated arbitrarily well, using a portfolio of vanilla calls, puts, and a position in the underlier and/or a risk-free asset.

The basic building blocks in option strategies are call and put payoffs. For a unit notional $N = 1$, their terminal payoffs are

$$
  \Pi_T^\text{call} = (S_T - K)^+,\quad \Pi_T^\text{put} = (K - S_T)^+
$$

As functions of $S_T$, these are piecewise-linear functions with slopes $\pm 1$ on their respective in-the-money regions and slope $0$ elsewhere. By combining options with different strikes and notionals, we can construct a wide range of piecewise-linear payoff profiles. In the limit, sufficiently regular nonlinear payoff can be represented using a continuum of vanilla options. This observation underlies the static replication of European contingent claims.

Below are some of the most common vanilla option strategies

#### Call spread

A call spread, or vertical bull call spread, consists of
- long one call with strike $K_1$
- short one call with strike $K_2$ 

with $K_1 < K_2$. Its payoff is

$$
  \Pi_T = (S_T - K_1)^+ - (S_T - K_2)^2
$$

Equivalently,

$$
  \Pi_T = \begin{cases}
    0,\quad& S_T \leq K_1 \\
    S_T - K_1,\quad& K_1 < S_T < K_2 \\
    K_2 - K_1,\quad& S_T \geq K_2
  \end{cases}
$$

The strategy provides a bullish exposure over the interval $[K_1, K_2]$, while limiting both its maximum profit and its cost relative to a standalone long call. When the two strikes are close, the value of the spread is particularly sensitive to the relative implied volatilities at the two strikes, and therefore to the local shape of the volatility smile or skew, rather than simply to the overall volatility level.

#### Put spread

A put spread, or vertical bear put spread, consists of
- long one put with strike $K_1$
- short one put with strike $K_2$

with $K_1 > K_2$. Its payoff is

$$
  \Pi_T = (K_1 - S_T)^+ - (K_2 - S_T)^+
$$

The position provides bearish exposure over the interval $[K_2, K_1]$, while limiting the maximum profit and reducing the premium relative to purchasing the $K_1$ put outright

#### Risk reversal

A risk reversal combines options with opposite directions and typically different strikes. A common structure is
- long one OTM call with strike $K_\text{call}$
- short one OTM put with strke $K_\text{put}$

with $K_\text{put} < S_0 < K_\text{call}$. The strikes are often selected so that the initial new premium is approximately zero. The resulting position provides asymmetric directional exposure: it benefits from a sufficiently large upward move and loses value if the underlier falls sufficiently far.

#### Butterfly

A standard call butterfly consists of
- long one call at $K_1$
- short two calls at $K_2$
- long one call at $K_3$

where $K_1 < K_2 < K_3$ and

$$
  K_2 = \frac{K_1 + K_3}{2}
$$

Its payoff is

$$
  \Pi_T = (S_T - K_1)^+ - 2(S_T - K_2)^+ + (S_T - K_3)^+
$$

The payoff is maximized when $S_T = K_2$ and vanishes when $S_T \leq K_1$ or $S_T \geq K_3$. Thus, a long butterfly profits when the underlier finishes near the middle strike $K_2$, relative to the initial cost of the strategy.

#### Collar

A collar typically consists of 
- a long position in the underlier
- a long OTM put
- a short ITM call

with the put strike below the current underlier price and the call strike above it. The put establishes a downside floor, while the short call limits the upside beyond the call strike. The premium received from selling the call can partially or fully offset the cost of the protective put.

#### Straddle

A long straddle consists of
- one long call
- one long put

with the same strike $K$ and maturity $T$. The strike is typically chosen close to the ATM level. Its payoff is

$$
\begin{align*}
  \Pi_T =& (S_T - K)^+ + (K - S_T)^+ \\
  =& |S_T - K|
\end{align*}
$$

The strategy therefore benefits from a sufficiently large move in either direction and is primarily an expression of a view that the realized movement of the underlier will exceed that implied by the option premiums.

#### Strangle

A long strangle consists of
- one long OTM call
- one long OTM pu

with the same maturity, but different strikes $K_\text{put} < S_0 < K_\text{call}$. Its payoff is

$$
  \Pi_T = (K_\text{put} - S_T)^+ + (S_T - K_\text{call})^+
$$

#### Call ratio spread

A call ratio spread combines calls in unequal quantities. A common $1\times 2$ structure consists of
- long one call at $K_1$
- short two calls at $K_2$

with $K_1 < K_2$. Its payoff is

$$
  \Pi_T = (S_T - K_1)^+ - 2(S_T - K_2)^+
$$

Unlike a standard call spread, the short-call notional exceeds the long-call notional. Consequently, the strategy can have unlimited downside for sufficiently large increases in the underlier price. This feature is important when assessing the risk of ratio spreads.

#### Covered call

A covered call, or buy/write, consists of
- a long position in the underlier
- a short call on the same underlier, typically with an OTM strike

The strategy generates premium income from selling the call in exchange for giving up some of the upside beyond the call strike. It is commonly used when the investor has a neutral to moderately bullish view and is willing to cap the upside in exchange for option premium.

The short call is economically covered by the long underlier position: if the call is exercised, the investor can deliver the underlying asset. This reduces the need for additional collateral relative to an uncovered short call, although the precise collateral and margin treatment depends on the market and counterparty arrangement.

#### Calendar spread

A calendar spread, or horizontal spread, consists of otherwise similar options with different expiration dates. For example, a call calendar spread may involve
- buying a longer-dated call
- selling a shorter-dated call

typically with the same strike. Because the two options have different maturities, the position is particularly sensitive to the term structure of implied volatility and to the evolution of volatility over time. Calendar spreads can therefore be used to express views on the relative pricing of different maturities and, in appropriate settings, on forward volatility.

The term horizontal comes from representing maturity on the horizontal axis of a diagram, in contrast to a vertical spread, where the maturity is fixed and the strikes differ.

#### Directional trading with options

Market participants, including hedge funds, may use slightly OTM calls or puts to obtain directional exposure to an underlier. Relative to an ATM option, an OTM option generally requires a lower upfront premium, although this does not necessarily mean that it provides “cheaper” directional exposure in an economically equivalent sense. Its delta, gamma, probability of finishing ITM, and sensitivity to volatility and time decay are all different.

In many trading strategies, the option position is intended to express a view over a finite horizon rather than to be held until expiration. The position may therefore be unwound, rolled, or otherwise offset before maturity. For this reason, liquidity and transaction costs are important considerations when selecting an option. Highly liquid standardized options are generally easier to enter and exit at competitive prices than bespoke OTC or exotic options, whose secondary-market liquidity can be limited.

Thus, the choice between a vanilla and an exotic option is not determined solely by the desired terminal payoff. It also depends on the liquidity, hedging requirements, transaction costs, model risk, and expected holding period of the position.

### American options

An American option may be exercised by its holder at any time to and including its expiration date $T$. If the option can be exercised only at a specified finite set of dates (e.g., every month), it is called a Bermudan option. However, in some market contexts, options with discrete exercise opportunities are sometimes informally called American options.

For an American option, the holder must determine the optimal exercise time. At any exercise opportunity t, immediate exercise is optimal when the exercise value is at least as large as the value of retaining the option. More formally, if $E_t$ denotes the immediate exercise value and $V_t$ the value of continuing to hold the options, exercise is optimal whenever $E_t \geq V_t^\text{cont}$, where $V_t^\text{cont}$ denotes the value conditional on not exercising at $t$.

**American versus European options**

Let $c_t$ and $p_t$ denote the time-$t$ price of the European call option and put option with strike $K$ and maturity $T$, while $C_t$ and $P_t$ denote the corresponding American option prices. Due to its exercise right at the discretion of option holder, an American option's price should be at least the price of its counterpart European options, i.e.

$$
\begin{equation*}
  C_t \geq c_t,\quad P_t \geq p_t
\tag{\label{equation-8}}
\end{equation*}
$$

The inequality follows directly from the fact that the holder of the American option can always choose to exercise only at $T$. Thus, the set of admissible exercise strategies for the American option contains the European exercise strategy as a special case.

**Early exercise of an American call**

The price of an American call is equal to the price of the corresponding European call under the following conditions:
- dividend and repo rate are all zero for the underlier during the tenor of the option
- the continuously compounded risk-free interes rate $r$ is non-negative.

Under these conditions early exercise of the call is never optimal and therefore

$$
  C_t = c_t
$$

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>

We first show that the European call prices has the lower bound

$$
  c_t \geq (S_t - K e^{-r(T-t)})^+,\, \forall t < T
$$

Define two portfolios at time $t < T$
- Portfolio $A$: Long one European call $c_t$ together with a zero-coupon bond with the value $Ke^{-r(T-t)}$, so that the bond pays $K$ at $T$
- Portfolio $B$: Long one share $S_t$ with zero dividend yield

At maturity $T$, the porfolio payoffs are
- Portfolio $A$: $(S_T - K)^+ + K = \max\set{S_T, K}$
- Portfolio $B$: $S_T$

Since $\max\set{S_T, K} \geq S_T$, Portfolio $A$ dominates Portfolio $B$ at maturity. By the no-arbitrage principle

$$
  c_t + K e^{-r(T-t)} \geq S_t
$$

or, because $c_t \geq 0$

$$
\begin{equation*}
  c_t \geq (S_t - Ke^{-r(T-t)})^+
\tag{\label{equation-9}}
\end{equation*}
$$

Because $r \geq 0$, it follows that $e^{-r(T-t)} \leq 1$ and therefore

$$
  S_T - Ke^{-r(T-t)} \geq S_t - K
$$

Taking positive parts gives

$$
  (S_T - Ke^{-r(T-t)})^+ \geq (S_t - K)^+
$$

From the inequalities $\eqref{equation-8}$ and $\eqref{equation-9}$

$$
  C_t \geq c_t \geq (S_t - Ke^{-r(T-t)})^+ \geq (S_t - K)^+
$$

Because the early exercise value of the American call is $(S_t - K)^+$, exercising the call before maturity cannot be optimal. Since early exercise provides no additional value, the American and European calls have the same value, i.e.

$$
  C_t = c_t,\, \forall t < T
$$
</details>

The economic intuition is straightforward. Exercising the call early requires paying the strike $K$ before maturity and gives up the remaining optionality. If the stock pays no dividends, there is no benefit from owning the stock earlier. Moreover, when $r \geq 0$, postponing the payment of $K$ has non-negative financing value. If the holder wishes to monetize an appreciated call position before maturity, selling the option is therefore preferable to exercising it: selling preserves the remaining time value, whereas exercise destroys it.

For a given underlying, strike, and maturity, an American option provides at least as much value to its holder as the corresponding European option because it offers a larger set of exercise opportunities. Consequently,

$$
  V_t^\text{American} \geq V_t^\text{European}
$$

However, this does not imply that an American option is always economically preferable at the same quoted premium. In a frictionless market, the premium should reflect the additional value associated with the early-exercise right. Moreover, the possibility of terminating a European option before maturity should not generally be described as an “unwind” at a value below intrinsic value: the market value of a European option is itself constrained by no-arbitrage bounds and, under standard assumptions, is at least its intrinsic value. An OTC unwind may involve transaction costs, bid–ask spreads, funding costs, or other contractual adjustments, but these are separate from the fundamental distinction between American and European exercise rights.

Thus, the essential advantage of an American option is not that it guarantees a higher liquidation value at any time, but that it gives the holder additional exercise rights. The economic value of those rights is reflected in the American option's price relative to the corresponding European option.

## Exotic options

Any option that is not a vanilla option is an exotic option. Each exotic option offers specific exposure to the performance of the underlier. If a payoff is determined by more than one price of a single underlier over time, the option is called path-dependent. Actually, the proliferation of structured products and the advances in pricing techniques made it unnecessary to name each new product with an exotic and fancy name. However, well-studied exotic options named in the old days are often used for comparing/understanding a financial product’s features, risks, and pricing/hedging issues.

### Barrier options

A barrier option is a type of call or put option whose value depends not only on the underlier price at expiration, but also on whether the underlier reaches a specified barrier level during a prescribed monitoring period. Barrier options are commonly classified as
- **Knock-in:** the option becomes active only if the barrier is reached or breached during the monitoring period
- **Knock-out:** the option is terminated if the barrier is reached or breached during the monitoring period

The terms down-and-in, down-and-out, up-and-in, and up-and-out specify the direction of the barrier and the type of activation function
- **down:** the relevant event occurs when the underlier falls to or below a lower barrier $L$
- **up:** the relevant event occurs when the underlier rises to or above and upper barrier $H$
- **in:** the option is activated when the barrier is hit
- **out:** the option is extinguished when the barrier is hit

| Type | Barrier event | Payoff condition |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Down-and-in | $\min_{t\in[0,T]} S_t \leq L$ | Barrier must be hit |
| Down-and-out | $\min_{t\in[0,T]} S_t > L$ | Barrier must not be hit |
| Up-and-in | $\max_{t\in[0,T]} S_t \geq H$ | Barrier must be hit |
| Up-and-out | $\max_{t\in[0,T]} S_t < H$ | Barrier must not be hit |

**Down-and-in options**

A down-and-in option is activated if the underlier reaches or falls below the lower barrier $L$ at some point during the monitoring period. Its terminal payoff is therefore

$$
\begin{align*}
  \Pi_T^\text{DI,call} =& (S_T - K)^+ \mathbf{1}_{\set{\min_{t\in[0,T]} S_t \leq L}} \\
  \Pi_T^\text{DI,put} =& (K - S_T)^+ \mathbf{1}_{\set{\min_{t\in[0,T]} S_t \leq L}}
\end{align*}
$$

Thus, the option pays the corresponding vanilla payoff at $T$ if and only if the underlier has reached or breached $L$ during the monitoring period.

**Down-and-out options**

A down-and-out option is extinguished if the underlier reaches or falls below $L$. Its terminal payoff is

$$
\begin{align*}
  \Pi_T^\text{DO, call} =& (S_T - K)^+ \mathbf{1}_{\set{\min_{t\in[0,T]} S_t > L}} \\
  \Pi_T^\text{DO, put} =& (K - S_T)^+ \mathbf{1}_{\set{\min_{t\in[0,T]} S_t > L}}
\end{align*}
$$

Thus, the option pays the corresponding vanilla payoff only if the underlier remains strictly above $L$ throughout the monitoring period.

**Up-and-in options**

An up-and-in option is activated if the underlier reaches or exceeds the upper barrier $H$. Its terminal payoff is

$$
\begin{align*}
  \Pi_T^\text{UI, call} =& (S_T - K)^+ \mathbf{1}_{\set{\min_{t\in[0,T]} S_t \geq H}} \\
  \Pi_T^\text{UI, put} =& (K - S_T)^+ \mathbf{1}_{\set{\min_{t\in[0,T]} S_t \geq H}}
\end{align*}
$$

Thus, the option pays the corresponding vanilla payoff at $T$ if and only if the underlier has reached or breached $H$ during the monitoring period.

**Up-and-out options**

An up-and-out option is extinguished if the underlier reaches or exceeds $H$. Its terminal payoff is

$$
\begin{align*}
  \Pi_T^\text{UO, call} =& (S_T - K)^+ \mathbf{1}_{\set{\min_{t\in[0,T]} S_t < H}} \\
  \Pi_T^\text{UO, put} =& (K - S_T)^+ \mathbf{1}_{\set{\min_{t\in[0,T]} S_t < H}}
\end{align*}
$$

Thus, the option pays the corresponding vanilla payoff only if the underlier remains strictly below $H$ throughout the monitoring period.

#### In-out parity

A barrier option generally has a lower price than the corresponding vanilla option when the barrier condition restricts the set of states in which the option pays. For example, an up-and-out call satisfies

$$
\begin{align*}
  0 \leq& (S_T - K)^+ \mathbf{1}_{\set{\max_{t\in[0,T]} S_t < H}} \\
  \leq& (S_T - K)^+
\end{align*}
$$

so, under an abritrage-free pricing measure

$$
  V_t^\text{UO,call} \leq V_t^\text{vanilla call}
$$

The same intuition applies to knock-in options: a knock-in option pays only when an additional barrier event occurs, so its value is generally below that of the corresponding vanilla option. In fact, under matching contractual specifications and continuous monitoring, in–out parity gives

$$
  V_t^\text{KI} + V_t^\text{KO} = V_t^\text{vanilla}
$$ 

This follows because exactly one of the knock-in and knock-out conditions is satisfied for a given path, subject to the precise treatment of barrier touching.

The additional barrier condition therefore generally makes barrier options less expensive than their vanilla counterparts, while allowing investors to obtain more targeted exposures at a lower upfront premium. This combination of lower premium and path-dependent risk makes barrier options particularly common in OTC markets. However, the lower premium comes at the cost of additional path dependence: the option's value depends not only on the terminal price $S_T$, but also on the path followed by the underlier before expiration.

#### Barrier option pricing

Barrier options are classified as
- up-and-in call, $c_\text{ui}$
- up-and-out call, $c_\text{uo} = c - c_\text{ui}$ by in-out parity
- down-and-in call, $c_\text{di}$
- down-and-out call, $c_\text{do} = c - c_\text{di}$ by in-out-parity
- up-and-in put, $p_\text{ui}$
- up-and-out put, $p_\text{uo} = p - p_\text{ui}$ by in-out parity
- down-and-in put, $p_\text{di}$
- down-and-out put, $p_\text{do} = p - p_\text{di}$ by in-out-parity

Under the Black-Scholes-Merton model, the barrier option prices are given by

$$
\begin{align*}
  c_\text{ui} =& S_0 e^{-qT} N(x_1) - Ke^{-rT} N(x_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T}) \\
  &- S_0 e^{-qT} \left(\frac{H}{S_0}\right)^{2\lambda} [N(-y) - N(-y)] \\
  &+ Ke^{-rT} (H/S_0)^{2\lambda - 2} [N(-y + \sigma\sqrt{T}) - N(-y_1 + \sigma\sqrt{T})] \\
  c_\text{di} =& S_0 e^{-qT} \left(\frac{H}{S_0}\right)^{2\lambda} N(y) - Ke^{-rT} \left(\frac{H}{S_0}\right)^{2\lambda - 2} N(y - \sigma\sqrt{T}) \\
  p_\text{ui} =& -S_0 e^{-qT} \left(\frac{H}{S_0}\right)^{2\lambda} N(-y) + Ke^{-rT} \left(\frac{H}{S_0} \right)^{2\lambda - 2} N(-y + \sigma\sqrt{T}) \\
  p_\text{di} =& -S_0 e^{-qT} N(-x_1) + Ke^{-rT} N(-x_1 + \sigma\sqrt{T}) \\
  &+ S_0 e^{-qT} \left(\frac{H}{S_0}\right)^{2\lambda} [N(y) - N(y_1)] \\
  &- Ke^{-rT} \left(\frac{H}{S_0} \right)^{2\lambda - 2} [N(y - \sigma\sqrt{T}) - N(y_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T})]
\end{align*}
$$

where $c$ and $p$ are the closed-form vanilla call and put prices, respectively, and

$$
\begin{align*}
  \lambda =& \frac{r - q + \sigma^2 /2}{\sigma^2} \\
  y =& \frac{\ln(H^2/S_0 K)}{\sigma\sqrt{T}} + \lambda\sigma\sqrt{T} \\
  x_1 =& \frac{\ln(S_0/H)}{\sigma\sqrt{T}} + \lambda\sigma\sqrt{T} \\
  y_1 =& \frac{\ln(H/S_0)}{\sigma\sqrt{T}} + \lambda\sigma\sqrt{T}
\end{align*}
$$

### Binary options

A binary option is a contingent claim whose payoff is a fixed amount, either in cash or in an underlying asset, conditional on the occurrence of a specified event $A$. A cash-or-nothing binary has payoff $\Pi_T = N \mathbf{1}_A$, where $N$ is the predetermined cash amount. An asset-or-nothing binary has payoff $\Pi_T = S_T \mathbf{1}_A$ if one unit of the underlier is delivered up occurrence of $A$.

Binary options are commonly classified according to whether the event is determined by the terminal value of the underlier or by its path over a monitoring period.

#### European binary options

A European binary option has a payoff determined solely by the underlier's value at the expiration date $T$. For example, a cash-or-nothing binary call with threshold $H$ pays

$$
  \Pi_T^\text{BC} = N \mathbf{1}_{\set{S_T \geq H}}
$$

while a cash-or-nothing binary put with threshold $L$ pays

$$
  \Pi_T^\text{BP} = N\mathbf{1}_{\set{S_T \leq L}}
$$

Thus, the binary call pays $N$ if the terminal underlier price is at or above $H$, and zero otherwise. Similarly, the binary put pays $N$ if the terminal price is at or below $L$.

#### American binary options and touch options

An American binary option is path-dependent: its payoff depends on whether a specified event occurs at any time during a monitoring interval, rather than only on the terminal value $S_T$. Such options are also called touch options.

For example, a one-touch paying a fixed cash amount $N$ when the underlier reaches an upper barrier $H$ during $[0,T]$ has payoff

$$
  \Pi_T^\text{OT} = N \mathbf{1}_{\set{\max_{t\in [0,T]} S_t \geq H}}
$$

assuming $S_0 < H$.

A double-no-touch (DNT) pays a fixed amount provided that neither of two barriers is touched during the monitoring period. For lower barrier $L$ and upper barrier $H$, with $L < S_0 < H$, its payoff is

$$
  \Pi_T^\text{DNT} = N \mathbf{1}_{\set{\min_{t\in[0,T]} S_t \geq L \cap \max_{t\in[0,T]} S_t \leq H}}
$$

Touch and binary options are particularly important in the FX options market, where one-touch, no-touch, and double-no-touch structures are actively traded, especially for major currency pairs. They are often regarded as relatively standard products in that market, although they are path-dependent and therefore are not vanilla options in the strict sense.

#### Barrier options with rebates

A barrier option may include a rebate, which is a predetermined payment made when the barrier event occurs. For example, consider an up-and-out call with strike $K$, upper barrier $H$, and cash rebate $c$. If the option is knocked out when

$$
  \max_{t\in[0,T]} S_t \geq H
$$

then its terminal payoff, assuming the rebate is paid at maturity for simplicity, is

$$
\begin{align*}
  \Pi_T =& (S_T - K)^+ \mathbf{1}_{\set{\max_{t\in[0,T]} S_t < H}} + c \mathbf{1}_{\set{\max_{t\in[0,T]} S_t \geq H}} \\
  =& \Pi_T^\text{UO,call} + \Pi_T^\text{UI,bin} (c)
\end{align*}
$$

Thus, by no-arbitrage

$$
  V_t^\text{UO,call,rebate} = V_t^\text{UO,call} + V_t^\text{UI,bin} (c)
$$

with the understanding that if the rebate is paid immediately upon the barrier being hit, its valuation must account for the payment time rather than treating it as a payment at $T$.

### Asian option

An Asian option is a path-dependent option whose payoff depends on an average of the underlier price over a specified observation period, rather than solely on the underlier price at maturity. Depending on the contract specification, the averaging can be applied to the underlier used in the payoff, to the strike, or to both.

Let $t_1 < \cdots < t_m = T$ denote the observation dates, and define the arithmetic average of the underlier over these dates by

$$
  \bar{S}= \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^m S_{t_i}
$$

More generally, the average may be weighted

$$
  \bar{S} = \sum{i=1}^m w_i S_{t_i},\, w_i \geq 0
$$

with

$$
  \sum_{i=1}^m w_i = 1
$$

The weighting scheme and observation dates are contractual features of the Asian option.

#### Assian average-price option

When the average underlier price replaces the terminal spot price in the payoff while the strike remains fixed, the option is commonly called an Asian average-price option.

The payoff for Asian average-price options with strike $K$ are

$$
\begin{align*}
  \Pi_T =& (\bar{S} - K)^+ \\
  \Pi_T =& (K - \bar{S})^+
\end{align*}
$$

When the averaging takes place over a period immediately preceding maturity, the option is sometimes described as an Asian tail or Asian-out option.

#### Asian average-strike option

Alternatively, the average underlier price can determine the strike while the terminal underlier price remains in the payoff. Such an option is commonly called an Asian average-strike option.

The payoff for Asian average-strike options are

$$
\begin{align*}
  \Pi_T =& (\bar{S} - S_T)^+ \\
  \Pi_T =& (S_T - \bar{S})^+
\end{align*}
$$

#### Economic intuition

A principal feature of an Asian option is that averaging makes the payoff less sensitive to any single observation of the underlier. In particular, an average-price option depends on the entire set of observations rather than only on $S_T$, as in a European vanilla option. Consequently, Asian options generally have lower sensitivity to short-lived price fluctuations near a particular observation date than otherwise comparable European options.

This averaging feature can be useful for managing fixing risk and timing risk. In particular, a company that buys or sells an underlying asset progressively over a period may be exposed to the risk that a single spot price at a contractual fixing or expiration date is unfavorable. An Asian option can instead reference an average price over the relevant period, making the hedge more closely aligned with the company's effective average purchase or sale price.

### Lockback option

A lookback option is a path-dependent option whose payoff depends on the maximum or minimum value of the underlier observed over a specified monitoring period. The extremum can enter the payoff in one of two ways: it can determine the effective strike price, or it can determine the effective terminal underlier price.

Lookback options generally command a higher premium than comparable vanilla options because the path-dependent feature gives the holder the benefit of selecting the most favorable observed price within the specified range. The precise value depends strongly on the monitoring convention: continuous monitoring and discrete monitoring can produce materially different prices, since the observed maximum or minimum can differ between the two conventions.

Let $t_1 < \cdots < t_m$ denote the relevant observation dates. The exact set of observation dates, and whether the monitoring is continuous or discrete, are contractual features of the option.

#### Lookback strike option

In a lookback strike option, the maximum or minimum observed underlier price determines the effective strike.

For example, consider a lookback strike call whose effective strike is the minimum observed underlier price,

$$
  S_0^* = \min_{1 \leq i \leq m} S_{t_i}
$$

If the observation period ends no later tthan the option maturity, $t_m \leq T$, a normalized payoff can be written as

$$
  \Pi_T = N \left(\frac{S_T}{S_0^*} - 1 \right)^+
$$

The minimum observed price therefore acts as the strike reference. Economically, the holder benefits from being able to compare the terminal price with the most favorable low price observed during the lookback period.

#### Lookback spot option

In a lookback spot option, the terminal underlier price in the payoff is replaced by an extremum observed during the monitoring period.

For example, a lookback spot call based on the maximum observed price has

$$
  S_T^* = \max_{n-m+1 \leq i \leq n} S_{t_i},\, T_n = T
$$

and payoff

$$
  \Pi_T = \left(\frac{S_T^*}{S_0} - 1 \right)^+
$$

Here, $S_0$ is the initial price, while the maximum observed price $S_T^*$ replaces the terminal spot $S_T$. Thus, the holder effectively benefits from the highest underlier price achieved during the loockback period rather than being exposed solely to the price at maturity.

### Quanto option and composite option

When the currency in which an option payoff is settled differs from the currency in which the underlier is denominated, the option must specify how the foreign-currency performance of the underlier is translated into the payoff currency. Two important structures are quanto options and composite options.


| | Quanto | Composite |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Underlier               | Foreign-currency asset        | Foreign-currency asset                   |
| Payoff currency         | Different currency            | Different currency                       |
| FX rate in payoff       | Fixed/contractual             | Realized (X_T)                           |
| Investor's FX exposure  | Hedged/eliminated from payoff | Explicitly retained                      |
| Performance measured on | Foreign-currency underlier    | Underlier converted into payoff currency |

#### Quanto options

A quanto option is an option whose payoff is determined by the performance of the underlier measured in its domestic currency, while the payoff is settled in a different currency at a pre-specified or contractually fixed exchange rate. Thus, the payoff does not depend on the realized FX rate at maturity.

The term quanto is commonly understood as referring to a quantity-adjusted structure: the foreign underlier's return is preserved, while the currency conversion is effectively fixed.

For example, suppose a stock is denominated in EUR, with price $S_t^€$, and consider a USD-settled quanto call with notional $N$. If the option is specified in terms of the normalized stock performance, its payoff takes the form

$$
  \Pi_T^\text{quanto} = N \left(\frac{S_T^€}{S_0^€} - K \right)^+
$$

where $K$ is the strike expressed in terms of the stock's return or normalized price.

More generally, if the option is based on a monetary payoff in the underlier's domestic currency and converted at a fixed exchange rate $X^\text{fix}$, the corresponding payoff would be $\Pi_T^\text{quanto} = X^\text{fix} \Phi(S_T^€)$ for an appropriate domestic-currency payoff function $\Phi$.

The key feature is that the realized FX rate $X_T$ does not enter the payoff. Consequently, an investor whose investment currency is USD can obtain exposure to the EUR-denominated stock without taking direct exposure to movements in the EUR/USD exchange rate.

Quanto options are therefore useful when the investor wants exposure to the performance of a foreign underlier while eliminating the FX risk from the payoff. They are particularly natural for investors who have a view on the underlier but do not wish to take a view on the associated exchange rate.

It is worth noting, however, that eliminating FX exposure from the payoff does not mean that the FX market is irrelevant for pricing. In a no-arbitrage pricing framework, the valuation of a quanto derivative generally depends on the relationship between the underlier, the FX rate, and the relevant interest rates, including a quanto adjustment reflecting the correlation between the underlier and the exchange rate.

#### Composition options 

A composite option, or compo, also has a payoff in a currency different from the denomination currency of the underlier. Unlike a quanto option, however, the underlier's performance is measured after converting its price into the payoff currency using the realized FX rate.

Let $X_t$ denote the EUR/USD exchange rates, expressed as USD per EUR. The stock price expressed in USD is then $S_t^\$ = S_t^€ X_t$. A USD-settled composite call therefore has payoff

$$
  \Pi_T^\text{compo} = N \left(\frac{S_T^\$}{S_0^\$} - K \right)^+ = N \left(\frac{S_T^€ C_T}{S_0^€ X_0} - K \right)^+
$$

Thus, unlike the quanto payoff, the composite payoff depends on both the performance of the underlying stock in EUR and the EUR/USD exchange rate. Consequently, a quanto investor is primarily exposed to the foreign underlier, whereas a composite investor is exposed to the combined performance of the underlier and the exchange rate.

From another perspective, $S_t^€ X_t$ can be regarded as the price of a synthetic or "composite" asset denominated in USRD. The option is therefore written on the USD value of the foreign asset rather than on its EUR-denominated price alon.

Composite options are generally less intuitive for an investor who wants a pure view on the foreign underlier, because the payoff depends on two sources of uncertainty, $S_T^€$ and $X_T$. They can nevertheless be useful when the investor naturally has exposure to the foreign asset after currency conversion, or when hedging the domestic-currency interest-rate and funding risks associated with the foreign underlier is difficult or expensive. This consideration can be particularly relevant in markets where local-currency funding or interest-rate hedging instruments are relatively illiquid.

### Basket, worst-of, best-of and rainbow options

Let $S_t^i$ denote the price of the underlier $i$ at time $t$, for $i=1,\dots,n$. It is often convenient to work with the normalized performance

$$
  R_t^i = \frac{S_T^i}{S_0^i}
$$

where $S_0^i$ is the initial reference price of underlier $i$.

#### Basket options

A basket option is an option whose payoff depends on a weighted combination of the performances of several underliers. For a basket with weights $w_i$, satisfying

$$
  \sum_{i=1}^n w_i = 1
$$

a normalized basket call can be written as

$$
  \Pi_T^\text{basket,call} = \left(\sum_{i=1}^n w_i \frac{S_T^i}{S_0^i} - K \right)^+
$$

the equally weighted case corresponds to $w_i = 1/n$.

Because the basket aggregates the performance of several underliers, idiosyncratic fluctuations in individual underliers tend to partially offset one another. Consequently, a basket payoff is generally smoother than the corresponding payoff on an individual underlier.

From a risk perspective, a basket option has exposure to the forward levels, volatilities, and correlations of its constituent underliers. For a long basket call, an increase in the relevant forward levels generally increases the option's value. The option also has positive exposure to the volatility of the basket. The effect of correlation, however, requires some care: for a standard call on a positively weighted basket, increasing the pairwise correlations generally increases the variance of the basket and therefore increases the option value, all else being equal. Thus, a long basket call is typically long correlation.

#### Worst-of options

A worst-of option bases its payoff on the poorest-performing underlier in a basket. For normalized performances $R_T^i$, worst-of call has payoff

$$
  \Pi_T^\text{worst-of,call} = \left(\min_{1\leq i\leq n} R_T^i - K \right)^+
$$

Unlike a basket option, which averages the performances of the underliers, a worst-of option is determined entirely by the weakest constituent at maturity.

For a long worst-of call, the position is typically described as long forward and short correlation. The latter reflects the fact that increasing correlation makes it more likely that the underliers move together. This reduces the diversification benefit that would otherwise make it less likely for one constituent to substantially underperform the others, thereby reducing the value of the minimum.

Consequently, all else equal, a worst-of option tends to become less valuable as the correlation between its constituents increases.

#### Best-of options

A best-of option bases its payoff on the best-performing underlier in the basket. A best-of call has payoff

$$
  \Pi_T^\text{best-of,call} = \left(\max_{i\leq 1\leq n} R_T^i - K \right)^+
$$

The best-of option has the opposite correlation exposure to a worst-of option. For a long best-of call, increasing correlation generally reduces the value of the option because greater correlation makes the constituents more likely to perform similarly and therefore reduces the benefit of having the right to select the best performer. Hence, a long best-of option is typically described as long dispersion or short correlation.

Best-of options can command relatively high premiums because the holder benefits from an implicit selection mechanism: among several underliers, the option can effectively select whichever performs best. This makes them less attractive for cost-sensitive structured products.

#### Rainbow options

A rainbow option generalizes the worst-of and best-of structures by assigning different weights to the ranked performances of the underliers.

For example, suppose the performances are ranked as $R_T^{(1)} \leq \cdots \leq R_T^{(n)}$, where $R_T^{(1)}$ is the worst-performing underlier and $R_T^{(n)}$ is the best-performing underlier. The payoff of a rainbow call takes the form

$$
  \Pi_T^\text{rainbow,call} = \left(\sum_{i=1}^n a_i R_T^{(i)} - K \right)^+
$$

where the weights $a_j$ are specified contractually. Worst-of and best-of options can be viewed as limiting cases of rainbow structures.

# Black-Scholes-Merton (BSM) model

Under the Black-Scholes-Merton model, the price of a non-dividend paying underlier follows a geometric Brownian motion

$$
\begin{equation*}
  \d S_t = \mu S_t \;\d t + \sigma S_t \;\d W_t
\tag{\label{equation-11}}
\end{equation*}
$$

where $\mu$ denotes the drift and $\sigma > 0$ denotes the volatility of the underlier.

<MathBox title="" boxType="proposition">
Let $S_t$ be the process defined by the stochastic differential equation $\eqref{equation-11}$. Then

$$
\begin{equation*}
  S_t = S_0 \exp\left(\left[\mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right] t + \sigma W_t \right)
\tag{\label{equation-10}}
\end{equation*}
$$

and

$$
  S_T = S_t \exp\left(\left[\mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right](T - t) + \sigma (W_T - W_t) \right)
$$

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>

Define $f(t, x) = \ln(x)$. Then

$$
\begin{align*}
  \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} =& \frac{1}{x} \\
  \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2} =& -\frac{1}{x^2} \\
  \frac{\partial f}{\partial t} =& 0
\end{align*}
$$

By the Itô-Doeblin formula, we obtain

$$
\begin{align*}
  \d\ln(S_t) =& \d f(t, S_t) \\
  =& 0 \cdot \d t + \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} (t, S_t) \;\d S_t + \frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2} (t, S_t) \;\d S_t^2 \\
  =& \frac{1}{S_t} + \frac{1}{2} \left(-\frac{1}{S_t^2} \right) \d S_t^2 \\
  =& \left(\mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2} \right) \;\d t + \sigma \;\d W_t
\end{align*}
$$

where we have used that $\d S_t^2 = \sigma^2 S_t^2$. Integrating from $0$ to $t$, we obtain $\eqref{equation-10}$. Then the second equation follows

$$
\begin{align*}
  S_T =& S_0 \exp\left(\left[\mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right] T + \sigma W_ T \right) \\
  =& S_t \exp\left(\left[\mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right] (T - t) + \sigma (W_T - W_t) \right)
\end{align*}
$$
</details>
</MathBox>

<MathBox title="" boxType="corollary">
The logarithm of $S_t$ is an arithmetic Brownian motion

$$
\begin{align*}
  \ln(S_t) =& \ln(S_0) + \left(\mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2} t + \sigma W_t \right) \\
  \sim& \mathcal{N}\left(\ln(S_0) + \left[\mu - \sigma^2 \right]t, \sigma^2 t \right)
\end{align*}
$$

Thus, $S_t$ has a log-normal distribution with

$$
  \mathbb{E}(S_t) = S_0 e^{\mu t}
$$

and

$$
  \operatorname{var}(S_t) = S_0^2 e^{2\mu t} (e^{\sigma^2 t} - 1)
$$

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>

The distribution of $\ln (S_t)$ follows from taking the logarithm of $\eqref{equation-10}$. Since $W_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0,t)$, then $W_t = \sqrt{t}\epsilon$, with $\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)$s. Applying the moment-generating function for normal variables, we have

$$
\begin{align*}
  \mathbb{E}(S_t) =& \mathbb{E}\left[S_0 \exp\left(\left[\mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2} \right] t + \sigma W_t \right) \right] \\
  =& S_0 \exp\left(\left[\mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2} \right] t \right) \mathbb{E} (e^{\sqrt{t}\epsilon}) \\
  =& S_0 \exp\left(\left[\mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2} \right] t \right) \exp\left(\frac{\sigma^2}{2} t \right) = S_0 e^{\mu t}
\end{align*}
$$

Similarly, we have

$$
\begin{align*}
  \mathbb{E}(S_t^2) =& \mathbb{E}\left[S_0^2 \exp\left(\left[2\mu - \sigma^2 \right] t + 2\sigma W_t \right) \right] \\
  =& S_0^2 \exp\left(\left[2\mu - \sigma^2 \right] t \right) \mathbb{E} (e^{2\sigma\sqrt{t}\epsilon}) \\
  =& S_0^2 \exp\left(\left[2\mu + \sigma^2 \right] t \right)
\end{align*}
$$

Thus

$$
\begin{align*}
  \operatorname{var}(S_t) =& \mathbb{E}(S_t^2) - \mathbb{E}(S_t)^2 \\
  =& S_0^2 e^{2\mu t} (e^{\sigma^2 t} - 1)
\end{align*}
$$
</details>

## Derivation of the Black-Scholes equation

The BSM model assumes a "perfect market" in which
- there is no arbitrary opportunity
- underlier can be bought or sold at any time for any quantity (including a fraction of the underlier)
- there is no short selling restriction and any security can be borrowed in the market
- there is no transaction cost
- there is a risk-free interest rate $r$ for borroing and lending

Without loss of generality, we present the option modelling for a European option $V$ linked to a single underlier whose total return price process is $S_2t$. However, the results can be generalized to $V$ being a portfolio of European options linked to a common underlier.

Denote $V_t$ as the option price process. By Itô-Doeblin formula, we obtain $V_t$ as an Itô process

$$
\begin{equation*}
\begin{split}
  \d V_t =& \left(\mu S_t \frac{\partial V_t}{\partial S_t} + \frac{1}{2} \sigma_t^2 S_t^2 \frac{\partial V}{\partial S^2}} + \frac{\partial V_t}{\partial t} \right) \\
  &+ \sigma_t S_t \frac{\partial V_t}{\partial S_t} \;\d W_t
\end{split}
\tag{\label{equation-12}}
\end{equation*}
$$

We construct a portfolio $\Pi_t$ consisting of a short position of the option and a position of $\Delta_t$ units of the underlier at time $t$

$$
\begin{equation*}
  \Pi_t = - V_t + \Delta_t S_t
\tag{\label{equation-17}}
\end{equation*}
$$

The key idea is to find the value of $\Delta_t$ so that the portfolio becomes instantaneously deterministic, i.e. by eliminating the $\d W_t$ term representing the randomness of the underlier price. The differential of $\Pi_t$ is

$$
\begin{equation*}
  \d\Pi_t = -\d V_t + \Delta_t \d S_t
\tag{\label{equation-13}}
\end{equation*}
$$

Applying $\eqref{equation-11}$ and $\eqref{equation-12}$ to $\eqref{equation-13}$, we get

$$
\begin{equation*}
\begin{split}
  \d\Pi_t =& -\left(\mu S_t \frac{\partial V}{\partial S_t} + \frac{1}{2} \sigma_t^2 S_t^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} + \frac{\partial V}{\partial t} - \mu\Delta_t S_t \right) \;\d t \\
  &+ \sigma_t S_t \left(-\frac{\partial V}{\partial S_t} + \Delta_t \right) \;\d W_t
\end{split}
\tag{\label{equation-14}}
\end{equation*}
$$

By setting

$$
  \Delta_t = \frac{\partial V_t}{\partial S_t},\, \forall t
$$

the risk from the randomness of underlier price change is eliminated and $\eqref{equation-14}$ becomes

$$
\begin{equation*}
  \d\Pi_t = -\left(\frac{1}{2} \sigma_t^2 S_t^2 \frac{\partial^2 V_t}{\partial S_t^2} + \frac{\partial V}{\partial t} \right) \;\d t
\tag{\label{equation-15}}
\end{equation*}
$$

Under the no-arbitrage assumption, the following is satisfied if there is no risk-taking

$$
  \d\Pi_t = r \Pi_t \;\d t
$$

or, equivalently

$$
\begin{equation*}
  -\d V_t + r V_t \;\d t + \Delta_t \d S_t - \Delta_t r \d S_t \;\d t = 0
\tag{\label{equation-16}}
\end{equation*}
$$

If $\d\Pi_t > r\Pi_t \;\d t$, an arbitrager could make a riskless profit by borrowing an amount $\Pi_t$ at rate $r$ to invest in the portfolio. Conversely, if $\d\Pi_t < r\Pi_t \;\d t$, then the arbitrager would short the portfolio and invest $\Pi_t$ at the risk-free rate $r$.

Combining $\eqref{equation-15}$, $\eqref{equation-16}$ and $\eqref{equation-17}$, we get

$$
  \left(\frac{1}{2}\sigma_t^2 S_t^2 \frac{\partial^2 V_t}{\partial S_t^2} + \frac{\partial V_t}{\partial t} \right) \;\d t = r (V_t - \Delta_t S_t) \;\d t
$$

which leads to the Black-Scholes equation

$$
\begin{equation*}
  \frac{\partial V_t}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{2} \sigma_t^2 S_t^2 \frac{\partial^2 V_t}{\partial S_t^2} = rV_t - rS_t \frac{\partial V_t}{\partial S_t}
\tag{\label{equation-19}}
\end{equation*}
$$

This equation does not contain the drift $\mu$ of the underlier. The price will depend on the risk-free interest rate $r$ and the volatility $\sigma_t$.

Delta-hedging process can be conceptually considered as a succession of the below operations for each infinitesimal period $\d t$ until the termination of the option
- at the beginning og $\d t$: short the call option at price $V_{t^-}$ and invest the proceeds at rate $r$; long $\Delta_t$ units of the underlier funded at rate $r$
- at the end of $\d t$: close the whole position to cash. The P/L $Z_t$ is $-\d V_t$ (by option price change) $+ r V_{t^-} \;\d t$ (carry) $+ \Delta_t \;\d S_t$ (by underlier price change) $-\Delta_t r S_{t^-} \;\d t$ (funding cost for underlier units). By $\eqref{equation-16}$, it follows that $Z_t = 0$.

In practical application of the BSM model, the parameter $\sigma_t$ is the best estimation by market consensus at time $t$ of the constant volatility to be realized from the valuation date until the maturity of the derivative (implied volatility) as its value is reverse-calculated from the market price of vanilla options.

Since the market's view on $\sigma_t$ changes over time, it should be set dynamically at the implied volatility lever so that the Black-Scholes equation holds while avoiding arbitrage.

To illustrate the PDE method for option pricing, consider a European call option $c(t, S)$ with exercise price $K$ and expiry date $T$. The terminal condition is given by the payoff

$$
\begin{equation*}
  c(T, S_T) = \max\set{S_T - K, 0}
\tag{\label{equation-24}}
\end{equation*}
$$

The boundary conditions are applied at $S_t = 0$ and $S_t \to \infty$. From $\eqref{equation-11}$, if $S_t$ touches zero, it will stay at zero forever, leading to $0$ payoff for the call option

$$
\begin{equation*}
  c(t, 0) = 0,\, \forall t \geq 0
\tag{\label{equation-20}}
\end{equation*}
$$

If $S_t \to\infty$, it becomes certain that the option will be exercise and the value of the will be the underlier price minus the discounted exercise part

$$
\begin{equation*}
  \lim_{S_t \to\infty} c(t, S_t) = S_t - Ke^{-r(T - t)},\, \forall t > 0
\tag{\label{equation-23}}
\end{equation*}
$$

Similarly, for a vanilla European put option price $p(t, S_t)$, the terminal condition is

$$
  p(T, S_T) = \max\set{K - S_T, 0}
$$

while the boundary conditions are

$$
  p(t, 0) = K e^{-r t},\, \forall t > 0
$$

and

$$
  \lim_{S_t \to\infty} p(t, S_t) = 0
$$

## Black-Scholes formulas for vanilla options

<MathBox title="Black-Scholes formula for European call options" boxType="proposition" tag="1">
The time $t$ value of a vanilla European call with strike $K$ and maturity $T$ is given by

$$
\begin{equation*}
  c(t, S_t) = S_t N(d_1) - K e^{-r(T - t)} N(d_2)
\tag{\label{equation-18}}
\end{equation*}
$$

where $N(x)$ is the cumulative distribution function for standarad normal random variables

$$
\begin{equation*}
  d_1 = \frac{\ln(S_t /K) + (r + \sigma^2/2) (T - t)}{\sigma\sqrt{T - t}}
\tag{\label{equation-21}}
\end{equation*}
$$

and 

$$
\begin{equation*}
  d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T - t}
\tag{\label{equation-22}}
\end{equation*}
$$

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>

Firstly, $\eqref{equation-18}$ satisfies the Black-Scholes equation $\eqref{equation-19}$. We note below for calculating the partial derivatives

$$
\begin{align*}
  \frac{\partial N(d_i)}{\partial x} =& \frac{\partial N (d_i)}{\partial d_i} \frac{\partial d_1}{\partial x} \\
  =& \frac{e^{-d_i^2 /2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \frac{\partial d_i}{\partial x}
\end{align*}
$$

For the boundary condition $\eqref{equation-20}$, we first note by $\eqref{equation-21}$ and $\eqref{equation-22}$, then $d_1, d_2 \xrightarrow{S_t \to 0} 0$ and $N(-\infty) = 0$. Thus

$$
\begin{align*}
 c(t, 0) =& 0 \cdot N(-\infty) - Ke^{-r(T - t)} N(-\infty) \\
 =& 0
\end{align*}
$$

For the boundary condition $\eqref{equation-23}$, we note again that $d_1, d_2 \xrightarrow{S_t \to\infty} \infty$ and $N(\infty) = 1$. Thus

$$
\begin{align*}
  \lim_{S_t \to\infty} c(t, S_t) = S_t N(\infty) - Ke^{-r(T - t)} N(\infty) \\
  \sim& S_t - Ke^{-r(T - t)}
\end{align*}
$$

Consider the terminal condition $\eqref{equation-24}$. At $t = T$, if $S_T > K$, then $d_1, d_2 \to\infty$, so that $c(T, S_T) = S_T - K$. If $S_T < K$, then $d_1, d_2 \to -\infty$, so that $c(T, S_T) = 0$. If $S_T = K$, by continuity, $c(T, S_T) = 0$.

</MathBox>

### Approximation formula

For at-the-money forward (ATMF) call/put options, the option price is approximated by

$$
  c = p \approx 0.4 S_0 \sigma\sqrt{T}
$$

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>

The strike of an ATMF option is $K = e^{tT} S_0$, where $S_0$ is the spot level at $t = 0$. In this case, the European call and put options have the same price according to put-call parity.

Note that $S_0 = e^{-rT} K$, $d_1 = \sigma\sqrt{T}/2$ and $d_2 = -\sigma\sqrt{T}/2$. From Taylor expansion, we have

$$
  N(x) = N(0) + N'(0)x + \frac{1}{2} N''(0) x^2 + O(x^3)
$$

Using $N'(0) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \approx 0.4$, we obtain

$$
\begin{align*}
  c =& S_0 N(d_1) - e^{-rT} KN(d_2) \\
  =& S_0 \left[N\left(\frac{\sigma\sqrt{T}}{2} \right) - N\left(-\frac{\sigma\sqrt{T}}{2} \right) \right] \\
  \approx& S_0 N'(0) \sigma\sqrt{T} \approx 0.4 S_0 \sigma\sqrt{T}
\end{align*}
$$
</details>

## Price sensitivities (greeks)

In the Black-Scholes-Merton mode, the price of portfolio $\Pi_t$ depends on the underlier price, time-to-maturity of the options, volatility, and risk-free interest rate. That is, $\Pi_t = \Pi(T - t, S_t, \sigma_t, r)$. The sensitivities with respect to these parameters are denoted by Greek letters
- Delta: $\Delta_t = \partial\Pi_t /\partial S_t$
- Theta: $\Theta_t = \partial \Pi_t /\partial t$
- Rho: $\rho_t = \partial\Pi_t /\partial r$
- Gamma: $\Gamma_t = \partial^2 \Pi_t / \partial S_t^2$
- Vega: $\mathcal{V}_t = \partial\Pi_t / \partial\sigma_t$

From the Itô-Doeblin formula, the price change of $\Pi_t$ can be expressed by its greeks

$$
  \Pi_t = \Theta_t \d t + \Delta_t \d S_t + \frac{1}{2} S_t^2 \sigma_t^2 \Gamma_t \;\d t
$$

The Black-Scholes equation

$$
  \frac{\partial\Pi_t}{\partial t} + r S_t \frac{\partial\Pi_t}{\partial S_t} + \frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial^2 \Pi_t}{\partial S_t^2} \sigma_t^2 S_t^2 = r\Pi_t
$$

can be expressed in terms of greeks as

$$
  \Theta_t + rS_t \Delta_t + \frac{1}{2} \Gamma_t \sigma_t^2 S_t = r\Pi_t
$$

In the case that $\Pi_t$ is delta-neutral with $\Delta_t = 0$, the Black-Scholes equation becomes

$$
\begin{equation*}
  \frac{1}{2} \Gamma_t \sigma_t^2 S_t^2 = -\Theta_t + r\Pi_t
\tag{\label{equation-26}}
\end{equation*}
$$

which is known as the Gamma-Theta relationship under the continuous delta-hedging assumption.

### Delta $\Delta_t$

The delta of a portfolio measures the value change with respect to the price change of the underlier. Delta-hedging consists of holding $\Delta_t$ units of the underlier at any time $t$. As $\Delta_t$ is not a constant for option derivatives, the delta position needs to be adjusted frequently (continuously in theory, but usually once at the end of the day in practice). Any adjustment should be carried out with the self-financing principal, i.e. any underlier purchase is financed by a loan and any cash is lent out. The long position of the underlier is lent to the market. The negative delta position is implemented by borrowing and selling units of the underlier.

As the underlier price changes, a P/L will be produced by the delta. According to the BSM model, the cumulative P/L from the continuous delta-hedging for a single option combined with the option premium will be exactly the option price for any time $t$. In particular, when $t$ is expiry time, the option price is the payoff amount.

Consider the example of hedging for a call sold by an option writer who borrowed $\Delta_0 S_0$ for the delta at inception. The initial portfolio is composed of the call option (short), option premium (long), $\Delta_0$ units of the underlier and a loan of $\Delta_0 S_0$. As the option premium offsets the value of the short option, we have $\Pi_0 = 0$. During the life of the trade, $\Delta_t$ units of the underlier should be maintained at any time $t$. The cash in the portfolio is lent out, and the underlier position is also lent to the market at repo rate (i.e. security lending rate).

### Gamma $\Gamma_t$

Gamma measures the change of delta with respect to the price change of the underlier. If gamma is zero, the delta amount is constant throughout the entire considered tenor, i.e. static hedging. A nonzero gamma portfolio leads to dynamic delta hedging. Gamma represents the convexity part that is not captured by delta-hedging when the derivative price change is not linear to underlier's price change. For a continuously delta-hedged portfolio, the gamma impact is offset by theta and the portfolio's carry, as indicated by $\eqref{equation-26}$. However, in practice only discrete hedging is possible and the natural offsetting of gamma impact by theta does not hold anymore.

### Theta $\Theta_t$

Theta measures the value change of a portfolio with time passing. It is also called time decay, which reflects the loss in value due to having less time for exercising the option. Taking the example of a long call option on a stock with zero dividend and repo, the option price curve will be approaching the intrinsic value line progressively from the above over time. For a delta-neutral portfolio, theta is compensated by the gamma term through the Gamma-Theta relationship $\eqref{equation-26}$.

### Vega $\mathcal{V}_t$

In the BSM model, the volatility is a constant, which is a simplistic assumption. In practice, it is a perimeter that is difficult to estimate, especially for the future period until the option's expiry.

Vega measures the impact of volatility to option price. A vega position can be hedged with other options having the opposite amount of vega. For the motivation of vega hedging, we can image a pure theoretical extreme case that the underlier price has never moved after the option's inception, which implies that the time value is not compensated by gamma. This risk can be mitigated by vega hedging. In practice, the frequency of vega hedging is far less than delta hedging.

Under the BSM model there is a Vega-Gamma relationship for European options: $\mathcal{V}_t = \sigma(T - t)S_t^2 \Gamma_t$, which implies that if the gamma of a portfolio of European options is zero, its vega should also be zero, and vice versa.

### Rho $\rho_t$

The parameter rho measures the sensitivity of the portfolio's value with respect to the change of interest rate level. In practive, the risk-free rate is replaced by the borrow/lending rate available for hedging transactions, such as the internal funding rate from the same institution.

## Practical issues in hedging

### Hedging instruments and hedging ratios

Let $\Pi_t$ be a portfolio comprising one or more derivatives, which is sensitive to a variety of factors $x_t^{(1)}, \dots, x_t^{(n)}$ that can be securities' prices and their volatilities, interest rates, foreign exchange rates, etc.

THe assumption of the BSM model are only partially satisfied in financial markets and delta-hedging alone is not perfect. As hedging is performed in discrete time, Taylor expansion should be used for the analysis, while the Itô-Doeblin formula is applicable for continuous time analysis. If $\Pi_t$ is at least twice differentiable with respect to the related factors, the portfolio's value change over time interval $\Delta t$ can be represented by the Taylor expansion

$$
\begin{equation*}
  \Delta\Pi_t = \frac{\partial\Pi_t}{\partial t} \Delta t + \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{\partial\Pi_t}{\partial x_t^{(i)}} + \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i,j=1}^n \frac{\partial^2 \Pi_t}{\partial x_t^{(i)} \partial x_t^{(j)}} \Delta x_i^{(i)} \Delta x_t^{(j)} + \epsilon_t
\tag{\label{equation-27}}
\end{equation*}
$$

where $\Delta\Pi_t = \Pi_{t + \Delta t} - \Pi_t$ and $\Delta x_t^{(i)} = x_{t + \Delta t}^{(i)} = x_{t + \Delta t}^{(i)} - x_t^{(i)}$ and $\epsilon_t$ represents the total value of higher order terms.

Dynamic hedging consists of offsetting the impact of the value change of the factors to $\Pi_t$ by dynamically including a self-financing position of liquid instruments that are also sensitive to these factors. In practice, not all the terms in $\eqref{equation-27}$ can be perfectly hedged with simple and liquid instruments.

Consider hedging against a single factor $x_t$ with a liquid instrument $f(x_t, \dots)$ which provides efficient sensitivity to $x_t$. The hedging quantity of $f(x_t,\dots)$ denoted by $Q_t$ satisfies the followin general relation for hedging the first or second order sensitivities

$$
  \frac{\partial^k}{\partial x_t^{(k)}} \left(\Pi_t + Q_t (f_t - c) \right) = 0
$$

or, equivalently

$$
  Q_t = -\frac{\partial^k \Pi_t}{\partial x_t^k} / \frac{\partial^k f_t}{\partial x_t^k}
$$

where $k = 1,2$ and $c$ represents the cost for including $f_t$ in the portfolio at time $t$, i.e. $c = f_t$, which implies that the portfolio's value remain unchanged at the time of hedging.
- for $k = 1$ and $x_t$ being the underlier's price $S_t$, we can use $f(S_t) = S_t$ to obtain the delta-hedging amount $Q_t = -\partial\Pi_t /\partial S_t$
- for $k = 1$ and $x_t$ being the underlier's implied volatility, $\sigma_t$, then $f(S_t, \sigma_t)$ can be a delta-hedged option, which leads to the vega-hedging with $Q_t = -\frac{\partial\Pi_t}{\partial\sigma_t} / \frac{\partial f_t}{\partial\sigma_t}$
- for $k = 2$ and $x_t$ being the underlier's price $S_t$, then $f(S_t, \sigma_t)$ can be a delta-hedged options, which leads to gamma-hedging with $Q_t = -\frac{\partial^2 \Pi_t}{\partial S_t^2} / \frac{\partial^2 f_t}{\partial S_t^2}$

As $\frac{\partial\Pi_t}{\partial x_t} \Delta x_t = \frac{\partial\Pi_t}{\partial x_t} \frac{\Delta x_t}{\Delta y_t} \Delta y_t$, then $y_t$ can be used instead of $x_t$ for hedging when it is more convenient, e.g. index futures for delta-hedging and ETF option.

### Discrete heding

If delta-hedging is continuous, the impact of gamma and theta are offset according to the Gamma-Theta relationship $\eqref{equation-26}$. In practice, delta-hedging can only be carried out at discrete times and the P/L impact from gamma and theta may not be offset according to $\eqref{equation-26}$ between two delta-hedging times, despite that the implied volatility is adjusted to make the price and theta "fair" for the projected market condition: if the underlier price has almost not changed, theta impact should be more signifiact than gamma impact that is near zero, while if the underlier price makes large moves, the gamma impact will be more significant than theta impact. A negative gamma position will make the portfolio under-hedged while a positive gamma position will make the portfolio over-hedged.

Consider a delta-hedged long call position with ATMF strike. Thus, we have $\Gamma > 0$. At inception, the hedging is done by shorting $\Delta_0$ shares at spot price $S_0$, where $\Delta_0 \approx 0.5$. Then the next day

| Scenario | Hedging | Result |
|---|---|---|
| $S_t$ drops and $\Delta_t \to 0.45$ | Need to short less, buy back at cheaper price | Buy low |
| $S_t$ rises and $\Delta_t \to 0.55$ | Need to short more, sell at higher price | Sell high |

In discrete re-hedging, the $\Gamma > 0$ position enjoys a "buy low/sell high" benefit while the $\Gamma < 0$ position leads to a "buy high/sell low" situation. However, the re-hedging P/L is counterbalanced by the theta impact ($\Theta < 0$ for positive gamma position and $\Theta > 0$ for negative gamma position).

The decision on the amount of theta and gamma for the portfolio is one of the major focuses for option traders, which depends on their day-to-day forecast on the scale of the price move to be compared with the theta from the position carry. In a market with liquid options, the gamma position of a portfolio (i.e., long, short or neutral) can be constructed by trading delta-hedged options having the opposite gamma amount.

### Delta-hedging P/L

The realized volatility during the life of an option trade may differ from the implied volatility used for pricing and hedging. Let us analyze the P/L impact of the realized volatility assuming a constant implied volatility for pricing and hedging.

Let $S_t$ be the underlier price that follows the process

$$
  \d S_t = \mu S_t \;\d t + \sigma_t S_t W_t
$$

where $\sigma_t$ represents the realized volatility for the infinitesimal period $\d t$.

Denote $\hat{\sigma}_t$ as the implied volatility used for hedging/pricing that satisfies the Black-Scholes equation $\eqref{equation-19}$. Let $r$ be the interest rate. The P/L for the short party of a delta-hedged contingent claim paying out $V_T$ at maturity $T$ is

$$
\begin{equation*}
  \frac{1}{2} \int_0^T e^{r(T-t)} \Gamma_t S_t^2 (\sigma_t^2 - \hat{\sigma}_t^2) \;\d t
\tag{\label{equation-29}}
\end{equation*}
$$

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>

Note that $V_t$ satisfies the Black-Scholes equation related to the hedging volatility $\hat{\sigma}_t$

$$
\begin{equation*}
  \frac{\partial V_t}{\partial t} = -\left(rS_t \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} + \frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} \hat{\sigma}_t^2 S_t^2 - rV_t \right)
\tag{\label{equation-28}}
\end{equation*}
$$

Applying the Itô-Doeblin formula to $V_t$ as a function of $t$, $S_t$ and $\hat{\sigma_t}$

$$
  \d V_t = \frac{\partial V}{\partial t} \;\d t + \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} \;\d S_t + \frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2}\sigma_t^2 S_t^2 \;\d t
$$

Substituting $\eqref{equation-28}$

$$
  \d V_t =& rV_t \;\d t - rS_t \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} \;\d t + \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} \;\d S_t + \frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} S_t^2 (\sigma_t^2 - \hat{\sigma}_t^2) \;\d t
$$

Substituting the greeks $\Delta_t = \partial V/\partial S$ and $\Gamma_t = \partial^2 V/\partial S^2$ and rearranging

$$

$$

Multiplying by the integrating factor $e^{-rt}$ and applying the product rule

$$
  \d(e^{-rt} V_t) = \Delta_t \d(e^{-rt} S_t) + \frac{1}{2} e^{-rt} \Gamma_t S_t^2 (\gamma_t^2 - \hat{\sigma}_t^2) \;\d t
$$

For a portfolio composed of the short position of a derivative and its delta-hedging, the portfolio’s delta is zero. Thus, the discounted hedging P/L for the $\d t$ period is

$$
  \frac{1}{2} e^{-rt} \Gamma_t S_t^2 (\gamma_t^2 - \hat{\sigma}_t^2) \;\d t
$$

and the cumulative hedging P/L of the portfolio at $T$ is $\eqref{equation-29}$.

### Volatility modelling

To mitigate the delta-hedging P/L related to the realized gamma and variance, traders may choose to trade delta-hedged vanilla options to offset the gamma (i.e., gamma-hedging). As the derivative price $V_t$ represents the hedging portfolio's value, it will be a function of $S_t$ and option price that depends on the implied volatility for vanilla options $\hat{\sigma}_t$, i.e. $V_t (t, S_t, \hat{\sigma}_t)$. Generally, $\hat{\sigma}_t$ is random and correlated to $S_t$. Applying the Itô-Doeblin formula to $V_t (t, S_t, \hat{\sigma}_t)$, results in second order terms $\d^2 V_t / \d\hat{\sigma}_t^2$ (known as volga) and $\d^2 V_t /(\d S_t \;\d\hat{\sigma}_t)$ (known as vanna or spot-vega) that are not hedged out by delta/gamma hedging. Modelling $\hat{\sigma}_t$ using stochastic volatility models offer a consistent pricing and risk management framework with all the $\hat{\sigma}_t$ related sensitivities quantified and a theta offsetting all the second orders terms in particular.

In some products, such as snowball autocallable structures with KI barrier, volga and vanna may become quite significant during the life of the product, which may result in large vega rehedging risk under some market condition (i.e. a sharp increase in volatility may trigger the KI-barrier and makes trading to buy back volatility at a higher level). Currently, the liquid volatility instruments are vanilla options, which do not provide efficient hedging for these sensitivities.

### Transaction cost

Let $k$ be the round trip transaction cost per unit value of the underlier. A self-financing hedging portfolio with no transaction cost satisfies

$$
  \delta V \approx \Delta\delta S + (V - \Delta S) \delta t
$$

where $\delta t$ is a small discrete change in time. The cost for adjusting the $\Delta$ position during the time interval $\delta t$ is

$$
  \delta K = \frac{k}{2} S|\delta\Delta| \approx \frac{k}{2} S|\Gamma\sigma S\delta W|
$$

where

$$
  \Gamma = \frac{\delta\Delta}{\delta S}
$$

and $\delta S \approx \sigma S\delta W$. In this analysis, $\mathbb{E}(|\delta W|)$ is applied instead of $|\delta W|$. By direct integration and the fact that $\delta W \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\delta t)$, we can obtain

$$
\begin{align*}
  \mathbb{E}(|\delta W|) =& \int_{-\infty}^\infty \frac{|x|}{\sqrt{2\pi\delta t}} \exp\left(-\frac{x^2}{2\delta t} \right) \;\d x \\
  =& 2 \int_0^\infty \frac{x}{\sqrt{2\pi\delta t}} \exp\left(-\frac{x^2}{2\delta t} \right) \;\d x \\
  =& \sqrt{\frac{2\delta t}{\pi}} \int_0^\infty \exp\left(-\frac{x^2}{2\delta t} \right) \;\d\left(\frac{x^2}{2\delta t} \right) \\
  =& \sqrt{\frac{2\delta t}{\pi}} \int_0^\infty e^{-y} \;\d y = \sqrt{\frac{2\delta t}{\pi}}
\end{align*}
$$

Defining the Leland number

$$
  \operatorname{Le} = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}} \left(\frac{k}{\sigma\sqrt{\delta t}} \right)
$$

we get

$$
  \delta K = \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 S^2 |\Gamma| \operatorname{Le} \delta t
$$

Adding in the cost for rebalancing the delta $\delta K$, we obtain

$$
  \delta V \approx \Delta\delta S + r(V - \Delta S) \delta t + \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 S^2 |G| \operatorname{Le} \delta t
$$

From the Itô-Doeblin formula, we have

$$
  \d V = \frac{\partial V}{\partial t} \;\d t + \frac{\partial V}{\partial S}\;\d S + \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} \;\d t
$$

Thus

$$
  \frac{\partial V}{\partial t} \;\d t + \frac{\partial V}{\partial S}\;\d S + \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} \;\d t = \Delta\;\d S + r(V - \Delta S)\;\d t - \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 S^2 |\Gamma| \operatorname{Le}\;\d t
$$

Arranging terms, we obtain the modified BSM model with transaction cost

$$
  \frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \left| \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} \right| \operatorname{Le} + rS \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} - rV = 0
$$

If $1 + \operatorname{sgn}(\Gamma) \operatorname{Le} \geq 0$, we have

$$
  \frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{2} \tilde{\sigma}^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} + rS\frac{\partial V}{\partial S} - rV = 0
$$

with adjusted volatility

$$
  \tilde{\sîgma}^2 = \sigma^2 (1 + \operatorname{\sgn}(\Gamma) \operatorname{Le})
$$

The impact on option price for small $\tilde{\sigma} - \sigma$ is roughly $\veta \times (\tilde{\sigma} - \sigma)$ with

$$
  \tilde{\sigma} - \sigma \approx \frac{k}{\sqrt{2\pi\delta t}} \operatorname{sgn}(\Gamma) (\tilde{\sigma}^2 - \sigma^2) = \sigma^2 \operatorname{sgn}(\Gamma) \operatorname (Le)
$$

and

$$
  \tilde{\sigma}^2 - \sigma^2 = (\tilde{\sigma} + \sigma)(\tilde{\sigma} - \sigma) \approx 2\sigma(\tilde{\sigma} - \sigma)
$$

We can see that the more frequent the rebalancing (smaller $\d$), the higher will be the transaction cost.

# Risk-neural pricing framework

The derivative contracts with constant delta property are called delta-one products. The delta for such products can be determined by arbitrage without stochastic modelling. Other derivatives are often called options generally, whose delta is dynamic and requires stochastic modelling.

## Money market account and discounting

Let $r_t$ be an instantaneous interest rate, which can be deterministic of stochastic. The money market account is defined by the following process

$$
  M_t = \exp\left(\int_0^t r_s \;\d s \right)
$$

In particular, when $r_t$ is a constant $r$, we get

$$
  M_t = e^{rt}
$$

Here $M_t$ can be considered as the value of a default-free cash depoist account and $1/M_t$ can be used to discount cash flows at time $t$. We note the following properties

$$
\begin{equation*}
  \d M_t = r_t M_t \;\d t
\tag{\label{equation-30}}
\end{equation*}
$$

and

$$
\begin{equation*}
  \d\frac{1}{M_t} = -\frac{r_t}{M_t} \;\d t
\tag{\label{equation-31}}
\end{equation*}
$$

The discounting of an asset price $S_t$ is $S_t /M_t$, which can be understood as the number of units of money market account $M_t$. The role of money market account is the so-called numéraire, which is the asset used for financing.

By $\eqref{equation-30}$ and $\eqref{equation-31}$, and $\d t \;\d t = 0$ and $\d t\;\d W_t = 0$, we have the following special result on cross-variations with $M_t$ (i.e. money market account numéraire)

$$
\begin{align*}
  \d\Braket{\frac{1}{M_t}, S_t} =& 0 \\
  \d\Braket{M_t, S_T} =& 0 \tag{\label{equation-32}} \\
  \d\Braket{\frac{1}{M_t}, M_t} =& 0 \\
  \d\braket{M_t, M_t} =& 0
\end{align*}
$$

where $S_t$ is assumed to be an Itô process or a jump-diffusion process.

## Self-financing portfolio

Consider a portfolio of an asset with total return price $S_t$ and money market account $M_t$, i.e. $\Pi_t = \Delta_t S_t + \zeta_t M_t$, where $\Delta_t, \zeta_t$ are predictable processes (which may take negative values) and represemt respectively the units of the asset and the units in money market account.

<MathBox title="Self-financing portfolio" boxType="definition">
A self-financing portfolio is defined as the portfolio as the portfolio satisfying

$$
  \Pi_T = \Pi_0 + \int_0^T \Delta_t \;\d S_t + \int_0^T \zeta_t \;\d M_t,\, \forall T
$$
</MathBox>

For a self-financing portfolio, the re-balancing of the portions $\Delta_t$ and $\zeta_t$ in the asset and money market account respectively does not change the value of the portfolio, i.e. no money is brought in or taken out of the portfolio. The discounted portfolio process is

$$
  \frac{\Pi_t}{M_t} = \Delta_t \frac{S_t}{M_t} + \zeta_t
$$

which illustrates that the increment of a discounted self-financing portfolio process is only contributed by the increment of the discounted asset process $S_t /M_t$.

<MathBox title="" boxType="proposition">
Portfolio $\Pi_t = \Delta_t S_t + \zeta_t M_t$ is self-financing if and only if

$$
\begin{equation*}
  \frac{\Pi_t}{M_t} = \frac{\Pi_0}{M_0} + \sum_{i=1}^t \nabla_u^{(i)} \frac{S_u^{(i)}}{M_u}
\tag{\label{equation-34}}
\end{equation*}
$$

</MathBox>

By

$$
  \d \frac{S_t}{M_t} = \frac{1}{M_t} \;\d S_t + S_t \d\frac{1}{M_t} + \d\Braket{\frac{1}{M_t}, S_t}
$$

and $\eqref{equation-32}$, the increment of the discounted asset price for $\d t$ period is

$$
\begin{equation*}
  \d\frac{S_t}{M_t} =& \frac{1}{M_t}(\d S_t - \frac{S_t}{M_t} \;\d M_t) \\
  =& \frac{1}{M_t} (\d S_t - r_t S_t \;\d t)
\tag{\label{equation-33}}
\end{equation*}

We observe that
- $r_t S_t \;\d t$ is the financing cost for one unit of the asset
- $(\d S_t - r_s S_t \;\d t)$ is the self-financing return and alsoe the exess return, which corresponds to practical implementation of the financial market
- $\left(\d S_t - \frac{S_t}{M_t} \;\d M_t \right)$ leads to a more general interpretation of the return from a self-financing implementation consisting of a long position of $1$ unit of asset $S_t$ financed by a short position of $S_t /M_t$ units of $M_t$ (which is another asset).

By

$$
  \d\frac{\Pi_t}{M_t} = \frac{1}{M_t} \;\d\Pi_t + \Pi_t \;\d\frac{1}{M_t} + \d\Braket{\Pi_t, \frac{1}{M_t}}

and

$$
  \d\frac{\Pi_t}{M_t} = \Delta_t \;\d \frac{S_t}{M_t}
$$

and $\eqref{equation-33}$, we obtain

$$
  \d\Pi_t = \Delta_t \left(\d S_t - \frac{S_t}{M_t} \;\d M_t \right) + \frac{\Pi_t}{M_t} \;\d M_t
$$

The increment $\d\Pi_t$ of the self-financing portfolio is therefore composed of the self-financing return and portfolio carry.

## Risk-neutral probability measure

<MathBox title="Risk-neutral probability" boxType="definition">
A risk-neutral probability measure $\tilde{\mathbb{P}}$, is defined as the one under which the discounted total return price of any tradable asset $S_t$ is a martingale, i.e.

$$
  \tilde{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{S_t}{M_t} \mid \mathcal{F}_0 \right] = \frac{S_0}{M_0}
$$
</MathBox>

For a portfolio of tradable assets, as its price $\Pi_t$ is a linear combination of asset total return prices, $\Pi_t /M_t$ must be a martingale under $\tilde{\mathbb{P}}$.

We carry out the continuous-time modelling analysis on a total return asset price that follows the stochastic differential equation

$$
  \d S_t = \mu_t S_t \;\d t + \sigma_t S_t \;\d W_t
$$

where $\mu_t$ and $\sigma_t > 0$ are $\mathcal{F}_t$-adapted processes.

$$
  \d\frac{S_t}{M_t} = \sigma_t \frac{S_t}{M_t} \;\d\tilde{W_t}
$$

or equivalently

$$
  \d S_t = r_t S_t \;\d t + \sigma_t S_t \;\d\tilde{W}_t

## Pricing and hedging of derivatives

Let $V_T$ be the terminal payoff at $T$ of a derivative instrument on $S_T$. If there exists a self-financing process $\Pi_t$ such that $\Pi_T = V_T$, then the spot price of the derivative instrument $V_t$ can be defined as $V_t \equiv \Pi_t$ because $\Pi_t$ can perfectly replicate the derivative's price process as described by $\eqref{equation-34}$. The pricing formula can be obtained by taking the expectation on $\eqref{equation-34}$

$$
  V_t = M_t \tilde{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{V_T}{M_T} \mid \mathcal{F}_t \right]

Since $V_t /M_t$ is a $\tilde{\mathbb{P}}$-martingale, the $\d t$ term should be zero, which leads to the Black-Scholes equation $\eqref{equation-36}$. By Proposition $\ref{proposition-2}$ we have

$$
\begin{align*}
  \d\frac{V_t}{M_t} =& \frac{\partial V_t}{\partial S_t} \sigma_t \frac{S_t}{M_t} \;\d\tilde{W}_t \\
  =& \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} \d\frac{S_t}{M_t}
\end{align*}
$$

while Proposition $\ref{proposition-4}$ shows that

$$
  \d\frac{V_t}{M_t} = \Delta_t \d\frac{S_t}{M_t}
$$

Equating these two equations, we obtain the delta amount $\Delta_t = \frac{\partial V_t}{\partial S_t}$.
</details>
</MathBox>

For multi-asset derivatives the same analysis using multi-dimensional Itô-Doeblin formula and $\eqref{equation-37}$ leads to

$$
  \Delta_t^{(i)} = \frac{\partial V_t}{\partial S^{(i)}} (t, S_t^{(1)}, \dots, S_t^{(i)}, \dots)
$$

## Discussion on hedging

Self-financing must be satisfied in hedging transactions. Including a new self-financing position in a portfolio at time $t$ does not change the value of the portfolio at time $t$, e.g. assets funded by borrowing in money market for delta-hedging, funded options for vega hedging. A self-financing investment return can be derived from the increments of the discounted asset prices.

The purpose of a risk-neutral probability $\tilde{P}$ is to find a self-financing hedging process for derivatives, which is achieved by constructing $\tilde{\mathbb{P}}$ in such a way that both the discounted underlying asset price and the discounted option price are $\tilde{\mathbb{P}}$-martingales. Here $\tilde{\mathbb{P}}$ is a mere mathematical tool without an economic meaning.

To illustrate the hedging process, we rewrite $\eqref{equation-38}$

$$
  V_T = \frac{M_T}{M_t} V_t + \int_t^T M_T \Delta_u \;\d\frac{S_u}{M_u}
$$

Expanding $\d\frac{S_u}{M_u}$ with $M_u = e^{ru}$, we obtain the equivalent self-financing hedging process

$$
\begin{equation*}
  V_T = e^{r(T - t)} V_t + \int_t^T e^{r(T - u)} \Delta_u (\d S_u - S_u r\;\d u)
\tag{\label{equation-39}}
\end{equation*}
$$

where
- $e^{r(T - t)} V_t$ is the accrued value of the initial option price $V_t$
- the integral term is the accrued heding P/L, in which $r S_u \;\d u$ represents the financing cost for holding one underlying asset unit over $\d u$ period and $(\d S_u - S_u r_u \;\d u)$ or equivalently $(\d S_u - \frac{S_u}{M_u} \;\d M_u)$, is the self-financing return for the period.

The hedging process consists of holding $\Delta_u$ asset units at $u$ by self-financing
- if $\Delta_u \geq 0$, the cash amount of $\Delta_u S_u$ is borrowed to purchase $\Delta u$ asset units
- otherwise, $|\Delta_u|$ asset units are borrowed and sold at the prevailing price $S_u$ with the selling proceeds $|\Delta_u| S_u$ lent to money market

Given a hedging process as described by $\eqref{equation-39}$, it seems possible to derived the value of $V_t$ is the expectation of the integral term is zero under a certain probability. Under the risk-neutral probability $\tilde{\mathbb{P}}$, since $S_u /M_u$ is a martingale, the expectation of the increment of $S_u /M_u$ must be zero and the expectation of the integral of increments of $S_u /M_u$ must also be zero. This leads to the general pricing formula

$$
  V_t = \tilde{\mathbb{E}}[e^{-r(T - t)} V_t | \mathcal{F}_t]
$$

or more generally

$$
  V_t = M_t \tilde{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{V_T}{M_T} \mid| \mathcal{F}_t \right]
$$

To show the hedging over the period $\d t$, we can derive formula $\eqref{equation-38}$ to obtain

$$
  \d V_t = \Delta_t \left(\d S_t - \frac{S_t}{M_t} \;\d M_t \right) + \fra{V_t}{M_t} \;\d M_t
$$

It shows that $\d V_t$ is the sum of the self-financing delta-hedging P/L and the carry of the cash value of the hedging portfolio in money market. We note that $\d M_t = M_t r \;\d t$ can be understood as the interest produced by the cash amount of $M_t$ in money market.

Here $\Delta_t = \frac{\partial V_t}{\partial S_t}$, which is the ratio of option price change with respect to asset price change over $\d t$ period. If the ratio is $0.5$ at time $t$, it is intuitive that the holding of $0.5$ unit of the stock may roughly replicate the option price change by the stock price change. If the stock price increases by $\$1$, the option price will increase by roughly $\$ 0.5$, while the holding of $0.5$ stock will also have a gain of $\$ 0.5€. However, the option price convexity and self-financing cost may make people doubt about delta-hedging accuracy. Magically, the suspected error is actually perfectly offset by the time decay of the option price.
