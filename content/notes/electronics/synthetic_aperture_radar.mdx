Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) is an active microwave remote-sensing system that exploits the relative motion between the radar platform and the observed scene to synthesize a large effective aperture. As the platform moves, a given target is observed from a sequence of spatially separated antenna positions. The corresponding wideband complex echoes are coherently processed using their amplitude and phase histories to form a two-dimensional image, typically resolved in the range (along-track) and azimuth (across-track) directions.

In the range direction, SAR commonly employs linear frequency-modulated (LFM) waveforms with a large time-bandwidth product. Pulse compression permits a relatively long transmitted pulse, and hence a higher pulse energy, while providing the range resolution associated with a much shorter compressed pulse. This principle is fundamentally the same as that used in conventional pulse-compression radar.

In the azimuth direction, SAR exploits the motion of the radar platform to synthesize an aperture much larger than the physical antenna. The changing radar-to-target geometry produces a characteristic phase history, or equivalently a Doppler history, in the received signal. By coherently processing the echoes acquired over the target's synthetic-aperture interval, SAR achieves an azimuth resolution substantially finer than the real-aperture beamwidth would permit.

# Resolution

## Range resolution

The range resolution of a radar is the minimum separation between two point targets in the range diraction for which their echoes can be distinguished as two separate responses. It is useful to distinguish between slant-range resolution, measured along the radar line of sight, and ground-range resolution, measured along the ground surface.

Consider two point targets located at different slant ranges from the radar, with a slant range separation of $\Delta R_s$. The difference in round-trip propagation time between the two targets is

$$
  \Delta t = \frac{2\Delta R_s}{c}
$$

For a transmitted pulse of duration $\tau_p$, the temporal separation between the two received echoes must be sufficiently large for the echoes to be distinguished. The two echoes can be separated in time only if the they do not overlap, i.e. if $\Delta t \geq \tau_p$ implying

$$
\begin{equation*}
  \Delta R_s = \frac{c\tau_p}{2}
\tag{\label{equation-9}}
\end{equation*}
$$

Let $\theta$ denote the incidence angle, measured with respect to the local ground normal. For a locally flat surface, a small displacement in ground range $\Delta R_g$, corresponds to a slant-range displacement

$$
  \Delta R_s = \Delta R_g \sin(\theta)
$$

Thus, the ground-range resolution is

$$
\begin{equation*}
  \Delta R_g = \frac{\Delta R_s}{\sin(\theta)} = \frac{c\tau_p}{2\sin(\theta)}
\tag{\label{equation-10}}
\end{equation*}
$$

This expression assumes that the incidence angle remains approximately constant over the resolution cell. Several important implications can be drawn from this result:
1. **Loss of range resolution at nadir:** As $\theta \to 0$, $\sin(\theta) \to 0$ and $\Delta R_g \to\infty$, in which case the flat surface approximation breaks down. The reason is that iso-range contours (circles about the sensor) are tangent to the ground at nadir, so points on either side of the nadir at equal distance are indistinguishable in range. This is why imaging radars must be side-looking, with $\theta > 0$.

3. **Variation across the swath:** Since $\sin(\theta)$ increases monotonically with $\theta$ on $(0, \pi/2]$, then $\delta R_g$ decreases with increasing incidence angle. Ground-range resolution is therefore coarsest in the near range (small $\theta$) and finest in the far range (large $\theta$). This is the opposite of a nadir-viewing optical sensor, whose ground sample distance is finest at nadir and degrades off-nadir.

### Pulse compression

By $\eqref{equation-9}$, achieving a fine range resolution with an unmodulated radar pulse requires a very short pulse duration. However, for a given peak transmit power, reducing the pulse duration also reduces the energy transmitted in each pulse. This can reduce the received signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), particularly when observing weak targets or operating at long ranges.

Pulse compression provides a way to overcome this trade-off. A radar can transmit a relatively long-duration waveform, thereby transmitting more energy, while modulating the waveform so that it can be compressed to a much shorter effective duration after reception. Linear frequency-modulated (LFM) waveforms, commonly called chirps, are widely used for this purpose.

#### Linear modulated chirp

An LFM chirp can be represented as

$$
\begin{equation*}
  s(t) = p(t) \exp\left[i2\pi\left(f_0 t + \frac{k}{2}t^2 \right)\right]
\tag{\label{equation-11}}
\end{equation*}
$$

where $p(t)$ is the pulse envelope

$$
  p(t) = \operatorname{rect}\left(\frac{t}{\tau_p} \right) = \begin{cases}
    1,\quad& |t| \leq \frac{\tau_p}{2} \\
    0, \quad& |t| > \frac{\tau_p}{2}
  \end{cases}
$$

Here $f_0$ is the carrier frequency, $\tau_p$ is the transmitted pulse duration, and $k$ is the chirp rate, with unit $\tunit{Hz/s}$. The instantaneous phase of the waveform is $\phi(t) = 2\pi f_0 t + \pi k t^2$. The instantaneous frequency $f(t)$ is given by

$$
  f(t) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \frac{\d\phi(t)}{\d t} = f_0 + kt
$$

which varies linearly with time. At the centre of the pulse, $t = 0$, the instantaneous frequency equals the carrier frequency $f (0) = f_0$. For a pulse extending from $-\tau_p /2$ to $\tau_p /2$, the chirp bandwidth is

$$
  B = \left| f\left(\frac{\tau_p}{2} \right) - f\left(-\frac{\tau_p}{2} \right) \right| = |K| \tau_p
$$

In terms of angular frequency, the chirp can be written as

$$
  c(t) = p(t) \exp\left[i \left(\omega_0 t + \frac{\gamma t^2}{2} \right)\right]
$$

where $\omega_0 = 2\pi f_0$ and $\gamma = \d\omega(t) /\d t$ is the angular-frequency chirp rate, with units of $\tunit{rad/s^2}$. The instantaneous angular frequency is

$$
  \omega(t) = \frac{\d}{\d t} \left(\omega_0 t + \frac{\gamma}{2} t^2 \right) = \omega_0 + \gamma t
$$

The two chirp-rate conventions are related by $\gamma = 2\pi k$.

#### Matched filtering

After reception, the radar correlates the received signal with a replica of the transmitted chirp using a matched filter. For a known transmitted waveform, the matched filter provides the optimum linear filter for detecting the waveform in additive white Gaussian noise. Its impulse response is proportional to the time-reversed complex conjugate of the transmitted signal:

$$
  h(t) = C s^* (-t)
$$

where $C$ is a constant that determines the filter gain. For the LFM in $\eqref{equation-11}$

$$
  s^* (-t) = p(-t) \exp\left[-i2\pi \left(-f_0 t + \frac{k}{2}t^2 \right)\right]
$$

Since the rectangular pulse envelope is even, $p(-t) = p(t)$, the matched-filter impulse response can be written as

$$
  h(t) = C p(t) \exp\left[i2\pi \left(f_0 t - \frac{k}{2} t^2 \right)\right]
$$

Suppose a target produces an echeo with propagation delay $t_0$ and Doppler frequency $f_d$. Ignoring amplitude attenuation and an arbitrary constant phase, the received complex signal can be modeled as

$$
  r(t) = p(t - t_0) \exp\left[i2\pi \left(f_0 (t - t_0) + \frac{k}{2}(t - t_0)^2 + f_d t \right) \right]
$$

The matched-filter output is the convolution of the received signal with the filter impulse response

$$
\begin{align*}
  g(t_0, \omega_d) =& (r * h)(t) \\
  =& K \int_{-\tau/2}^{\tau/2} \cos\left[(\omega_0 + \omega_d)t + \frac{1}{2} k(2\pi)t^2 \right] \cos[\omega_0 (t_0 - t)] + \frac{1}{2}k(2\pi) (t_0 - t)^2 \;\d t \\
  =& G \cos\left[\left(\omega_0 + \frac{\omega_d}{2} \right) t_0 \right] \frac{\sin\left[\frac{\omega_d + 2\pi k t_0}{2} (\tau - |t_0|) \right]}{\frac{\omega_d + 2\pi kt_0}{2}}
\end{align*}
$$

The return echo from the target is down converted by shifting the spectrum of return signal according to the reference signal carrier frequency and filtering the result to recover only the frequency band centred about baseband frequency with bandwidth $B$. Both in-phase and quadrature-phase components of the signal can be extracted and this operation is known as “I, Q detection”. The output of the filtering process can be written as,

$$
  g(t - t_0) = E_0^2 B \sin[c\pi] B(t_t_0)
$$

##### Range resolution after pulse compression

Since the characteristic duration of the LFM compressed pulse is of order $1/B$, the corresponding slant-range resolution is approximately

$$
  \Delta R_s \approx \frac{c}{2B} = \frac{c}{2|K|\tau_p}
$$

while the ground-range resolution is

$$
  \Delta R_s \approx \frac{c}{2B \sin(\theta)}
$$

The transmitted pulse duration and waveform bandwidth play different roles:
- The pulse duration $\tau_p$ largely determines the amount of energy that can be transmitted in each pulse for a given peak power.
- The bandwidth $B$ primarily determines the achievable range resolution.

## Azimuth resolution

In SAR systems, high resolution in the azimuth direction (parallel to the platform flight direction) is achieved through synthetic aperture techniques, which is the fundamental difference between SAR systems and other radar systems. SAR systems utilize the motion of the radar antenna to form a virtual antenna array, sequentially acquiring and storing the echo signals during platform motion.

Let
- $D_\text{az}$ be the physical antenna dimension in the azimuth direction
- $\lambda$ be the radar wavelength
- $R$ be the slant range from the radar to the target

For a real-aperture antenna, the azimuth beamwidth can be approximated as

$$
  \beta_\text{az} \approx K_\beta \frac{\lambda}{D_\text{az}}
$$

where $K_\beta$ depends on the antenna illumination and on the definition of beamwidth. A commonly used value is $K_\beta \approx 0.886$. The target therefore remains within the antenna beam over an approximate along-track distance $L_\text{sa} \approx R \beta_\text{az}$. Thus

$$
\begin{equation*}
  L_\text{sa} \approx K_\beta \frac{\lambda R}{D_\text{az}}
\tag{\label{equation-3}}
\end{equation*}
$$

where $L_\text{sa}$ is the physical synthetic-aperture length associated with the target. 

It is important to distinguish this quantity from the physical antenna length. The radar antenna itself does not become physically longer. Rather, the motion of the platform allows the radar to collect coherent observations from many different positions, effectively synthesizing a much larger aperture.

If the platform velocity is $v$, the corresponding synthetic-aperture time is approximately

$$
  T_\text{sa} = \frac{L_\text{sa}}{v}
$$

### Target phase history

The mechanism that enables SAR focusing is the changing radar-to-target distance as the platform moves.

Let $\eta$ denote slow time, measured relatively to the point of closest approach to the target. Assuming a straight flight path and a stationary target, the instantaneous slant range is

$$
  R(\eta) = \sqrt{R_0^2 + (v\eta)^2}
$$

where $R_0$ is the minimum slant range.

For $v|\eta| \ll R_0$, a second-order Taylor expansion gives

$$
\begin{equation*}
  R(\eta) = R_0 + \frac{v^2 \eta^2}{2 R_0} + O(\eta^3)
\tag{\label{equation-1}}
\end{equation*}
$$

The phase $\phi$ of the received signal is determined by the two-way propagation path

$$
  \phi(\eta) = -\frac{4\pi}{\lambda} R(\eta)
$$

Substituting $\eqref{equation-1}$ gives

$$
  \phi(\eta) = -\frac{4\pi R_0}{\lambda} - \frac{2\pi v^2}{\lambda R_0} \eta^2
$$

During signal processing, the phase introduced by the changing distance between the radar and the target is compensated, and the multiple echo signals from the same target are coherently added to achieve high azimuth resolution.

### Azimuth resolution for stripmap SAR

For a physical synthetic-aperture length $L_{sa}$, the effective angular resolution can be approximated as

$$
\begin{equation*}
  \beta_\text{SAR} \approx K_\beta \frac{\lambda}{2L_\text{sa}}
\tag{\label{equation-2}}
\end{equation*}
$$

where the factor of $2$ follows from the two-way propagation phase of the radar signal. The corresponding azimuth resolution is therefore

$$
  \Delta R_\text{az} \approx R\beta_\text{SAR}
$$

Substituting $\eqref{equation-2}$ gives

$$
  \Delta R_\text{az} \approx K_\beta \frac{\lambda R}{2 L_\text{sa}}
$$

Inserting the real-aperture beamwidth $\eqref{equation-3}$ results in

$$
\begin{align*}
  \Delta R_\text{az} \approx& K_\beta \frac{\lambda R}{2K_\beta \lambda R / D_\text{az}} \\
  =& \frac{D_\text{az}}{2}
\end{align*}
$$

which shows that the azimuth resolution of conventional stripmap SAR with is approximately independent of the radar-to-target range. This differs from real-aperture radar, where the azimuth resolution becomes poorer as the target moves farther away because the antenna beam covers a larger physical area.

SAR compensates for this effect through the synthetic aperture; the synthetic-aperture length increases approximately linearly with range, $L_\text{sa} \propto R$. At greater range, the target subtends a smaller angular extent at the antenna, but the target remains illuminated over a proportionally longer distance along the flight path. Consequently

$$
  \Delta R_\text{az} \propto \frac{R}{L_\text{sa}} \propto \frac{R}{R}
$$

The range dependence therefore cancels, leaving the approximate result

$$
\begin{equation*}
  \Delta R_\text{az} \approx \frac{D_\text{az}}{2}
\tag{\label{equation-5}}
\end{equation*}
$$

# Signal-to-noise ratio

The received signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) is a fundamental parameter in SAR system design because it determines whether the backscattered signal from the observed scene can be detected and coherently processed with sufficient quality. For a distributed target and under the assumptions of a monostatic SAR system, the SNR after range compression and coherent integration over the synthetic aperture can be expressed in the form

$$
\begin{equation*}
  \mathrm{SNR} = \frac{P_t G_t G_r \lambda^3 f_\text{PRF} T_p c \sigma_0}{4 (4\pi)^3 R^3 v_s \sin(\eta) F k T B_r}
\tag{\label{equation-4}}
\end{equation*}
$$

where 
- $P_t$ is the peak power of the system transmit signal
- $T_p$ is the pulse duration
- $G_t$ and $G_r$ are the transmit and receive antenna gains
- $\sigma_0$ is the normalized radar cross section, or backscattering coefficient, of the detection target
- $R$ is the range of the detection target
- $F$ is the noise factor
- $k = \scinot{1.38}{-23} \unit{J/K}$ is the Boltzmann constant
- $T$ is the system ambient temperatur
- $B_r$ is the signal bandwidth
- $f_\text{PRF}$ is the pulse repetition frequency
- $\eta$ is the incidence angle

Ignoring parameters that are fixed for a given system and operating geometry, the SNR $\eqref{equation-4}$ has the approximate dependence

$$
  \mathrm{SNR} \propto \frac{P_t G_t G_r \lambda^3 f_\text{PRF} T_p}{R^3 v_s \sin(\eta) F T B_r}
$$

Several conclusions can be drawn from this relationship.

## Transmit power

Increasing the peak transmit power $P_t$ increases the received SNR approximately linearly, 

$$
  \mathrm{SNR} \propto P_t
$$

However, increasing the peak power places greater demands on the transmitter, including the power amplifier, high-voltage supply, thermal management, and radio frequency components. These requirements can increase system size, mass, power consumption, cost, and design complexity, while potentially reducing reliability.

## Antenna gain

Increasing the transmit and receive antenna gains also improves SNR, 

$$
  \mathrm{SNR} \propto G_t G_r
$$

For a monostatic system with the same antenna used for transmission and reception, $G_t = G_r = G$ such that $\mathrm{SNR} \propto G^2$. However, antenna size also affects other SAR performance parameters. In particular, increasing the physical antenna dimension in the azimuth direction generally reduces the real-aperture beamwidth and, for conventional stripmap SAR, increases the achievable azimuth resolution according to $\eqref{equation-5}$. Thus, increasing the antenna size does not generally reduce SAR resolution. In the azimuth direction, a larger antenna can actually provide finer resolution. Nevertheless, increasing antenna size introduces practical constraints involving antenna mass, mechanical structure, beamwidth, swath width, and system integration.

## Signal bandwidth

The SNR expression contains an inverse dependence on the processed range bandwidth,

$$
  \mathrm{SNR} \propto \frac{1}{B_r}
$$

For fixed transmitted pulse energy and all other parameters unchanged, reducing the receiver noise bandwidth reduces the integrated thermal noise power and therefore increases the SNR. However, reducing the transmitted bandwidth directly degrades range resolution. For a conventional pulse-compression SAR system, the range resolution $\eqref{equation-6}$ is inversely proportional to the bandwidth. Thus, increasing $B_r$ improves range resolution but generally increases the noise bandwidth and consequently places a greater SNR requirement on the system. This represents an important fundamental trade-off between range resolution and SNR.

## Pulse duration and transmitted energy

The energy contained in transmitted pulse, $E_p$, is the time integral of the intantaneous transmit power

$$
  E_p = \int_0^{T_p} P_t (t) \;\d t
$$

For a rectangular pulse with constant peak power $P_t$ and duration $T_p$, the transmitted energy per pulse reduces to $E_p = P_t T_p$.

From the SAR SNR expression $\eqref{equation-4}$, the SNR scales with the product of peak transmit power and pulse duration, $\mathrm{SNR} \propto P_t T_p$. This implies that. for a fixed radar configuration, the received SNR is fundamentally related to the transmitted pulse energy

$$
  \mathrm{SNR} \propto E_p
$$

For a fixed peak transmit power $P_t$, increasing the pulse duration $T_p$ increases the transmitted pulse energy linearly. This is one of the principal motivations for pulse compression in SAR. A long-duration linearly frequency-modulated (LFM) pulse can transmit substantially more energy than a short pulse at the same peak power, while its large bandwidth allows the received signal to be compressed to achieve fine range resolution.

For example, an LFM pulse can have a duration $T_p$ that is much longer than the inverse of its bandwidth, $B_r T_p \gg 1$, while still providing a compressed-pulse width on the order of

$$
  \tau_\text{comp} \sim \frac{1}{B_r}
$$

Thus, $T_p$ and $B_r$ play fundamentally different roles: $T_p$ primarily determines the amount of energy transmitted for a given peak power, while $B_r$ primarily determines the achievable range resolution via $\eqref{equation-6}$. This separation is not completely independent in a practical system, since waveform design, transmitter limitations, sampling requirements, sidelobes, and processing constraints impose additional relationships between these parameters.

### Peak transmit power versus pulse duration

The relationship $E_p = P_t T_p$ establishes a direct trade-off between peak transmit power and pulse duration for a fixed pulse energy. Suppose the pulse duration is increased by a factor $K > 1$,

$$
  T'_p = KT_p
$$

To maintain the same pulse energy, the peak transmit power can be reduced by the same factor

$$
  P'_t = \frac{P_t}{K}
$$

The resuling pulse energy is

$$
\begin{align*}
  E'_p =& P'_t T'_p = \frac{P_t}{K} (KT_p) \\
  =& P_t T_p = E_p
\end{align*}
$$

and, under otherwise identical conditions, then SNR is preserved. This result illustrates an important SAR system-design principle: for a fixed required pulse energy, peak transmit power and pulse duration can, within practical limits, be traded against each other.

Rather than achieving the required SNR through a very high peak transmit power, the radar can transmit a longer waveform at a lower peak power while maintaining approximately the same pulse energy and, consequently, approximately the same SNR.

However, the trade-off is subject to practical constraints. The pulse duration cannot be increased indefinitely because it affects the SAR timing architecture, duty cycle, minimum and unambiguous range, range ambiguities, transmitter–receiver isolation, and other waveform and system-level constraints. Therefore, a longer pulse should be viewed not as a universally lossless improvement, but as a means of redistributing the required transmitted energy from peak power toward pulse duration, with the achievable benefit determined by the overall SAR architecture.

## Pulse-repetition frequency and average transmit power

The average transmit power $\bar{P}_t$ of a pulsed SAR transmitter is determined by the energy transmitted per pulse $E_p$ and the pulse-repetition frequency $f_\text{PRF}$. For a rectangular pulse with peak transmit power $P_t$ and pulse duration $T_p$, the energy transmitted per pulse is $E_p = P_t T_p$. The average transmit power is therefore

$$
\begin{equation*}
  \bar{P}_t = E_p f_\text{PRF} = P_t T_p f_\text{PRF}
\tag{\label{equation-7}}
\end{equation*}
$$

It is useful to introduce the pulse-repetition interval (PRI)

$$
  T_\text{PRI} = \frac{1}{f_\text{PRF}}
$$

The transmit duty cycle $D$ is defined as the fraction of each PRI during which the transmitter is actively transmitting. For a non-overlapping pulsed waveform with $T_p \leq T_\text{PRI}$, the duty cycle is

$$
  D = \frac{T_p}{T_\text{PRI}} = T_p f_\text{PRF} \in (0, 1]
$$

Consequently, the average transmit power can also be written $\bar{P}_t = P_t D$.

For a low-duty-cycle pulsed SAR, $T_p \ll T_\text{PRI}$, and thus $D \ll 1$. The peak transmit power can therefore be substantially higher than the average transmit power

$$
  P_t = \frac{\bar{P}_t}{D}
$$

This distinction is important for transmitter design. A pulsed SAR may require a high peak power to generate sufficient pulse energy, even though its average electrical power consumption associated with RF transmission is considerably lower.

### Relationship between SNR and average transmit power

In the SAR SNR expression $\eqref{equation-4}$, the relevant dependence on transmit power, pulse duration and pulse-repetition frequency is $\mathrm{SNR} \propto P_t T_p f_\text{PRF}$. Using $\eqref{equation-7}$, this can be expressed as

$$
  \mathrm{SNR} \propto \bar{P}_t = P_t D = E_p f_\text{PRF}
$$

For a fixed average transmit power, the same radio frequency energy can be distributed between a higher peak power with a lower duty cycle or a lower peak power with a higher duty cycle. 

For example, if the duty cycle is increased by a factor $K$, while the average transmit power is held constant, the required peak power decreases by the same factor $D' = KD$, and thus

$$
  P'_t = \frac{\bar{P}_t}{D'} = \frac{P_t D}{K D} = \frac{P_t}{K}
$$

The corresponding pulse energy becomes $E'_p = P'_t T'_p$. If the pulse duration is also increased by the same factor, $T_p'=KT_p$, then

then

$$
  E_p' = \frac{P_t}{K}(KT_p) = P_t T_p = E_p
$$

Thus, the pulse energy remains unchanged while the peak transmit power is reduced.

In practice, however, the pulse duration and duty cycle cannot be increased arbitrarily. They are constrained by the required PRF, unambiguous range, range ambiguities, minimum range, transmitter/receiver switching, thermal considerations, and the SAR acquisition geometry.

### Frequency-modulated continuous wave SAR

Frequency-modulated continuous-wave (FMCW) SAR provides an alternative waveform architecture. Instead of transmitting isolated high-power pulses with a low duty cycle, an FMCW SAR continuously transmits a frequency-modulated waveform, typically with a duty cycle approaching unity, $D \approx 1$. For an FMCW waveform with approximately continuous transmission, the peak transmit power and average transmit power are approximately equal, $\bar{P} = P_t$.

For a given average transmitted power, an FMCW SAR can therefore operate with a substantially lower peak transmit power than a conventional pulsed SAR. This can relax the requirements on the power amplifier, power supply, thermal management, and other radio frequency components.

The range resolution of an FMCW SAR is still primarily determined by the signal bandwidth given by $\eqref{equation-6}$.

# Point target analysis

Because a SAR imaging system is, to a good approximation, a linear system, its performance can be characterized by its impulse response. The impulse response is obtained by measuring the system response to an isolated point-like scatterer on the ground, such as a corner reflector. Such a discrete scatterer is commonly referred to as a point target. Many important SAR image-quality parameters can be estimated from the measured point-target response. Because a SAR image is two-dimensional, several of these parameters must be evaluated independently in the two image-coordinate directions, typically the range and azimuth directions.

In a processed complex SAR image, the response to a point target has a sinc-like main lobe and sidelobe structure, rather than being a pure sine function. Two of the most important image-quality parameters derived from the point-target response are the impulse-response width (IRW), which characterizes spatial resolution, and the peak sidelobe ratio (PSLR) and integrated sidelobe ratio (ISLR), which characterize different aspects of image contrast and energy spreading.

Because the processed image is sampled, the peak of a point-target response will generally fall between image samples. Consequently, the sampled peak and its local structure cannot, in general, be characterized accurately without interpolation. A common procedure is therefore to extract a two-dimensional window, or chip, centered approximately on the point-target peak, typically containing between $16\times16$ and $64\times64$ samples. The chip is then interpolated or expanded by a factor of approximately 8 or 16. A Fourier-domain interpolator, implemented by taking the discrete Fourier transform (DFT), zero-padding the spectrum, and applying an inverse DFT (IDFT), provides an effective sinc-like interpolation of the sampled data.

More specifically, if the sampled point-target response is represented by $p[m,n]$, its two-dimensional DFT is computed and the resulting spectrum is embedded in a larger array by zero padding, with the spectral samples positioned consistently with the desired interpolation convention. Applying the inverse two-dimensional DFT then produces a more densely sampled representation of the same band-limited response. The procedure does not add information to the original data; it provides a more finely sampled representation from which the peak, nulls, sidelobes, and phase behavior can be estimated more accurately.

The expanded impulse response can be analyzed both as a two-dimensional surface and through one-dimensional profiles taken through the peak.

In the following discussion, let 

$$
\begin{equation*}
  p(t_1, t_2) = |p(t_1, t_2)| e^{i\phi(t_1, t_2)}
\tag{\label{equation-13}}
\end{equation*}
$$

denote the complex point-target response. The phase is then

$$
  \phi (t_1, t_2) = \operatorname{arg}[p(t_1, t_2)] \pmod{2\pi}
$$

## Impulse-response width (IRW)

The impulse-response width (IRW) is the width of the main lobe measured at a specified amplitude or power level. A common convention is the 3-dB width, defined as the distance between the two points at which the impulse-response magnitude falls to $1/\sqrt{2}$ of its peak value.

Let $p(t)$ denote the impulse response as a function of a spatial coordinate $t$, and lets is peak magnitude be

$$
  A_\text{peak} = \max_t |p(t)|
$$

The two 3-dB points $t_-$ and $t_+$ are defined by

$$
  20\log_{10} \left(\frac{|p(t_\pm)|}{A_\text{peak}}\right) = -3.0103 \unit{dB},
$$

or, equivalently,

$$
  |p(t_\pm)| = \frac{A_\text{peak}}{\sqrt{2}}.
$$

The 3-dB impulse-response width is then

$$
  \mathrm{IRW}_{3 \unit{dB}} = t_+ - t_-
$$

where $t_-$ and $t_+$ denote the crossings on either side of the main-lobe peak. The exact 3-dB level corresponds to a power ratio of $1/2$, or an amplitude ratio of $1/\sqrt{2}$. Then value $-3 \unit{dB}$ is rounded from $-3.0103 \unit{dB}$.

For a two-dimensional synthetic aperture radar (SAR) image, the IRW is generally measured independently along the range and azimuth directions, yielding $\mathrm{IRW}_r$ and $\mathrm{IRW}_a$, respectively, or along the corresponding image-coordinate axes $\mathrm{IRW}_x$ and $\mathrm{IRW}_y$. These measurements characterize the width of the focused response in each direction and are commonly used as indicators of spatial resolution.

The IRW may be reported in image samples or pixels. For physical interpretation, however, it is preferable to express it in units of distance, such as metres, using the appropriate spatial sampling interval or a georeferenced coordinate system. For a locally uniform sampling interval $\Delta x$, for example, an IRW measured as $N_x$ samples corresponds approximately to

$$
  \mathrm{IRW}_\text{x,phys}$ = N_x \Delta x
$$

provided that the sample spacing adequately represents the spatial coordinate in question. Where the image geometry is nonuniform or the image is projected onto the ground, the relevant ground-projected distances should be used instead.

## Peak sidelobe ratio (PSLR)

The peak sidelobe ratio (PSLR) is the ratio of the largest sidelobe magnitude to the peak magnitude of the main lobe. Define

$$
  A_\text{peak} = \max_t |p(t)|
$$

If $\mathcal{S}$ denotes the region containing the sidelobes, excluding the main-lobe region, the maximum sidelobe magnitude is

$$
  A_\text{SL,max} = \max_{t\in\mathcal{S}} |p(t)|
$$

The PLSR is then defined as

$$
  \mathrm{PSLR} = 20\log_{10} \left(\frac{A_\text{SL,max}}{A_\text{peak}}\right) \unit{dB}
$$

Because $A_\mathrm{SL,max} < A_\text{peak}$ for a conventional impulse response, the PSLR is negative. Thus, more negative PLSR values indicate lower sidelobe levels relative to the main-lobe peak.

PSLR is particularly important when targets of substantially different radar cross sections are spatially close. The sidelobes generated by a strong point target can extend into the response of a nearby weaker target, potentially masking it or biasing its measured amplitude. Thus, PSLR provides a quantitative measure of the peak sidelobe contamination associated with a point-target response.

### Rectangular spectral weighting

For an ideal rectangular spectral weighting, the corresponding one-dimensional impulse response is a sinc function,

$$
  p(t)\propto \sinc(\pi Bt) = \frac{\sin(\pi Bt)}{\pi Bt}
$$

where $B$ is the processed signal bandwidth. The first sidelobe occurs at the first local maximum of the sinc function outside the main lobe. If $x_1$ denotes the positive solutions of

$$
  \tan(x) = x,\, x_1 \in (\pi, 2\pi)
$$

then the first-sidelobe-to-peak amplitude ratio is

$$
  \frac{A_\text{SL,1}}{A_\text{peak}} = |\sinc(x_1)| \approx 0.21712
$$

Consequently

$$
  \mathrm{PSLR} = 20 \log_{10} \left(A_\text{SL,1}}{A_\text{peak} \right) \approx -13.26 \unit{dB}
$$

The value is independent of $B$: changing the bandwidth changes the scale of the main lobe, and hence the resolution, but not the normalized sidelobe level of the ideal sinc response.

### Spectral tapering

Applying a tapered spectral weighting suppresses sidelobes relative to the rectangular case. In general, this introduces a trade-off: stronger tapering produces lower sidelobe levels but broadens the main lobe, thereby reducing spatial resolution.

Consequently, PSLR and impulse-response width (IRW) should generally be considered together. A more negative PSLR indicates greater suppression of the strongest isolated sidelobe, whereas a smaller IRW indicates a narrower impulse response and hence finer spatial resolution. Improving one characteristic through spectral weighting can therefore come at the expense of the other.

A value around $-20 \unit{dB}$ is a commonly encountered design target, although the appropriate requirement depends on the SAR system and application.

## Isolated sidelobe ratio (ISLR)

### One dimension

Although a SAR impulse response is intrinsically two-dimensional, its sidelobe performance is often characterized first along the range and azimuth dimensions separately. The integrated sidelobe ratio (ISLR) quantifies the total sidelobe energy relative to the energy contained in the main lobe.

Let $\mathcal{W}$ be the finite analysis interval over which the response is evaluated. Define the main-lobe region as $\mathcal{M} \subsetneq \mathcal{W}$. The integrated main-lobe power is

$$
\begin{equation*}
  P_\text{main} = \int_\mathcal{M} |p(t)|^2 \;\d t
\tag{\label{equation-12}}
\end{equation*}
$$

while the total power within the analysis window is

$$
  P_\text{main} = \int_\mathcal{W} |p(t)|^2,dt
$$

The sidelobe power is therefore 

$$
\begin{align*}
  P_\text{SL} =& P_\text{total} - P_\text{main} \\
  \int_{\mathcal{W}\setminus\mathcal{M}} |p(t)|^2 \;\d t
\end{align*}
$$

The one-dimensional ISLR is defined as

$$
  \text{ISLR} = 10\log_{10} \left(\frac{P_\mathrm{total}-P_\mathrm{main}}{P_\mathrm{main}}\right) \unit{dB}
$$

where the factor of $10$, rather than $20$, is used because the ratio is formed from integrated power (energy) rather than amplitude.

Unlike PSLR, which depends only on the largest sidelobe, ISLR characterizes the total sidelobe energy. It therefore provides a measure of the cumulative sidelobe contamination produced by a strong target or illuminated region and can be particularly relevant when assessing the extent to which sidelobe energy contributes to surrounding weak or dark regions.

The numerical value of ISLR depends on the definition of the main-lobe region $\mathcal{M}$. A common convention is to define the main-lobe interval as a specified multiple of the IRW, centered on the peak:

$$
  \mathbb{M} = \left[t_0 - \frac{\alpha \cdot \mathrm{IRW}}{2}, t_0 + \frac{\alpha \cdot \mathrm{IRW}}{2}\right]
$$

where $t_0$ is the location of the main-lobe peak and $\alpha$ is a prescribed dimensionless factor. If the IRW is the 3-dB width, for example, this definition explicitly specifies the extent of the integration region in terms of that measured width. Values of $\alpha$ in the approximate range $2$-$2.5$ are sometimes used, but there is no universal value.

An alternative, and often more physically intrinsic, convention is to define the main lobe as the interval between its first nulls:

$$ 
  \mathcal{M} = [t_{-1}, t_{+1}]
$$

where $t_{-1}$ and $t_{+1}$ are the nearest zeros of the impulse response on either side of the main-lobe peak. Under this convention, the sidelobe region consists of the portions of the analysis window outside the first-null-to-first-null interval. Because these definitions integrate over different regions, ISLR values obtained using different main-lobe conventions are not directly interchangeable. An ISLR specification should therefore state both the main-lobe definition and the analysis window $\mathcal{W}$ used for the calculation

For an ideal sinc-like response with rectangular spectral weighting, a one-dimensional ISLR of approximately

$$
  \mathrm{ISLR }\approx -17.0\ \mathrm{dB}
$$

is obtained when the main lobe is defined by its first nulls.

### Two dimensions

The two-dimensional ISLR extends the preceding definition to the full point-target response. Let $\mathcal{M}_\text{2D}$ denote the two-dimensional main-lobe region, for example a rectangle centered on the peak and defined by specified multiples of the IRW in the two image-coordinate directions:

$$
\begin{align*}
  \mathcal{M}_\text{2D} =& \left[t_{1,0} - \frac{\alpha_x \mathrm{IRW}_x}{2}, t{1,0} + \frac{\alpha_x\mathrm{IRW}_x}{2}\right]
  &\times \left[t{2,0} - \frac{\alpha_y \mathrm{IRW}y}{ 2 }, t{2,0} + \frac{\alpha_y \mathrm{IRW}_y}{2}\right]
\end{align*}
$$

where $\alpha_x$ and $\alpha_y$ specify the chosen main-lobe extents. Other definitions, such as a region bounded by the first nulls in each direction, are also possible.

The main-lobe power is

$$
  P_\text{main,2D} = \iint_{\mathcal{M}_\text{2D}} |p(t_1,t_2)|^2 \;\d t_1 \;\d t_2
$$

and the total power is evaluated over the specified two-dimensional analysis region $\mathcal{W}_\text{2D}$:

$$
  P_\text{total,2D} = \iint_{\mathcal{W}_{2D}} |p(t_1,t_2)|^2 \;\d t_1 \;\d t_2.
$$

The two-dimensional ISLR is consequently

$$
  \mathrm{ISLR}_\text{2D} = 10\log_{10} \left(\frac{P_\mathrm{total,2D} - P_\mathrm{main,2D}}{P_\mathrm{main,2D}}\right) \unit{dB}
$$

An approximate relationship between the two-dimensional and one-dimensional ISLRs can be obtained under the assumption that the impulse response is separable:

$$
  p_1(t_1, t_2) = C p_x (t_1) p_y (t_2)
$$

where $C$ is a constant and $p_x$ and $p_y$ are the corresponding one-dimensional impulse responses.

Suppose, in addition, that both the main-lobe region and analysis regions are Cartesian products of their one-dimensional counterparts

$$
\begin{align*}
  \mathcal{M}_\text{2D} =& \mathcal{M}_x \times \mathcal{M}_y \\
  \mathcal{W}_\text{2D} =& \mathcal{W}_x \times \mathcal{W}_y
\end{align*}
$$

Because

$$
  |p(t_1, t_2)|^2 = |C|^2 |p_x (t_1)|^2 |p_y (t_2)|^2
$$

the two-dimensional integrals factor into products of one-dimensional integrals. Using the one-dimensional main-lobe power $\eqref{equation-12}$, we define the linear ISRL

$$
  r_i 0 \frac{P_\text{SL,i}}{P_\text{main,i}},\, i = x, y
$$

Thus, the one-dimensional total-to-min power ratios are

$$
  \frac{P_\text{total,x}}{P_\text{total,y}} = 1 + r_i,\, i = x, y
$$

Under the separability assumptions, the corresponding two-dimensional total-to-main ratio is therefore

$$
  \frac{P_\text{total,2D}}{P_\text{main,2D}} = (1 + r_x)(1 + r_y)
$$

Consequently, the two-dimensional linear sidelobe-to-main ratio is

$$
\begin{align*}
  r_\text{2D} =& \frac{P_\text{SL,2D}}{P_\text{main,2D}} \\
  =& (1 + r_x)(1 + r_y) - 1 = r_x + r_y + r_x r_y
\end{align*}
$$

The exact relationship between the corresponding decibel ISLRs is therefore

$$
  \mathrm{ISLR}_\text{2D} = 10 \log_{10} (r_x + r_y + r_x r_y)
$$

If the two one-dimensional ISLRs are equal, $r_x = r_y = r$, then $r_\text{2D} = 2r + r^2$ and

$$
  \mathrm{ISLR}_\text{2D} = 10\log_{10} (2r + r^2)
$$

When the sidelobe energy is small compared with the main-lobe energy, $r\ll 1$, the quadratic $r^2$ term is negligible compare with $2r$, giving

$$
  \mathrm{ISLR}_{2D} \approx 10\log{10} (2r)
$$

Since

$$
  10 \log_{10} (2r) = 10 \log_{10} (r) + 10 \log_{10} (2)
$$

and $10 \log_{10} (2) \approx 3.0103 \unit{dB}$, we obtain

$$
  \mathrm{ISLR}_{2D} \approx \mathrm{ISLR}_{1D} + 3.0103 \unit{dB}
$$

This approximation relies on separability and on the particular definition of the two-dimensional main-lobe region; it should therefore not be treated as a general identity.

## Peak position

The peak position is the image-coordinate location at which the magnitude of the impulse response attains its maximum. The continuous peak position is defined by

$$
  (t_{1,\text{max}}, t_{2,\text{max}}) = \argmax_{(t_1, t_2) |p(t_1, t_2)|
$$

For sampled SAR data, the maximum generally occurs between pixel centers. The discrete estimate

$$
  (\hat{t}_{1,\text{max}}, \hat{t}_{2,\text{max}}) = \argmax_{(m, n) |p[m,n]|
$$

therefore provides only a quantized estimate of the continuous peak position. Subpixel interpolation or parametric fitting is normally used when higher positional accuracy is required.

### Geometric interpretation

Peak-position measurements are important for assessing SAR geometric accuracy, because the estimated image position of a known point target can be compared with its independently known ground position. A SAR image is typically associated with a geolocation model that maps image coordinates to geographic coordinates, such as latitude, longitude, and height:

$$
  (t_1, t_2) \to (\varphi, \lambda, h)
$$

where $\varphi$ denotes latitude, $\lambda$ longitude, and $h$ elevation. The precise coordinate representation depends on the image product and geolocation model.

Absolute geometric accuracy characterizes the discrepancy between the geolocated position of an image feature or calibrated point target and an independently determined reference position. For a point target with reference ground coordinates $\mathbf{x}_\text{ref}$ and estimated image-derived coordinates $\unitvec{x}$, a positional error can be expressed as

$$
  \Delta\mathbf{x} = \unitvec{x} - \mathbf{x}_\text{ref}
$$

with an appropriate metric used to report the magnitude of the error, for example

$$
  e = \norm{\Delta x}
$$

Relative geometric accuracy, in contrast, concerns the preservation of spatial relationships within the image rather than the absolute position of the image as a whole. For two points $A$ and $B$, for example, one can compare the image-derived displacement

$$
  \Delta \mathbf{x}_{AB} = \unitvec{x}_B - \unitvec{x}_A
$$

with the corresponding reference displacement

$$
  \Delta \mathbf{x}_{AB,\text{ref}} = \mathbf{x}_{B,\text{ref}} - \mathbf{x}_{A,\text{ref}}
$$

The relative displacement error is then

$$
  e_{AB} = \Delta \mathbf{x}_{AB} - \Delta \mathbf{x}_{AB,\text{ref}}
$$

This distinction is important because a constant translational bias

$$
  \unitvec{x} = \mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}
$$

changes the absolute positions by the same offset $\mathbf{b}$, but leaves pairwise separations unchanged

$$
  \unitvec{x}_B - \unitvec{x}_A = \mathbf{x}_B - \mathbf{x}_A
$$

Thus, a product can exhibit a systematic absolute geolocation offset while retaining good relative geometric fidelity.

Conversely, distortions that vary spatially can affect relative geometry. Examples include scale errors, axis nonorthogonality (skew), rotation errors, and higher-order or spatially varying distortions. A general local affine model, for example, can be written as

$$ 
  \unitvec{x} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}
$$

where $\mathbf b$ represents a translational bias and the matrix $\mathbf A$ describes changes in scale, rotation, and axis geometry. The translational component affects absolute positioning, whereas departures of $\mathbf A$ from the identity matrix can alter relative distances and angles.

## Signal Peak Magnitude

The signal peak magnitude is the maximum magnitude of the complex point-target impulse response. The peak magnitude is defined as

$$
  A_\text{peak} = \max_{(t_1,t_2)} |p(t_1,t_2)|
$$

The quantity $A_\text{peak}$ is an amplitude-domain measure of the strength of the point-target response. In sampled imagery, the maximum sample generally provides only a discrete estime of the continuous peak

$$
  A_\text{peak} = \max_{(m, n)} |p[m, n]|
$$

Subpixel interpolation or local model fitting is used when the peak magnitude is intended as a calibrated measurement.

Peak response measurements are commonly used in the characterization and calibration of SAR point targets. For a calibrated target with known radar cross section (RCS), $\sigma$, the measured response can be related to the target's RCS through an appropriate radiometric calibration model.

A peak-response measurement is not necessarily the only, or always the preferred, radiometric observable. In some calibration procedures, an integrated target response is used instead, because integrating over the point-target response can reduce sensitivity to sampling of the peak and to small differences in the impulse-response shape.

The distinction between these quantities is important. For example, the integrated power over a region $\mathcal{W}$ is

$$
  P_\mathcal{W} = \iint_\mathcal{W} |p(t_1, t_2)|^2 \;\d t_1 \;\d t_2
$$

whereas an integrated amplitude is

$$
  A_\mathcal{W} = \iint_\mathcal{W} |p(t_1, t_2)| \;\d t_1 \;\d t_2
$$

These quantities have different dimensions and different physical meanings. In general

$$
  \max |p| \neq A_\mathcal{W} \neq P_\mathcal{W}
$$

## Phase

The phase response of a SAR point target characterizes the phase of its complex-valued impulse response $\eqref{equation-13}$ and is an important quality metric for applications that rely on coherent information, including interferometry and polarimetry. Phase information is also exploited internally by several processing algorithms, including Doppler-centroid estimation, motion compensation, and autofocus.

The phase behavior of a compressed point target can differ between the two image-coordinate directions. Accordingly, the phase should generally be examined using separate one-dimensional cuts through the point-target response, for example,

$$
  \phi_x (t_1) = \arg p(t_1, t_{2,0})
$$

and

$$
  \phi_y (t_2) = \arg p(t_{1,0}, t_2)
$$

where $(t_{1,0}, t_{2,0})$ denotes the estimated peak position.

For an ideal focused response, the expected phase behavior depends on the signal representation and processing convention. In a matched-filter formulation, the compressed response can contain a residual phase variation across the impulse response. Over a limited interval, this variation may be approximately linear,

$$
  \phi_i (t_i) \approx \phi_{i,0} + k_i (t_i - t_{i,0})
$$

where $k_i$ is the phase slope in direction $i$. The corresponding complex response contains a phase factor of the form $e^{i k_i (t_i - t_{i, 0})}$. The numerical value and even the presence of such a phase ramp depend on the choice of carrier or center frequency, basebanding convention, demodulation reference, coordinate definition, and matched-filter implementation. Consequently, a nonzero phase slope is not by itself evidence of a processing error; it must be evaluated against the phase behavior expected for the particular processing chain.

### Phase regularity

The regularity of the phase profile across the compressed point-target response provides a useful diagnostic of focusing and residual phase errors. After removal of any known or expected phase trend, a well-focused response should exhibit a phase behavior consistent with the adopted signal and processing model.

For example, if the expected phase is represented by $\phi_\text{ref}(t_i)$, a residual phase error can be defined, after appropriate phase unwrapping, as

$$
  \Delta\phi_i(t_i) = \operatorname{unwrap}\left[\phi_i(t_i)\right] - \phi_\text{ref}(t_i)
$$

The magnitude and structure of $\Delta\phi_i(t_i)$ can then be used to identify departures from the expected response. Significant residual phase errors may be associated with incorrect or incomplete focusing, uncompensated platform motion, timing or synchronization errors, or other imperfections in the acquisition and processing chain. The physical cause cannot, however, generally be inferred from the phase profile alone.

Because phase is inherently wrapped, phase-profile analysis should distinguish between the wrapped phase $\phi(t)\in(-\pi,\pi]$ and an unwrapped phase representation used to estimate trends or slopes. Apparent discontinuities of approximately $2\pi$ in a wrapped phase profile do not necessarily represent physical phase discontinuities.

### Phase at peak position

A single-valued peak-phase measurement can be defined from the estimated peak position $(t_{1,\text{peak}},t_{2,\text{peak}})$ as

$$
  \phi_\text{peak} = \arg p(t_{1,\text{peak}},t_{2,\text{peak}}) \pmod{2\pi}. }
$$

For sampled data, this should be evaluated at the estimated continuous peak if an interpolated complex response is available; otherwise, the phase of the maximum-magnitude sample provides the discrete estimate.

The peak phase is a wrapped phase quantity, so

$$
  \phi_\text{peak} \equiv \phi_\text{peak} + 2\pi n,\, n\in\mathbb{Z}
$$

Its absolute numerical value is therefore meaningful only relative to a specified phase reference. In addition, the phase of a point target can depend on its position through the propagation phase and on the reference adopted by the SAR processing chain. Consequently, the measured peak phase should not generally be interpreted as an intrinsic property of the target independent of geometry.

For interferometric or other coherent applications, what is often more physically meaningful is a phase difference between measurements made with a common or explicitly defined reference. For two complex responses $p_1$ and $p_2$, for example, the interferometric phase can be expressed as

$$
  \Delta \phi = \arg (p_1 p_2^*) \pmod{2\pi}
$$

## Sampling requirements

Accurate point-target analysis requires adequate sampling of the complex SAR data in both image-coordinate directions to represent the relevant spatial-frequency content without significant aliasing. For complex SAR data, the sampling intervals are normally selected during image formation to accommodate the processed signal bandwidth and the desired image sampling density. Additional care is required, however, when point-target analysis is performed on detected rather than complex data.

Detection is a nonlinear operation. For complex data $s(t)$, magnitude detection produces $a(t) = |s(t)|$, while power detection produces

$$
  I(t) = |s(t)|^2 = s(t) s^*(t)
$$

Neither operation preserves the spectrum of $s(t)$. In particular, the nonlinear transformation generates new spectral components through frequency mixing. Consequently, a sampling rate that is adequate for the original complex signal is not necessarily sufficient to represent the detected signal without aliasing.

For power detection, this can be seen explicitly from the Fourier-domain relation

$$
  F{|s(t)|^2} = S(f) * S^* (−f)
$$

where $S(f) = F{s(t)}$. Thus, if the complex signal occupies a bandwidth \(B\), the support of the power-detected signal can, in the worst case, extend to approximately twice that bandwidth. Magnitude detection is also nonlinear and can generate additional spectral content, although its exact spectral support depends on the signal and its amplitude distribution.

Sampling below the Nyquist rate of the relevant detected signal can therefore introduce aliasing. Once aliased, these spectral components cannot in general be removed by subsequent processing and may distort quantities derived from the point-target response.

Generally, if the relevant detected signal has effective two-sided bandwidth $B_\text{det}}$, a sampling frequency $f_s$ should satisfy the appropriate Nyquist condition

$$
  f_s > 2B_\text{det}
$$

with additional margin where required by the desired measurement accuracy and by nonideal filtering. Equivalently, an oversampling factor can be defined as

$$
  \eta = \frac{f_s}{2B_\text{det}}
$$

so that $\eta > 1$ represents sampling above the nominal Nyquist rate.

For two-dimensional SAR data, this consideration applies independently in the two image-coordinate directions. If the detected signal has effective bandwidths \(B_{\mathrm{det},x}\) and \(B_{\mathrm{det},y}\), then the corresponding sampling frequencies should satisfy

$$
\begin{align*}
  f_{s,x} >& 2 B_{\text{det},x} \\
  f_{s,y} >& 2 B_{\text{det},y}
\end{align*}
$$

subject to the actual two-dimensional spectral support and any coupling between the coordinates.

# Pulse compression of linear frequency-modulated signals

Pulse compression is a type of spread spectrum method, designed to minimize peak power, maximize signal-to-noise ratio, and obtain fine resolution of the sensed object (e.g., to achieve sensitive target detection or good image quality).

## Linear frequency-modulated signals

### Time-domain representation

In the time domain, an ideal frequency-modulated signal or pulse has a duration $T$ seconds with a constant amplitude, a center frequency $f_0$, and a characteristic phase component $\phi(t)$, which varies with time in a specific manner. For linear frequeny modulation, the phase is a quadratic function of time. When $f_0$ is set to zero, the complex form of the signal is

$$
\begin{equation*}
  s(t) = \operatorname{rect}\left(\frac{t}{T} \right) e^{i\pi K t^2}
\tag{\label{equation-14}}
\end{equation*}
$$

where $t$ is the time variable in seconds, and $K$ is the linear frequency-modulation rate in $\tunit{Hs/s}$. The phase of the pulse is given by the argument of the exponential, $\phi(t) = \pi K t^2$. The instantaneous frequency is the derivative with respect to time

$$
  f(t) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \frac{\d\phi(t)}{\d t} = Kt
$$

This means that the frequency is a linear function of time $t$, with the slope $K$. When the slope is positive, the signal is called an up chirp. Similarly, when the slope is negative, the signal is called a down chirp.

The bandwidth is defined as the range of frequencies spanned by the significant energy of the chirp, or the frequency excursion of the signal

$$
  B = |K|T
$$

Another important signal parameter is the time bandwidth product (TBP) defined by

$$
  B_\text{TBP} = BT = |K|T^2
$$

The TBP of the baseband linear frequency-modulated signal in $\eqref{equation-14}$ can be measured by counting the number of zero crossings of the real or imaginary part of the time-domain signla. When $f_0 = 0$, the number of zero crossings in the signal is approximately equal to one-half of the TBP, or the number of complete cyclese is approximately equal to $B_\text{TBP}^2$.

# Continuous-wave SAR

FMCW SAR uses a dechirp reception method, and the bandwidth of the mixed signal depends on the modulation frequency of the transmit signal and the mapping bandwidth, which is usually much smaller than the transmit signal bandwidth, which greatly reduces the acquisition pressure of the receiver and has a limited impact on the range-oriented resolution.

# The Doppler effect

Consider a transmitter moving with velocity $v$ approaching and passing a stationary receiver at a distance $y_0$. The distance between the transmitter and receiver at time $t$ is

$$
\begin{equation*}
  x(t) = \sqrt{y_0^2 + (vt)^2}
\tag{\label{equation-8}}
\end{equation*}
$$

The received sinusoid arrives with a phase delay $\phi(t)$ given by $2\pi$ times at the distance expressed as a fraction a wavelength

$$
  \phi(t) = 2\pi \frac{x(t)}{\lambda}
$$

The Doppler frequency component is the time derivative of the phase delay

$$
\begin{align*}
  \omega_d = \frac{\d\phi(t)}{\d t} = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda} \frac{\d x(t)}{\d t}
\end{align*}
$$

so that from $\eqref{equation-8}$

$$
  f_d = \frac{1}{\lambda} \frac{\d x(t)}{\d t} = \frac{v^2}{\lambda} [y_0^2 + (vt)^2]^{-1/2} t
$$

Suppose $vt \ll y_0$, i.e. the transmitter is close to passing the receiver. In this case

$$
  x(t) = y_0 + \frac{v^2}{2y_0} t^2
$$

so that the Doppler frequency shift is

$$
  f_d = \frac{1}{\lambda} \frac{\d x(t)}{\d t} = \frac{v^2}{\lambda y_0} t
$$
