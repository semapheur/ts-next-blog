# Sampling

Digital signal processing (DPS) relies on an accurate and sufficiently efficient representation of continuous-time signals in discrete time. This requires the sampling rate to be high enough to preserve signal fidelity, but not so high as to be inefficient. If the sampling rate is too low relative to the signal bandwidth, spectral overlap occurs after sampling, producing aliasing and making the original signal, in general, impossible to reconstruct uniquely from its samples.

The appropriate sampling requirement depends on the nature of the signal and its spectral support. In particular, the relevant conditions differ for real-valued and complex-valued signals and for baseband and bandpass signals.

## Spectrum of sampled signals

An essential part of DSP is to examine the signal frequency spectrum before and after a processing operation. This makes it possible to verify that the expected spectral components are present and to determine how an operation has modified them. Some signal-processing operations can also be formulated directly in the frequency domain.

The spectrum of a discrete-time signal is different from that of a continuous-time signal. There are four cases to consider, depending on whether the signal is continuous-time or discrete-time, and whether its duration is finite or infinite.

Consider first an infinite-duration, continuous-time signal $x(t)$, with Fourier transform $X(f)$. Assume that the signal is bandlimited to $f_b$, meaning that $X(f) = 0$ for $|f| > f_b$. If the signal is sampled uniformly with sampling period $T_s$, the sampling frequency is

$$
  f_s = \frac{1}{T_s}
$$

and the resulting discrete-time sequence is $x[n] = x(nT_s)$. Ideal sampling produces periodic replicas of the original continuous-time spectrum. The spectrum of the sampled signal can be written as

$$
  X_s (f) = \frac{1}{T_s} \sum_{m=-\infty}^\infty X(f - mf_s)
$$

Thus, the spectrum is repeated at integer multiples of the sampling frequency $f_s$.

For a real-valued baseband signal whose spectrum is confined to $|f| \leq f_b$, the spectral replicas do not overlap provided that $f_s > 2f_b$. This is the Nyquist sampling condition for a real-valued, low-pass, bandlimited signal. If the replicas overlap, spectral components become indistinguishable in the sampled signal, producing aliasing.

The condition $f_s > 2f_b$, however, should not be regarded as a universal sampling rule. The minimum sampling rate depends on the spectral support of the particular signal and on whether the signal is real or complex and baseband or bandpass.

### The spectrum of an $N$-point DFT

Suppose $N$ samples of a signal are taken at sampling frequency $f_s$, and an $N$-point discrete Fourier transform (DFT) is computed:

$$
  X[k] = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n] e^{-i2\pi kn/N},\, k=0,\dots,N-1
$$

The DFT contains $N$ equally spaced frequency samples. The frequency spacing, or frequency resolution, is

$$
  \Delta f = \frac{f_s}{N}
$$

Using the conventional ordering of the DFT output, bin $k$ corresponds to

$$
  f_k = k \frac{f_s}{N},\, k=0,\dots,N-1
$$

Consequently, the frequency associated with the last DFT bin is

$$
  f_{N-1} = \frac{(N - 1) f_s}{N} = f_s - \frac{f_s}{N} \leq f_s
$$

There is an important subtlety here. A discrete-time spectrum is periodic with period $f_s$. Hence, frequencies that differ by an integer multiple of $f_s$ are equivalent. The frequency

$$
  f_s - \frac{f_s}{N}
$$

can therefore be interpreted as the negative frequency $-f_s /N$. This is why the upper portion of the conventional DFT output is normally interpreted as representing negative frequencies.

### Centered or shifted DFT spectrum

For analysis and visualization, it is often more convenient to reorder the DFT coefficients so that zero frequency appears at the center. This operation is commonly called an FFT shift.

For even $N$, the resulting frequency axis can be written as

$$
  f_k = \left(k - \frac{N}{2}\right) \frac{f_s}{N},\, k=0,\dots,N-1
$$

Thus, the shifter spectrum is naturally interpreted over one Nyquist interval

$$
  \left[-\frac{f_s}{2}, \frac{f_s}{2}\right)
$$

The endpoints require some care: $+f_s /2$ and $-f_s /2$ represent the same discrete-timee frequency because they differ by $f_s$. Consequently, both enpoints are not included simultaneously.

### Real-valued signals

For a real-valued sequence $x[n]$, the DFT satisfies conjugate symmetry

$$
  X[N - k] = X^* [k],\, k=1,\dots,N - 1
$$

Thus, the negative-frequency portion of the DFT contains no additional independent information; it is completely determined by the positive-frequency portion.

For even $N$, the independent frequency bins can consequently be taken as $k=0,\dots,N/2$ correspondning to frequencies

$$
  0 \leq f_k \leq \frac{f_s}{2}
$$

The remaining DFT coefficients are determined by conjugate symmetry.

This redundancy is a consequence of the signal being real-valued. It does not generally hold for complex-valued signals, for which the positive- and negative-frequency components can contain independent information.

## Quadrature modulation of real-valued signals

In physical measurement, signals are real-valued quantities. Within a digital signal processor, however, it is often advantageous to represent such signals using complex-valued sequences. Complex representations can simplify modulation and demodulation, frequency translation, filtering, and spectral analysis. The DFT is a familiar example: although it can be applied to real-valued input sequences, its output is generally complex-valued.

A real-valued signal can be represented by a complex-valued signal using quadrature demodulation (or complex demodulation). The essential idea is to translate a selected frequency band to baseband and represent its two orthogonal components as the real and imaginary parts of a complex signal.

Suppose the real-valued input is $x(t)$, and let the desired center frequency be $f_c$, with angular frequency $\omega_c = 2\pi f_c$. Then signal can be mixed with two orthogonal local oscillators $\cos(\omega_c t)$ and $\sin(\omega_c t)$. After low-pass filtering to remove the unwanted high-frequency components, the resulting signals form the in-phase and quadrature components. With an appropriate sign convention, they can be written as

$$
\begin{align*}
  I(t) =& \operatorname{LPF}[2x(t) \cos(\omega_c t)] \\
  Q(t) =& \operatorname{LPF}[-2x(t) \sin(\omega_c t)]
\end{align*}
$$

The corresponding complex baseband signal is

$$
  z(t) = I(t) + iQ(t)
$$

The factor of $2$ is a normalization convenction that compensates for the factor $1/2$ introduced by multiplication by a sinuisoid. Depending on the convention used for the quadrature oscillator and the definition of $Q(t)$, the sign of the imaginary component may instead be reversed. What matters is that the two components are in quadrature, meaning that they differ in phase by $\pi/2$ radians.

The reason this produces a complex baseband representation can be seen from Euler's identity

$$
  e^{-i\omega_c t} = \cos(\omega_c t) + i\sin(\omega_c t)
$$

Thus, an equivalent conceptual description of complex demodulation is to multiply the real signal by a complex local oscillator

$$
  z_\text{mix} (t) = 2x (t) e^{-i\omega_c t}
$$

and then low-pass filter the result

$$
  z(t) = \operatorname{LPF}[2x(t) e^{-i\omega_c t}]
$$

The complex signal $z(t)$ therefore contains two real-valued channels: the in-phase component $I(t)$ and the quadrature component $Q(t)$. The same operation can be performed in discrete time. The real signal may first be sampled, $x[n] = x(nT_s)$, after which digital oscillators and digital filters can be used to perform the frequency translation

$$
  z[n] = \operatorname{LPF}(2x[n] e^{-i\omega_c t)
$$

where $\omega_c$ is not the corresponding discrete-time angular frequency, measured in radians per sample. Equivalently, the real and imaginary components can be generated separately using cosine and sine sequences.

### Hilbert transform

An alternative method for constructing a complex representation is based on the Hilbert transform. If $x(t)$ is a real-valued signal and $\hat{x}(t)$ denote its Hilbert transform, then the associated analytic signal is

$$
  x_a (t) = x(t) + i\hat{x}(t)
$$

If $X(f)$ denotes the Fourier transfomr of $x(t)$, the Hilbert transform is characterized in the frequency domain by

$$
  \hat{X}(f) = -i \operatorname{sgn}(f) X(f)
$$

where

$$
  \operatorname{sgn}(f) = \begin{cases}
    1,\quad& f > 0 \\
    0,\quad& f = 0 \\
    -1,\quad& f < 0
  \end{cases}
$$

Consequently, positive-frequency components undergo a phase shift of $-\pi/2$, while negative-frequency components undergo a phase shift of $+\pi/2$. The Hilbert transform therefore generates a component in quadrature with the original signal, although the phase shift is frequency-dependent in sign rather than being a uniform $90^\circ$ shift over both positive and negative frequencies.

The Fourier transform of the analytic signal is

$$
  X_a (f) = X(f) + i\hat{X}(f)
$$

which, using the Hilbert-transform relation, becomes

$$
  X_a (f) = [1 + \operatorname{sgn}(f)] X(f)
$$

Thus,

$$
  X_a (f) = \begin{cases}
    2X(f),\quad& f > 0,
    X(0),\quad& f = 0,
    0,\quad& f < 0
  \end{cases}
$$

Thus, the analytic signal contains no negative-frequency components, $X_a(f) = 0$ for $f < 0$. The positive-frequency components are doubled, while the direct current (DC) component is retained with its original amplitude rather than doubled. This convention ensures that the real part of the analytic signal is exactly the original signal:

$$
  \Re[x_a (t)] = x(t)
$$

For a discrete-time signal, the same principle applies, but the periodicity of the discrete-time spectrum requires additional care. For an even-length $N$-point DFT, the positive-frequency components are retained and doubled except at the DC bin and the Nyquist bin. The DC component corresponds to $f = 0$, while the Nyquist component corresponds to

$$
  f = \frac{f_s}{2}
$$

which is equivalent to $-f_s /2$ because discrete-time frequencies are periodic with period $f_s$. Consequently, when constructing an analytic discrete-time signal using av even-length DFT, the spectral coefficients are modified according to

$$
  X_a [k] = \begin{cases}
    X[k],\quad& k = 0 \\
    2X[k],\quad& 1 \leq k < N/2 \\
    X[k],\quad& k = N/2 \\
    0,\quad& N/2 < k \leq N - 1
  \end{cases}
$$

The Nyquist component is therefore retained rather than doubled, just as the DC component is retained. For off $N$, there is no bin exactly at the Nyquist frequency, so the corresponding weighting differs slightly.

This suppression of negative-frequency content makes analytic signals particularly useful for complex-envelope representations, modulation and demodulation, instantaneous-amplitude and instantaneous-phase analysis, and instantaneous-frequency estimation.

## Signal bandwidth

The sampling requirements of a signal is dependent on its bandwidth. Let $X(f)$ denote the Fourier transform of a continuous-time signal $x(t)$.

For a real-valued signal, the spectrum satisfies the conjugate-symmetry property $X(-f) = X^* (f)$, so the negative frequency spectrum contains no additional independent information. Thus, bandwidth is conventionall specified using the positive-frequency spectrum. If $f_1$ and $f_2$ are the lowest and highest frequencies, respectively, containing significant spectral energy, with $0 \leq f_1 \leq f_2$, then the occupied bandwidth is $B = f_2 - f_1$.

For a complex-valued signal, positive and negative frequencies are not generally relative by conjugate symmetry, so both must be considered. If the significant spectral content extends from $f_\text{min}$ to $f_\text{max}$, where $f_\text{min}$ may be negative, the occupied bandwidth is $B = f_\text{max} - f_\text{min}$. Thus, for a complex signal, bandwidth is the width of the smallest frequency interval containing the relevant spectral content. It is not necessarily the difference between the largest and smallest positive frequency components.

### Effective bandwidth

Strictly speaking, a nonzero signal cannot be both time-limited and bandlimited. In particular, a signal of finite duration cannot have a Fourier transform with exactly finite support. In practical applications, this limitation is addressed by defining an effective bandwidth or occupied bandwidth: spectral components whose energy or power is sufficiently small relative to a specified reference level are neglected.

The choice of threshold is application-dependent. For example, a $-3 \unit{dB}$ criterion is sometimes used to define an effective bandwidth, while more stringent criteria, such as $-10 \unit{dB}$, may be appropriate in other applications.

Practical analog signals may also contain unwanted out-of-band components, including broadband noise. An antialiasing filter is therefore commonly placed before the sampler to attenuate spectral components outside the frequency range of interest. This permits the sampling frequency to be selected according to the desired signal bandwidth while sufficiently suppressing components that could otherwise alias into the retained frequency range.

## Fundamental frequency range

For a sampling frequency $f_s$, the spectrum of the resulting discrete-time signal is periodic with period $f_s$. Consequently, continuous-time frequencies that differ by an integer multiple of $f_s$ are indistinguishable after sampling:

$$
  f \sim f + kf_s,\, k\in\Z
$$

A fundamental frequency interval is an interval of width $f_s$ containing one representative from each equivalence class of frequencies modulo $f_s$.

For real-valued signals, a commonly used one-sided representation is

$$
  0 \leq f \leq \frac{f_s}{2}
$$

Because of conjugate symmetry, this interval contains all independent spectral information.

For complex-valued signals, a commonly used centered fundamental interval is

$$
  -\frac{f_s}{2} \leq f < \frac{f_s}{2}
$$

An equivalent choice is $0 \leq f < f_s$

These intervals contain the same discrete-time frequency information; they differ only in the choice of frequency origin.

The frequency $f_s/2$ is the Nyquist frequency and is the upper limit of the conventional one-sided fundamental interval for real-valued sampling.

## Baseband versus nonbaseband signals

### Real-valued signals

For a real-valued signal, a baseband signal is one whose significant spectral content extends to, or sufficiently close to, zero frequency. If $f_1$ denotes the lowest positive frequency containing significant spectral energy, then a signal may be regarded as baseband when $f_1 \ll B$.

An ideal lowpass signal is the limiting case in which the significant spectrum begins at zero frequency.

For example, white noise passed through a lowpass filter can have significant spectral energy extending continuously down to zero frequency. In this case, the sampling frequency must be chosen so that the relevant positive-frequency content lies within the fundamental interval

$$
  0 \leq f \leq \frac{f_s}{2}
$$

with sufficient margin to accommodate the transition band of the practical antialiasing filter.

If there is a substantial spectral gap between zero frequency and the lowest frequency containing significant spectral energy, the signal is commonly described as nonbaseband or bandpass. For example, a real signal whose significant spectrum occupies approximately

$$ 
  f_1 \leq |f| \leq f_2,\, 0 < f_1 < f_2
$$

is a bandpass signal. Such signals are common in communication systems, where an information-bearing signal is modulated onto a high-frequency carrier.

### Complex-valued signals

For a complex-valued signal, the baseband/nonbaseband distinction is most naturally defined relative to the chosen fundamental frequency interval.

A complex signal is baseband, for a given sampling frequency $f_s$, if its significant spectral support can be represented entirely within the centered fundamental interval

$$
  -\frac{f_s}{2} \leq f < \frac{f_s}{2}
$$

Equivalently, if the significant spectral support occupies a frequency interval of width no greater than \(f_s\), it can, in principle, be placed within a single fundamental interval without spectral overlap, subject to practical filtering requirements.

Conversely, a complex signal is nonbaseband with respect to a particular sampling frequency if its significant spectral content is not contained within the selected fundamental interval.

A nonbaseband signal can nevertheless be sampled without loss using bandpass sampling, provided the sampling frequency is chosen so that the resulting spectral replicas do not overlap. Thus, whether a signal is described as baseband or nonbaseband depends not only on the signal's spectrum but also on its position relative to the sampling frequency.

## Nyquist sampling rate and aliasing

The sampling requirements differ between real and complex signals, primarily because each complex sample has twice the information content as one real sample.

<MathBox title="Nyquist-Shannon sampling theorem" boxType="theorem">
Let $x: \R\to\mathbb{F}^n$ be a continuous-time signal whose Fourier transform $X(f)$ satisfies

$$
  \operatorname{supp}(X) \subseteq [-B, B]
$$

where $B > 0$ is the maximum absolute frequency. Equivalently, $X(f) = 0$ for $|f| > B$. Then $x(t)$ is bandlimited to $B \text{Hz}$, such that $x(t)$ is uniquely determined by its uniformly spaced samples $x[k] = x(kT_s)$ for $k\in\Z$, provided taht the sampling frequency $f_s = 1/T_s$ satisfies 

$$
\begin{equation*}
  f_s > 2B
\tag{\label{equation-3}}
\end{equation*}
$$
</MathBox>

In the scalar case, a signal $x: \R\to\mathbb{F}$ is reconstructed from the Shannon interpolation formula

$$
  x(t) = \sum_{k=-\infty}^\infty x(kT_s) \operatorname{sinc}\left(\frac{t - kT_s}{T_s} \right)
$$

The quantity $f_\text{NR} = 2B$ is called the Nyquist rate associated with the bandwidth $B$. It is the critical sampling rate for a baseband signal bandlimited to $B \unit{Hz}$. This is distinguished from the Nyquist frequency, which is defined as half the sampling frequency

$$
  f_N = \frac{f_s}{2}
$$

Consequently, the sampling condition $\eqref{equation-3}$ can equivalently be written as $f_N > B$.

### Real signals

For a real baseband signal, the bandwidth $B$ is the highest frequency present in the signal. Thus, if the signal contains no frequency components above $f_\text{max}$ exact reconstruction requires $f_s > 2f_\text{max}$.

If the sampling does not satisfy the required condition, the spectral replicas generated by sampling can overt. This phenomenon is called aliasing.

Sampling a continuous time-signal frequency $f_s$, makes frequencies separated by integer multiples of $f_s$ indistinguishable. In particular $f$ and $f + kf_s$ for $k \in \Z$ produce the same discrete-time sequence. In other words, continuous-time frequencies are idenfied modulo $f_s$, definin the equivalence relation

$$
  f_1 \sim f_2 \iff f_1 - f_2 \in f_s \Z
$$

Each equivalence class has a unique representative in the Nyquist intercal

$$
  \left[-\frac{f_s}{2}, \frac{f_s}{2} \right)
$$

Thus, the alias frequency $f_s$ corresponding to a continuous-time frequency $f$ is the unique value satisfying

$$
  -\frac{f_s}{2} \leq f_a < \frac{f_s}{2}
$$

and $f_a = f - kf-s$ for $k\in\Z$.

#### Bandpass

For a real bandpass signal, the highest frequency alone does not necessarily determine the minimum sampling rate. Suppose the positive frequency spectrum of the signal is confined to $f_L \leq f \leq f_H$ where $f_L$ and $f_H$ are the lower and upper band edges. The bandwidth is $B = f_H - f_L$.

Unlike a baseband signal, a bandpass signal may sometimes be sampled at a rate determined primarily by its bandwidth, rather than by its highest frequency $f_H$. This technique is known as bandpass sampling, undersampling, or subsampling.
However, the condition $f_s > 2B$ is not, by itself, sufficient for arbitrary bandpass signals.

When a signal is sampled, its spectrum is replicated periodically at integer multiples of the sampling frequency

$$
  X_s (f) = \frac{1}{T_s} \sum_{k=-\infty}^\infty X(f - kf_s)
$$

where $T_s = 1/f_s$. 

For bandpass sampling to preserve all of the signal information, these spectral replicas must remain non-overlapping. The sampling frequency must therefore be chosen so that the original band and its translated replicas do not intersect.
For example, one commonly expressed bandpass-sampling condition is that there exists an integer $m\in\mathbb{Z}$ such that

$$
  \frac{2f_H}{m} \leq f_s \leq \frac{2f_L}{m - 1}
$$

with the appropriate integer $m$ chosen so that the resulting spectral replicas remain separated. The exact admissible sampling intervals depend on the location and bandwidth of the signal spectrum. Thus, for a bandpass signal, the sampling problem has two distinct requirements:
1. The sampling rate must be sufficiently high relative to the bandwidth.
2. The sampling rate must be chosen so that the resulting spectral replicas do not overlap.

If these conditions are satisfied, the sampled signal can retain all of the information in the original continuous-time signal, even though its spectrum may appear at a different frequency after sampling. This frequency translation is a predictable consequence of the sampling process and is not necessarily destructive aliasing.

#### Downconversion from bandpass to baseband

In practical systems, a bandpass signal is often frequency-translated to baseband before sampling. This is commonly accomplished by mixing the signal with a local oscillator, followed by appropriate filtering. After downconversion, suppose the resulting baseband signal occupies $|f| \leq B$. The conventional Nyquist criterion can then be applied directly $f_s > 2B$. This approach avoid the additional constraints associated with direct bandpass sampling and makes the required sample rate depend explicitly on the signal bandwidth.

### Complex signals

For a complex-valued analytical signal whose spectrum is confined to the one-sided hand $0 \leq f \leq B$, the minimum sampling rate is $f_s > B$. Thus the critical sampling rate is equal to its bandwidth $B$.

This differs from the real baseband case because a real signal has a conjugate-symmetric spectrum: if $X(f)\neq 0$ at a positive frequency $f$, then $X(-f)\neq 0$ as well. Consequently, a real signal occupying $-B\leq f\leq B$ has a two-sided spectral width of $2B$, leading to the Nyquist rate $f_s > 2B$.

An analytic complex signal, by contrast, contains only the positive-frequency portion of the spectrum. Its spectral support has width $B$, so complex sampling can represent the signal at a rate exceeding $B$ complex samples per second.
The intuition that one complex sample carries twice the information of one real sample is useful, but it is not the fundamental reason for the different sampling rate. More precisely, a complex sample

$$
x[n]=x_I[n]+j\,x_Q[n]
$$

contains two real-valued degrees of freedom, $x_I[n]$ and $x_Q[n]$. Complex sampling therefore provides two real sample streams at the same sample rate. This makes it possible to represent a one-sided complex spectrum of width $B$ using a complex sampling rate approaching $B$, whereas representing the corresponding real signal requires a real sampling rate approaching $2B$.

### Window

Achieving the finest possible resolution while suppressing sidelobes is a fundamental trade-off in spectral analysis. In general, stronger sidelobe suppression is accompanied by a wider main lobe in the Fourier transform of the window, thereby reducing the ability to distinguish closely spaced spectral components. 

#### Kaiser window

The Kaiser window is widely used because its adjustable parameter $\beta$ provides a convenient means of controlling this trade-off.

For window of duration $T > 0$, centered at $t = 0$, the Kaiser window is defined in the time domain by

$$
  w_K (t; T, \beta) = \frac{I_0 (\beta \sqrt{1 - (2t/T)^2}}{I_0 (\beta)},\, t\leq \left|\frac{T}{2} \right|
$$

where $\beta$ is the adjustable roll-off or smoothing coefficient and $I_0 (\cdot)$ is the zeroth-order Bessel function defined by

$$
  I_0 (z) = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_0^\pi e^{z\cos(\theta)} \;\d\theta
$$

The parameter $\beta$ controls the degree of tapering toward the window boundaries. When $\beta = 0$, the Kaiser window reduces to the rectangular window 

$$
  w_K (t; T, 0) = \operatorname{rect}\left(\frac{T}{2} \right)
$$

In the frequency domain, a Kaiser window with total frequency span $F > 0$ is defined by

$$
  W_k (f, F) = \frac{I_0 (\beta \sqrt{1 - (2f/F)^2}}{I_0 (\beta)},\, t\leq \left|\frac{F}{2} \right|
$$

The Fourier transform of a Kaiser window is a continuous, even function expressible in terms of hyperbolic or trigonometric functions and a square-root term, depending on the frequency range.

For $\beta > 0$, the tapering reduces spectral leakage by suppressing sidelobes, at the cost of widening the main lobe. The amount of broadening can be quantified using the ratio

$$
  R_{3\tunit{dB} = \frac{\Delta f_{3\tunit{dB}, K}}{\Delta f_{3\tunit{dB}, R}}
$$

where $\Delta f_{3\tunit{dB}, K}$ is the full 3-dB width of the Kaiser window transform and $\Delta f_{3\tunit{dB}, R}$ is the corresponding widht for a rectangular window of the same duration. The 3-dB width is the frequency interval between the points at which the magnitude response falls to $1/\sqrt{2}$ of its peak value.

#### General cosine windows

Two other common windows are the Hanning and Hamming windows. Both are special cases of the general cosine windows. For a window of duration $T$, centered at $t = 0$, a general cosine window is defined by

$$
  w_G (t; T, \alpha) = \alpha - (1 - \alpha) \cos\left(\frac{2\pi t}{T}\right), t \leq \left|\frac{T}{2}\right|
$$

The $\alpha$ parameter determines the relative weights of the constant and cosine terms. It can be used to control the level of smoothing.


For the Hann window (historically called the Hanning window), $\alpha = 1/2$, which gives a cosine squared window. 

$$
\begin{align*}
  w_\text{Hann} (t; T) = \frac{1}{2} \left[1 + \cos\left(\frac{2\pi t}{T} \right) \right] \\
  =& \cos^2 \left(\frac{\pi t}{T} \right),\, |t| \leq \frac{T}{2}
\end{align*}
$$

For a Hamming window, $a = 0.54$, and the window is a raised cosine squared. 

$$
  w_\text{Hamming} (t; T) = 0.54 + 0.46 \cos\left(\frac{2\pi t}{T} \right),\, |t| \leq \fract{T}{2}
$$

Both windows are zero outside their specified intervals. The Hann window tapers to zero at the endpoints, whereas the Hamming window has a small nonzero endpoint value.

A corresponding frequency-domain general cosine window with support width $F$ is

$$
  W_G (f; F, \alpha) = \alpha - (1 - \alpha) \cos\left(\frac{2\pi f}{T}\right), t \leq \left|\frac{F}{2}\right|
$$

The inverse Fourier transform of this compactly supported frequency-domain window can be expressed in closed form. In particular, the cosine term produces shifted sinc-type terms, so the resulting time-domain function is a finite linear combination of sinc functions. However, these windows do not have quite as good a resolution/sidelobe tradeoff as the Kaiser window, so the Kaiser window is preferred for many DSP applications.

# Interpolation

Consider a signal $g(x)$ that is sampled at discrete positions $x = i$, where $x\in\R$ is a continuous independent variable and $i\in\Z$ is an integer (the sample number). The independent variable $x = t f_s$ is used instead of time $t$, since spatial coodinates are often used in practical applications. Alose, let $g_d (i)$ be the sampled signal such that $g_d (i) = g(x)$ when $x = i$. Using interpolation, we can resample $g_d (i)$ to evaluate it at some other noninteger values $x \neq i$.

The value of $g(x)$ for noninteger values of $x$ can be obtained by applying the DFT shifting/modulation property, or by zero padding the spectrum. However, these methods are not flexible enough for the general use, because the former gives pixels that are shifted by the same amount from the original signal, and the latter gives pixels that are separated by $1/M$ subsamples, where $M$ is the expansion factor (which must be an integer).

An interpolation can be implemented via a convolution

$$
  g(x) = \sum_i g_d (i) h(x - i)
$$

where $h(x)$ is called the interpolator or interpolation kernel. In practice, the kernel is an even function of $x$, so that $h(i - x) = h(x - i)$. A sample point at $i$ is then weighted by the kernel weight $h(i - x)$. The interpolated value $g(x)$, at the interpolation point $x$ is the sum of the products of the kernel weights and the samples in $g_d (i)$ under the kernel, or a weighted sum of the samples in the neighbourhood of $x$.

## Sinc interpolation

A sinc interpolator uses a kernel based on a sinc function, which follows from Shannon's sampling theorem.

If a signal $g(x)$ is sampled at discrete and evenly spaced intervals, the sampling theorem states that $g(x)$ can by reconstructed without error, if both of the following conditions are satisfied:
- The signal is bandlimited; that is, its highest frequency is finite. Measurements made of any physical system are indeed bandlimited.
- The sampling satisfies the Nyquist sampling rate. For a real signal, the sampling rate must be more than twice per cycle of the signal's highest frequency component. For a complex signal, the sampling rate must be higher than the signal's bandwidth.

### Reconstruction equation

The sampling theorem states that the original signal, with the above two conditions satisfied, can be reconstructed by a convolution. For a baseband signal, the interpolation kernel is a sine function

$$
\begin{equation*}
  h(x) = \sinc(x) = \frac{\sin(\pi x)}{\pi x}
\tag{\label{equation-1}}
\end{equation*}
$$

Then the interpolation is

$$
  g(x) = \sum_i g_d (i) \sinc(x - i)
$$

with the summation over all input samples.

The result in $\eqref{equation-1}$ can be understood via the frequency domain. The spectrum $G_d (f)$ of the sampled signal $g_d (i)$ is that of $g(x)$ but repeated at the sampling frequency. To form the signal $g(x)$ only one period of the spectrum is required (e.g. the one at baseband). Thus, an ideal lowpass filter, int the form of a rectangular function in the frequency domain, is needed to extract the baseband spectrum. The ideal filter is then a sinc function in the time domain. Since multiplication in the frequency domain is equivalent to convolution in the time domain, the interpolation can be performed by a convolution with the sinc function.

An infinite number of points is required in the kernel to obtain one particular point, $g(x)$, exactly. In practice, an infinite number of points is not available; besides, an interpolation that uses a large number of points would be very costly. Fortunately, the kernel weight decreases with distance away from the interpolation point $x$ suggesting that the kernel can be truncated without much loss in accuracy.

## Kernel normalization

Once the kernel is truncated, normalization is needed to unify the gain of the interpolator. If unnormalized, the sum of weights on the samples is no longer equal to one, and differs between interpolation points $x$. It is best to keep this sum constant, equal to one in this case, for all interpolation points. Assuming a uniform magnitude signal with the magnitude equal to $M_u$, the interpolated value is then equal to the product of $M_u$ and the sum of the weights. By normalizing the sum to one, then the interpolated value is also $M_u$, which is the desired result since the interpolation should keep the uniform magnitude unchanged. A normalization procedure is applied to modify the interpolated result to give

$$
\begin{equation*}
  g'(x) = \frac{g(x)}{S}
\tag{\label{equation-2}}
\end{equation*}
$$

where $S$ is the sum of the kernel weights at a particular interpolation point

$$
  S = \sum_i \sinc(x - i)
$$

where $i$ is now limited to the kernel size. Sometimes, it is desirable to keep the signal power constant. In this case, the normalization constant is

$$
  S_2 = \sqrt{\sum_i \sinc^2 (x - i)}
$$

As the kernel length increases, the normalization constant in either case becomes closer to one, and the distinction between these two forms of normalization reduces.

The procedure in $\eqref{equation-2}$ is equivalent to normalizing the interpolation kernel by dividing each weight $h(x - i)$ by $S$, so that the sum of the normalized weights is one.

### Windows sinc function

When a function with sharp edges is interpolated, using a truncated sine kernel, the results exhibit a "ringing" called the Gibb's phenomenon. To reduce this ringing, the interpolation kernel is weighted by a tapering window, such as the Kaiser window. The kernel normalization also takes this window into account.

For implementation efficiency, the interpolation kernel is generated at fine subsample intervals and stored in a table. In this way, the sine function, window, and normalization do not have to be performed at each interpolation point; only the appropriate coefficients need to be extracted from the table, using the table entries nearest to the interpolation shift. Accuracy is affected by both the kernel length and the number of subsamples in the table. Limiting the table size introduces a geometric error of, at most, one-half the subsample interval.

## Spectra of interpolation kernels

Special test signals can be defined to examine the interpolation accuracy, but another way is to examine the spectra of the interpolation kernels.

There are two methods to perform the interpolation on nonbaseband signals, and the choice is application-dependent:
- Translate the signal to baseband. This means shifting the signal to the left by $\Delta f$, the frequency offset from baseband. By the Fourier transform shift property, the signal in the time domain is multiplied by a linear phase term $e^{-i 2\pi\Delta f t}$. If desired, the data spectrum can be bandshifted back to its original center frequency after interpolation.
- Translate the baseband filter to the signal's center frequency. By the Fourier transform shift property, the interpolation kernel in the time domain filter is multiplied by a linear phase term $e^{i2\pi\Delta f}$, which makes the interpolator coefficiens complex.
