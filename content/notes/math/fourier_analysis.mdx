# Trigonometric sum formulas

<MathBox title='' boxType="lemma" tag="1">
If $x\in\R\setminus\set{2\pi n}_{n\in\Z}$, then

$$
\begin{equation}
	\frac{1}{2} + \sum_{k=1}^n \cos(kx) = \frac{1}{2} \frac{\sin\left[\left(n + \frac{1}{2}\right)x\right]}{\sin\left(\frac{1}{2}x \right)} \tag{\label{equation-2}}
\end{equation}
$$

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>

Writing the left side of $\eqref{equation-2}$ as a geometric sum of trigonometric exponential functions, we get

$$
\begin{align*}
	\frac{1}{2} + \sum_{k=1}^n \cos(kx) =& \frac{1}{2} + \sum_{k=1}^n \frac{1}{2} (e^{ikx} + e^{ikx}) = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{k=-n}^n e^{ikx} \\
	=& \frac{1}{2} e^{-inx} \sum_{k=0}^{2n} e^{ikx}
\end{align*}
$$

Applying the geometric sum formula $\sum_{k=0}^n q^k = \frac{1 - q^{n+1}}{1 - q}$ with $q = e^{ix}$, we obtain

$$
\begin{align*}
	\frac{1}{2}e^{-inx} \sum_{k=0}^{2n} e^{ikx} =& \frac{1}{2}e^{-inx} \frac{1 - e^{i(2n+1)x}}{1 - e^{ix}} \\
	=& \frac{1}{2}\frac{e^{-inx} - e^{i(n+1)x}}{1 - e^{ix}} \frac{-e^{-ix/2}}{-e^{-ix/2}} \\
	=& \frac{1}{2} \frac{e^{i\left(n + 1/2\right)x} - e^{-i\left(n + 1/2\right)x}}{e^{ix/2} - e^{-ix/2}} \frac{\frac{1}{2i}}{\frac{1}{2i}} \\
	=& \frac{1}{2} \frac{\sin\left[\left(n + \frac{1}{2}\right)x\right]}{\sin\left(\frac{1}{2}x \right)}
\end{align*}
$$
</details>
</MathBox>

<MathBox title='' boxType="lemma" tag="2">
If $x\in(0,2\pi)$, then

$$
	\sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{\sin(kx)}{k} = \frac{\pi - x}{2}
$$

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>

Noting that $\sin(kx)/k = \int_{\pi}^x \cos(kt)\;\d t$ and applying Lemma $\ref{lemma-1}$, we get

$$
\begin{align*}
	\sum_{k=1}^n \frac{\sin(kx)}{k} =& \sum_{k=1}^n \int_\pi^x \cos(kt) \;\d t = \int_\pi^x \sum_{k=1}^n \cos(kt)\;\d t \\
	=& \int_\pi^x \left(\frac{1}{2} \frac{\sin\left[\left(n + \frac{1}{2}\right)x\right]}{\sin\left(\frac{1}{2}x \right)} - \frac{1}{2} \right)\;\d t \\
	=& \underbrace{\int_\pi^x \frac{\sin\left[\left(n + \frac{1}{2}\right)t\right]}{2\sin\left(\frac{1}{2}t\right)}\;\d t}_{f_n (x)} - \frac{1}{2}(x - \pi)
\end{align*}
$$

Integrating the first term, $f_n$, by parts with 

$$
\begin{aligned}
	u =& \frac{1}{2\sin\left(\frac{1}{2}t\right)} \\
	u' =& -\frac{1}{2} \frac{\frac{1}{2}\cos(\frac{1}{2}t)}{\sin^2 (\frac{1}{2}t)}
\end{aligned}\quad
\begin{aligned}
	v' =& \sin\left[\left(n + \frac{1}{2}\right)t\right] \\
	v =& -\frac{1}{n + \frac{1}{2}} \cos\left[\left(n + \frac{1}{2}\right)t\right]
\end{aligned}
$$

we get

$$
\begin{align*}
	f_n (x) =& \left. -\frac{1}{n + \frac{1}{2}} \cos\left[\left(n + \frac{1}{2}\right)t\right] \right|_\pi^x \\
	&- \int_\pi^x \frac{1}{n + \frac{1}{2}} \frac{(-1)\cos\left[\left(n + \frac{1}{2}\right) t \right]\cos\left(\frac{1}{2}t\right)}{(-4)\sin^2 \left(\frac{1}{2}t\right)} \;\d t \\
	=& \frac{1}{n + \frac{1}{2}} \left(\frac{-\cos\left[\left(n + \frac{1}{2}\right) x \right]}{2\sin\left(\frac{1}{2}x \right)} \right. \\
	&- \left. \frac{1}{4}\int_\pi^x \frac{\cos\left[\left(n + \frac{1}{2}\right) t \right]\cos\left(\frac{1}{2}t\right)}{\sin^2 \left(\frac{1}{2}t\right)}\right) \\
	=& \frac{1}{n + \frac{1}{2}} \left(a(x) + b(x) \right)
\end{align*}
$$

To show uniform convergence, we require that $\norm{ f_n }_\infty \xrightarrow{n\to 0}$, where $\norm{\cdot}_\infty$ is the supremum norm. For $\epsilon > 0$, choose $x\in[\epsilon, 2\pi - \epsilon]$. Then, by the triangle inequality we get

$$
\begin{align*}
	\norm{ f_n }_\infty \tleq& \frac{1}{n + \frac{1}{2}} (\left \norm{ a }_\infty + \norm{ b }_\infty \right) \\
	\leq& \frac{1}{n + \frac{1}{2}} \left(\frac{1}{2\delta} + \frac{1}{4\delta^2} \pi \right) \xrightarrow{n\to\infty} 0 
\end{align*}
$$
</details>
</MathBox>

<MathBox title='' boxType="proposition" tag="3">
If $x\in[0,2\pi]$, then

$$
	\sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{\cos(kx)}{k^2} = \frac{(x - \pi)^2}{4} - \frac{\pi^2}{12}
$$

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>

Applying Lemma $\ref{lemma-2}$ and noting that $\sin(kx)/k = -\int_{x_0}^x \cos(kt)/k^2 \;\d t$, we get for $\epsilon > 0$ and $x, x_0 \in [\epsilon, 2\pi - \epsilon]$

$$
\begin{align*}
	\int_{x_0}^x \sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{\sin(kt)}{k}\;\d t =& \int_{x_0}^x \frac{\pi - t}{2}\;\d t = \left. \frac{(\pi - t)^2}{4}\right|_{x_0}^x \\
	=& -\frac{(x - \pi)^2}{4} + \underbrace{\frac{(x_0 - \pi)^2}{4}}_{C_0}
\end{align*}
$$

By uniform convergence, we have

$$
\begin{align*}
	\int_{x_0}^x \sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{\sin(kt)}{k}\;\d t =& \sum_{k=1}^\infty \int_{x_0}^x \frac{\sin(kx)}{k}\;\d t \\
	=& \sum_{k=1}^\infty \left.-\frac{-\cos(kt)}{k^2}\right|_{x_0}^x \\
	=& -\sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{\cos(kx)}{k^2} + \underbrace{\sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{\cos(kx_0)}{k^2}}_{C_1} 
\end{align*}
$$

Combining the results, we get

$$
\begin{equation}
	\sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{\cos(kx)}{k^2} = \frac{(x - \pi)^2}{4} + C \tag{\label{equation-3}}
\end{equation}
$$

By Weierstrass M-test, the left-hand side of $\eqref{equation-3}$ is uniformly convergent on $[0,2\pi]$ since $\left|\frac{\cos(kx)}{k^2}\right|\leq \frac{1}{k^2}$. It follows that

$$
	[0,2\pi] \ni x \mapsto \sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{\cos(kx)}{k^2}
$$

is continuous. Furthermore, the right-hand side of $\eqref{equation-3}$ is also continous for $x\in[0,2\pi]$. Thus, $\eqref{equation-3}$ is uniformly convergent on $[0,2\pi]$.

Integrating $\eqref{equation-1}$, we find

$$
	\int_0^{2\pi} \sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{\cos(kx)}{k^2} \;\d x = \int_0^{2\pi} \left( \frac{(x - \pi)^2}{4} + C \right)\;\d x = \underbrace{\left. \frac{(x - \pi)^3}{12}\right|_0^{2\pi}}_{=\pi^3 / 6} + 2\pi C
$$

By uniform convergence the left-hand integral can be written

$$
	\int_0^{2\pi} \sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{\cos(kx)}{k^2} \;\d x = \sum_{k=1}^\infty \int_0^{2\pi} \frac{\cos(kx)}{k^2} \;\d x = 0
$$

which follows since $\cos(kx)$ is integrated over a full period $2\pi$. Hence it follows that $C = -\pi^2 / 12$, completing the proof.
</details>
</MathBox>

# Trigonometric polynomials

<MathBox title='Periodic function' boxType="definition">
Let $k\in\R$. A function $f:\R\to\mathbb{F}$ is $k$-periodic if $f(x + k) = f(x)$ for all $x\in\R$. The set of all $k$-period real functions is denoted

$$
	\mathcal{F}_k (\R,\R) = \Set{f:\R\to\mathbb{F} | f(x + k) = f(x)\; \forall x\in\R}
$$
</MathBox>

<MathBox title='' boxType="proposition">
The set of $2\pi$-periodic functions $\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{2\pi} (\R,\R)$ given by

$$
	\mathcal{U} := \set{x\mapsto\sin(nx)}_{n\in\N_+} \cup \set{x\mapsto\cos(nx)}_{n\in\N}
$$

is linearly independent. The space spanned by $\mathcal{U}$ is denoted $P_{2\pi} := \operatorname{span}(\mathcal{U})$.
</MathBox>

<MathBox title='Trigonometric polynomial' boxType="definition">
Let $\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{2\pi} (\R,\R)$ be given by

$$
	\mathcal{U} := \set{x\mapsto\sin(nx)}_{n\in\N_+} \cup \set{x\mapsto\cos(nx)}_{n\in\N}
$$

A linear combination of $f\in\operatorname{span}(\mathcal{U})$ for $f:\R\to\R$ is called a (real) trigonometric polynomial and takes the form

$$
	f(x) = a_0 + \sum_{k=1}^n a_k \cos(kx) + \sum_{k=1}^n b_k \cos(kx),\; a_k, b_k \in\R
$$

If $\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{2\pi} (\R,\mathbb{C})$, the linear combination is called a complex trigonometric polynomial and takes the form

$$
	f(x) = \sum_{k=-n}^n c_k e^{ikx},\; c_k \in \mathbb{C}
$$
</MathBox>

<MathBox title='Trigonometric basis is orthogonal' boxType="proposition">
Let $\mathcal{F}_{2\pi} (\R,\R)$ be equipped with the inner product

$$
\begin{equation*}
	\braket{f, g} := \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi f(x)g(x)\;\d x,\; f,g\in\mathcal{F}_{2\pi}
\tag{\label{equation-8}}
\end{equation*}
$$

The set of $2\pi$-periodic functions $\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{2\pi} (\R,\R)$ given by

$$
	\mathcal{B} := \set{x\mapsto\sin(kx)}_{k\in\N_+} \cup \set{x\mapsto\cos(kx)}_{k\in\N}
$$

forms an orthogonal basis for $\mathcal{F}_{2\pi}$.


<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>

We calculate the inner product for all possible combinations from $\mathcal{B}$. For $x\mapsto 1$ we trivially have

$$
	\braket{ x\mapsto 1, x\mapsto 1 } = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi 1\;\d x = 1
$$

Next

$$
\begin{align*}
	\braket{ x\mapsto 1, x\mapsto\cos(kx) } =& \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi \cos(kx)\;\d x \\
	=& \frac{1}{2\pi k} [\sin(kx)]_{-\pi}^\pi = 0
\end{align*}
$$

Since $x\mapsto\cos(kx)\sin(lx)$ for $m,n\in\N$ is an odd function, it follows that

$$
\begin{align*}
	\braket{ x\mapsto\cos(kx), x\mapsto\sin(lx) } = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi \cos(kx)\sin(lx)\;\d x = 0
\end{align*}
$$

Noting that $\cos(x) = \frac{1}{2}(e^{ix} + e^{-ix})$, we get for $k\neq l$

$$
\begin{align*}
	&\braket{ x\mapsto\cos(kx), x\mapsto\cos(lx)} \\ 
	=& \frac{1}{8\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} e^{i(k+l)x} + e^{-i(k+l)x} + e^{i(k-l)x} + e^{-i(k-l)x}\;\d x \\
	=& \frac{1}{4\pi}\left[\frac{1}{k+l}\frac{1}{2i} (e^{i(k+l)x} + e^{-i(k+l)x}) +  \frac{1}{k-l}\frac{1}{2i}\left(e^{i(k-l)} + e^{-i(k-l)}\right) \right]_{-\pi}^{\pi} \\
	=& \frac{1}{4\pi} \left[\frac{1}{k+l} \sin[(k+l)x] + \frac{1}{k-l}\sin[(k-l)x] \right]_{-\pi}^\pi = 0
\end{align*}
$$

Similarly, we find

$$
	\braket{ x\to\sin(kx), x\to\sin(lx)} = \int_{-\pi}^\pi \sin(kx) \sin(lx)\;\d x = 0
$$

This shows that $\braket{ f,g} = 0$ for $f,g\in\mathcal{U}$ with $f\neq g$.
</details>
</MathBox>

<MathBox title='Complex trigonometric basis is orthogonal' boxType="proposition">
Let $\mathcal{F}_{2\pi} (\R,\mathbb{C})$ be equipped with the inner product

$$
	\braket{f, g} := \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi \overline{f(x)}g(x)\;\d x,\; f,g\in\mathcal{F}_{2\pi}
$$

The set of $2\pi$-periodic functions $\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{2\pi} (\R,\mathbb{C})$ given by

$$
	\mathcal{E} := \Set{e_k := e^{ikx} = \cos(kx) + i\sin(kx)}_{k\in\Z}
$$

forms an orthogonal basis for $\mathcal{F}_{2\pi}$.

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>

This is easily shown by calculating the inner product

$$
\begin{align*}
	\braket{ e_k, e_k} =& \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi e^{-ikx} e^{ilx}\;\d x = \int_{-\pi}^\pi e^{i(l - k)x}\;\d x \\
	=& \left.\frac{1}{i2\pi(l - k)}e^{i(l - k)x}\right|_{-\pi}^\pi = \delta_{kl}
\end{align*}
$$
</details>
</MathBox>

<MathBox title='Orthonormal trigonometric bases' boxType="proposition" tag="1">
1. The basis $\mathcal{B}_1 = \set{x\mapsto\sqrt{2}\cos(nx)}_{n\in\N} \cup \set{x\mapsto\sqrt{2}\sin(nx)}_{n\in\N_+}$ is orthonormal with respect to the inner product
$$
	\braket{ f, g }_1 := \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi f(x)g(x)\;\d x
$$
2. The basis $\mathcal{B}_2 = \set{x\mapsto 1} \cup \set{x\mapsto\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\cos(nx)}_{n\in\N_+} \cup \set{x\mapsto\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sin(nx)}_{n\in\N_+}$ is orthonormal with respect to the inner product
$$
	\braket{ f, g }_2 := \int_{-\pi}^\pi f(x)g(x)\;\d x
$$
3. The basis $\mathcal{B}_3 =  \set{x\mapsto 1/\sqrt{2}} \cup \set{x\mapsto\cos(nx)}_{n\in\N_+} \cup \set{x\mapsto\sin(nx)}_{n\in\N_+}$ is orthonormal with respect to the inner product
$$
	\braket{ f, g }_3 := \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^\pi f(x)g(x)\;\d x
$$

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>

We start by calculating $\norm{ x\mapsto\sin(nx) }^2 = \braket{ x\mapsto\sin(nx), x\mapsto\sin(nx) }_1$. Using integration by parts we find

$$
\begin{align*}
	\int_{-\pi}^\pi \sin^2 (nx) \;\d x =& \int_{-\pi}^\pi \underbrace{\sin(nx)}_{u} \underbrace{\sin(nx)}_{v' } \\
	=& \underbrace{\left[\sin(nx)\left(-\frac{1}{n} \right)\cos(nx)\right]_{-\pi}^\pi}_{=0} - \int_{-\pi}^\pi n\cos(nx) \left(-\frac{1}{n}\right) \cos(nx) \;\d x \\
	=& \int_{-\pi}^\pi \cos^2 (nx)^2 \; \d x = \int_{-\pi}^\pi 1 - \sin^2 (nx) \;\d x
\end{align*}
$$

We can rewrite this as

$$
\begin{gather}
	2 \int_{-\pi}^\pi \sin^2 (nx) \;\d x = \int_{-\pi}^\pi 1 \;\d x = 2\pi \\
	\implies \int_{-\pi}^\pi \sin^2 (nx) \;\d x = \pi
	\implies \norm{ x\mapsto\sin(nx) }^2 = \frac{1}{2}
\end{gather}
$$

It follows that $\mathbf{x} \mapsto \sqrt{2}\sin(nx)$ has unit norm. A similar calculation shows that $\mathbf{x} \mapsto \sqrt{2}\cos(nx)$ has unit norm. Hence, $\mathcal{B}_1$ is orthonormal with respect to $\braket{\cdot,\cdot}_1$.

Since $\int_{-\pi}^\pi \sin^2(nx) \;\d x = \int_{-\pi}^\pi \cos^2 (nx) \;\d x = \pi$ it follows that $\mathcal{B}_2$ is orthonormal with respect to $\braket{\cdot,\cdot}_2$, and that $\mathcal{B}_3$ is orthonormal with respect to $\braket{\cdot,\cdot}_3$.
</details>
</MathBox>

## Integrable functions

<MathBox title='Integrable periodic function' boxType="definition">
Let $k\in\R$. A $k$-period function $f\in \mathcal{F}_k (\R,\mathbb{F})$ is $n$-integrable if

$$
	\int_{-k/2}^{k/2} |f(x)|^n \;\d x < \infty
$$

The set of all integrable $k$-periodic functions is denoted

$$
	\mathcal{L}_k^n (\R,\R) = \Set{f\in\mathcal{F}_k (\R, \mathbb{F}) | \int_{-k/2}^{k/2} |f(x)|^n \;\d x < \infty}
$$

and forms a vector space, which is not a normed space. To form a normed space, we define the equivalence relation $f\sim g :\iff \norm{ f-g}_n = 0$ with respect to the norm

$$
	\norm{ f }_n := \left(\int_{-k/2}^{k/2} |f(x)|^n \;\d x\right)^{1/n}
$$

The quotient space $L_k^n (\R,\mathbb{C}) := \mathcal{L}_k^n (\R, \mathbb{F}) / \sim$ is a normed space with norm

$$
	\norm{ [f] }_n := \norm{ f }_n
$$

In particular, we have

$$
	L_{2\pi}^1 (\R, \mathbb{F}) \supset L_{2\pi}^2 (\R, \mathbb{F}) \supset\cdots\supset L_{2\pi}^\infty \supset \mathcal{P}_{2\pi} = \operatorname{span}(\set{x\mapsto\cos(mx)}_{m\in\N} \cup \set{x\mapsto\sin(mx)}_{m\in\N_+})
$$
</MathBox>

# Fourier series

<MathBox title='Fourier series in $L^2$' boxType="definition">
Let $f\in L_{2\pi}^2 (\R,\R)$ be a square integrable $2\pi$-periodic function with respect to the inner product

$$
	\braket{ f, g} = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi f(x)g(x) \;\d x
$$

If $\mathcal{U}_n = \operatorname{span}(\mathcal{B}_n)$ for $n\in\N$ is a finite-dimensional subspace spanned by the trigonometric basis

$$
	\mathcal{B}_n = \set{x \mapsto 1/\sqrt{2}} \cup \set{\cos(kx)}_{k=1}^n \cup \set{\sin(kx)}_{k=1}^n
$$

the orthogonal projection of $f$ onto $\mathcal{U}_n$ is given by

$$
	\mathcal{F}_n (f)(x) = \tilde{a}_0 \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} + \sum_{k=1}^n a_k \cos(kx) + \sum_{k=1}^n b_k \sin(kx)
$$

with

$$
\begin{align*}
	\tilde{a}_0 =& \Braket{ x\mapsto \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}, f} = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} f(x) \;\d x \\
	a_k =& \braket{ x\mapsto\cos(kx), f } = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi \cos(kx) f(x) \;\d x \\
	b_k =& \braket{ x\mapsto\sin(kx), f } = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi \sin(kx) f(x) \;\d x
\end{align*}
$$

The map $n\mapsto \mathcal{F}_n (f)(x)$ is the Fourier series of $f$
</MathBox>

Fourier series can also be expressed in terms of trigonometric exponential functions. If 

$$
\begin{align*}
	\mathcal{B}_n =& \Set{x \mapsto \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}} \cup \Set{\cos(kx)}_{k=1}^n \cup \Set{\sin(kx)}_{k=1}^n \\
	\mathcal{E}_n =& \Set{x\mapsto e^{ikx} = \cos(kx) + i\sin(kx)}_{k=-n}^n, \; n\in\N
\end{align*}
$$

then

$$
	\operatorname{span}(\mathcal{B}_n) = \operatorname{span}(\mathcal{E}_n)
$$

and

$$
	\frac{\tilde{a}_0}{\sqrt{2}} + \sum_{k=1}^n a_k \cos(kx) + \sum_{k=1}^n b_k \sin(kx) = \sum_{k=-n}^n c_k e^{ikx}
$$

with

$$
	c_k = \begin{cases}
		\frac{1}{2} \left(a_k + \frac{b_k}{i} \right), \quad& k > 0 \\
		\tilde{a_0} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}, \quad& k = 0 \\
		\frac{1}{2} \left(a_{-k} - \frac{b_{-k}}{i} \right), \quad& k < 0
	\end{cases}
$$

<MathBox title='Complex Fourier series in $L^2$' boxType="definition">
Let $f\in L_{2\pi}^2 (\R,\mathbb{C})$ be a square integrable $2\pi$-periodic function with respect to the inner product

$$
	\braket{ f, g} = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi \overline{f(x)}g(x) \;\d x
$$

If $\mathcal{U}_n = \operatorname{span}(\mathcal{E}_n)$ for $n\in\N$ is a finite-dimensional subspace spanned by the trigonometric exponential basis

$$
	\mathcal{E}_n = \Set{x\mapsto e^{ikx}}_{k=-n}^n,
$$

the orthogonal projection of $f$ onto $\mathcal{U}_n$ is given by

$$
	\mathcal{F}_n (f)(x) = \sum_{k=-n}^n c_k e^{ikx}
$$

with

$$
	c_k = \braket{ x \mapsto e^{ikx}, f } = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi e^{-ikx} f(x) \;\d x
$$

The map $n\mapsto \mathcal{F}_n (f)$ is the complex Fourier series of $f$.
</MathBox>

If $f \in L_{2\pi}^2 (\R,\mathbb{C})$ is a $2\pi$-periodic square integrable function, its complex Fourier series is given by

$$
	\mathcal{F}_n (f)(x) = \sum_{k=-n}^n \braket{ x\mapsto e^{ikx}, f} e^{ikx} = \sum_{k=-n}^n c_k e^{ikx},\; n\in\N
$$

Assuming $L_{2\pi}^2 (\R,\mathbb{C})$ is equipped with the inner product

$$
	\braket{ f, g} = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi \overline{f(x)}g(x) \;\d x
$$

we can write out the complex coefficients $c_k\in\mathbb{C}$ as

$$
\begin{align*}
	c_k =& \Braket{ f, e^{ikx}} = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^\pi e^{-ikx} f(x) \;\d x \\ 
	=& \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^\pi \left[\cos(kx) - i \sin(kx) \right]f(x)\;\d x \\
	=& \braket{ x\mapsto \cos(kx), f } - i\braket{ x\mapsto \sin(kx), f } = \alpha_k - i\beta_k
\end{align*}
$$

The complex Fourier series of $f$ can thus be rewritten as

$$
\begin{align*}
	\mathcal{F}_n (f)(x) =& \sum_{k=-n}^n c_n e^{inx} = \sum_{k=-n}^n \left( \alpha_k - i\beta_k \right) \left(\cos kx + i\sin kx \right) \\
	=& \left(\alpha_0 - i\beta_0 \right) + \sum_{k=1}^n \left[\left(\alpha_{-k} + \alpha_{k} \right)\cos kx + \left(\beta_{-k} - \beta_k \right)\sin kx\right] \\
	&+ i\sum_{k=1}^n \left[\left(\beta_{-k} + \beta_{k} \right)\cos kx + \left(\alpha_{-k} - \alpha_k \right)\sin kx\right]
\end{align*}
$$

If $f$ is real-valued, then $\alpha_{-k} = \alpha_{k}$, $\beta_{-k} = -\beta_{k}$ and $\beta_0 = 0$, so that the Fourier series reduces to

$$
	\mathcal{F}_n (f)(x) = \frac{a_0}{\sqrt{2}} + \sum_{k=1}^n \left[a_k \cos(kx) + b_k \sin(kx) \right]
$$

with 

$$
\begin{align*}
	a_k =& 2\alpha_k = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi \cos(kx)f(x)\;\d x \\
	b_k =& 2\beta_k = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi \sin(kx)f(x)\;\d x
\end{align*}
$$

The Fourier series for a $p$-periodic function $f\in L_p^2 (\R, \mathbb{C})$ can be similarly found using the orthogonal basis $\Set{ e_k := x\mapsto\exp\left(i\frac{2\pi k}{p}x\right)}_{k\in\Z}$ giving

$$
\begin{align*}
	\mathcal{F}_n (f) =& \frac{a_0}{\sqrt{2}} + \sum_{k=1}^n \left[ a_k \cos\left( \frac{2\pi k}{p} x\right) + b_k \sin\left( \frac{2\pi k}{p} x\right) \right] \\

\end{align*}

	c_n = \braket{ e_k, f } = \frac{1}{p}\int_0^L \exp\left(i\frac{2\pi k}{p}x\right) f(x)\;\d x

<MathBox title='Geometric properties of Fourier series' boxType="proposition" tag="2">
Let $f\in L_{2\pi}^2 (\R,\mathbb{C})$ have complex Fourier series 

$$
	\mathcal{F}_n (f) = \sum_{k=-n}^n \braket{ x\mapsto e^{ikx}, f} e^{ikx} = \sum_{k=-n}^n c_k e^{ikx}
$$

with respect to the inner product

$$
	\braket{ f, g } = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi \overline{f(x)} g(x) \;\d x,\; f,g\in L_{2\pi}^2 (\R,\mathbb{C})
$$

then

1. $\norm{ f - \mathcal{F}_n (f)}^2 = \norm{ f }^2 - \sum_{k=-n}^n |c_k|^2$
2. **Bessel's inequality:** $\sum_{k=-n}^n |c_k|^2 \leq \norm{ f }^2$ for all $n\in\N$
		- Consequently $\sum_{k=-\infty}^\infty |c_k|^2 \leq \norm{ f }^2$ and $c_k \xrightarrow{k\to\infty} 0$
3. **Parseval's identity:** 
$$
	\lim_{n\to\infty} \norm{ f - \mathcal{F}_n (f) } = 0 \iff \sum_{k=-\infty}^\infty |c_k|^2 = \norm{ f }^2
$$

It follows directly from Parseval's identity that any $f\in L_{2\pi}^2 (\R,\mathbb{F})$ can be written $f = \mathcal{F}_n (f) + r_n$ with $\norm{ r_n } \xrightarrow{n\to\infty} 0$.

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>

**(1):** By the Pythagorean theorem we have

$$
\begin{gather*}
	\norm{ f }^2 = \norm{ \mathcal{F}_n (f) }^2 + \norm{ f - \mathcal{F}_n (f) }^2 \\
	\implies \norm{ f - \mathcal{F}_n (f)}^2 = \norm{ f }^2 - \norm{ f - \mathcal{F}_n (f) }^2
\end{gather*}
$$

Since $\Set{x\mapsto e^{ikx}}_{k\in\Z}$ is orthonormal with respect to the the inner product, it follows that 

$$
\begin{align*}
	\norm{ \mathcal{F}_n (f) }^2 =& \braket{ \mathcal{F}_n (f), \mathcal{F}_n (f) } \\
	=& \Braket{ \sum_{k=-n}^n c_k e^{ikx}, \sum_{\ell=-n}^n c_k e^{i\ell x}} \\
	=& \sum_{k=-n}^n \sum_{\ell = -n}^n c_k \overline{c_\ell} \underbrace{\braket{ e^{ikx}, e^{i \ell x} }}_{=\delta_{k\ell}} \\
	=& \sum_{k=-n}^n |c_k|^2
\end{align*}
$$

<MathBox title='' boxType="proposition" tag="6">
Let $f\in L_{2\pi}^2 (\R,\mathbb{C})$ have complex Fourier series 

$$
	\mathcal{F}_n (f) = \sum_{k=-n}^n \braket{ x\mapsto e^{ikx}, f} e^{ikx} = \sum_{k=-n}^n c_k e^{ikx}

with respect to the inner product

$$

then the following are equivalent:
1. **Parseval's identity:** $\norm{ f }^2 = \sum_{k=-\infty}^\infty |\braket{ x\mapsto e^{ikx}, f }|^2$
2. The orthonormal system $\mathcal{E} = \Set{x\mapsto e^{ikx}}_{k\in\Z}$ is complete, i.e. 
$$
	\lim_{n\to\infty} \Norm{ f - \sum_{k=-n}^n e^{ikx} \braket{ x\mapsto e^{ikx}, f } } = 0
$$
3. The orthonormal system $\mathcal{E}$ gives the inner product
$$
	\braket{ f, g } = \sum_{k=-\infty}^\infty \braket{ f, x\mapsto e^{ikx} } \braket{ x\mapsto e^{ikx}, g }
$$
4. The orthonormal system $\mathcal{E}$ is total such that $\operatorname{span}(\mathcal{E})$ is dense in $L_{2\pi}^2 (\R, \mathbb{F})$. Equivalently,
$$
\begin{gather*}
	\forall f\in L_{2\pi}^2 (\R,\mathbb{F}) \; \forall \epsilon > 0 \; \exists N\in\N, \; \lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_N \in \mathbb{F}: \\
	\Norm{ f - \sum_{k=-N}^N \lambda_k e^{ikx} } < \epsilon
\end{gather*}
$$

Equivalently, we can state $(3)$ as

$$
	\sum_{k=-\infty}^\infty \ket{x\mapsto e^{ikx}}\bra{x\mapsto e^{ikx}} = \mathbf{1}
$$

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>

We want to express to inner product of $f,g\in L_{2\pi}^2 (\R,\mathbb{F})$ in terms of Fourier coefficients. By Parseval's identity (Proposition $\ref{proposition-2}$) we can write

$$
\begin{align*}
	f =& \mathcal{F}_n (f) + r_n\; \lim_{n\to\infty} \norm{ r_n } = 0 \\
	g =& \mathcal{F}_n (g) + \tilde{r}_n\; \lim_{n\to\infty} \norm{ \tilde{r}_n } = 0 \\
\end{align*}
$$

By the Cauchy-Schwarz and Bessel inequalities, we have

$$
	|\braket{ \mathcal{F}_n (g), r_n }| \leq \norm{ \mathcal{F}_n (g) } \cdot \norm{ r_n }\leq\norm{ g }\cdot\norm{ r_n } \xrightarrow{n\to\infty} 0
$$

Thus, we can write the inner product of $f$ and $g$ as

$$
\begin{align*}
	\braket{ f, g } =& \braket{ \mathcal{F}_n (f) + r_n, \mathcal{F}_n (g) + \tilde{r}_n } \\
	=& \braket{ \mathcal{F}_n (f), \mathcal{F}_n (g) } + \braket{ r_n, \mathcal{F}_n (g) } + \braket{ \mathcal{F}_n (f), \tilde{r}_n } + \braket{ r_n, \tilde{r}_n } \tag{\label{equation-1}}
\end{align*}
$$

The first term can be expressed as

$$
\begin{align*}
	\braket{ \mathcal{F}_n (f), \mathcal{F}_n (g) } =& \Braket{\sum_{k=-n}^n e^{ikx} \braket{x\mapsto e^{ikx}, f}, \sum_{\ell=-n}^n e^{i\ell x} \braket{x\mapsto e^{i\ell x}, g}} \\
	=& \sum_{k=-n}^n \sum_{\ell=-n}^n \overline{\braket{ x\mapsto e^{ikx}, f }} \langle x\mapsto e^{i\ell x} \underbrace{\braket{x\mapsto e^{ikx}, x\mapsto e^{i\ell x}}}_{=\delta_{k\ell}} \\
	=& \sum_{k=-n}^n \braket{ f, x\mapsto e^{ikx}} \braket{x\mapsto e^{ikx}, g}
\end{align*}
$$

Taking the limit $n\to\infty$, the residual terms of $\eqref{equation-1}$ vanish, such that we obtain

$$
	\braket{ f, g } = \braket{ \mathcal{F}_n (f) + r_n, \mathcal{F}_n (g) + \tilde{r}_n } \xrightarrow{n\to\infty} \sum_{k=-\infty}^\infty \braket{ f, x\mapsto e^{ikx}} \braket{ x\mapsto e^{ikx}, g }
$$

## Parseval's identity

<MathBox title="Parseval's identity for simple step functions" boxType="lemma" tag="3">
Parseval's identity holds for simple step functions $h_a \in L_{2\pi}^2 (\R,\R)$ defined by

$$
	h_a (x) = \begin{cases}
		1,\quad& x\in[-\pi, a] \\
		0,\quad& x\in(a, \pi)
	\end{cases},\; a\in [-\pi, \pi]
$$

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>

The Fourier coefficients for $h_a$ is given by

$$
\begin{align*}
	c_k =& \braket{ x\mapsto e^{ikx}, h_a } = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi e^{-ikx} h_a \;\d x = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\pi}^a e^{-ikx} \;\d x \\
	=& \begin{cases}
		\frac{a + \pi}{2\pi},\quad& k = 0 \\
		\frac{1}{2\pi(-ik)} (e^{-ika} - e^{ik\pi}),\quad& k \neq 0
	\end{cases}
\end{align*}
$$

Taking the square modulus of $c_k$ for $k\neq 0$ yields

$$
\begin{align*}
	|c_k|^2 =& \frac{1}{2\pi (-ik)} (e^{-ika} - e^{ik\pi}) \overline{\frac{1}{2\pi(-ik)} (e^{-ika} - e^{ik\pi})} \\
	=& \frac{1}{4\pi^2 k^2} (e^{-ika} - e^{ik\pi})(e^{ika} - e^{-ik\pi}) \\
	=& \frac{1}{4\pi^2 k^2} (1 - e^{ik(\pi + a)} - e^{-ik(\pi + a)} + 1) \\
	=& \frac{1}{4\pi^2 k^2} (2 - 2\cos[k(\pi + a)]) \\
	=& \frac{1}{2\pi^2 k^2} (1 - \cos[k(\pi + a)])
\end{align*}
$$

Summing over $k = -n,\dots,0,\dots,n$ gives

$$
\begin{align*}
	\sum_{k=-n}^n |c_k|^2 =& \left(\frac{a + \pi}{2\pi}\right)^2 + \frac{1}{2\pi^2} \sum_{\substack{k=-n \\ k\neq 0}}^n \left(\frac{1}{k^2} - \frac{\cos[k(\pi + a)]}{k^2} \right) \\
	=& \left(\frac{a + \pi}{2\pi}\right)^2 + \frac{1}{2\pi} \left(\sum_{k=1}^n \frac{1}{k^2} - \sum_{k=1}^n \frac{\cos[k(\pi + a)]}{k^2} \right)
\end{align*}
$$

Applying Proposition $\ref{proposition-3}$, i.e.

$$
	\sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{\cos(kx)}{k^2} = \frac{(x - \pi)^2}{4} - \frac{\pi^2}{12},\; x\in[0,2\pi]
$$

we get 

$$
\begin{gather*}
	\sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{1}{k^2} = \frac{\pi^2}{6} \\
	\sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{\cos[k(\pi + a)]}{k^2} = \frac{a^2}{4} - \frac{\pi^2}{12}
\end{gather*}
$$

Taking the limit $n\to\infty$, we obtain

$$
\begin{align*}
	\sum_{k=-\infty}^\infty |c_k|^2 =& \left(\frac{a + \pi}{2\pi} \right)^2 + \frac{1}{\pi^2}\left(\frac{\pi^2}{6} - \frac{a^2}{4} + \frac{\pi^2}{12} \right) \\
	=& \left(\frac{a + \pi}{2\pi}\right)^2 + \frac{1}{4} - \frac{a^2}{4\pi} = \frac{2a\pi + \pi^2}{4\pi^2} + \frac{1}{4} \\
	=& \frac{a}{2\pi} + \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{2\pi}(a + \pi) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\pi}^a 1 \;\d x \\
	=& \braket{ h_a, h_a } = \norm{ h_a }^2
\end{align*}
$$

proving Parseval's identity for $h_a$.
</details>
</MathBox>

<MathBox title="Parseval's identity for simple functions" boxType="proposition">
Parseval's identity holds for the set of $2\pi$-periodic simple functions $\mathcal{S}_{2\pi} (\R,\mathbb{C}) \subseteq L_{2\pi}^2 (\R,\mathbb{C})$ defined as

$$
	\mathcal{S}_{2\pi} (\R,\mathbb{C}) := \Set{g\in\mathcal{F}_{2\pi}| \exists m\in\N, a_i \in[-\pi,\pi], \lambda_i\in\mathbb{C}: g = \sum_{i=1}^m \lambda_i h_{a_i}}
$$

where $h_a \in L_{2\pi}^2 (\R,\R)$ is a simple step function defined by

$$
	h_a (x) = \begin{cases}
		1,\quad& x\in[-\pi, a] \\
		0,\quad& x\in(a, \pi)
	\end{cases},\; a\in [-\pi, \pi]
$$

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>

The Fourier coefficients for $g$ are given by 

$$
	c_k = \braket{ x\mapsto e^{ikx}, g } = \Braket{ x\mapsto e^{ikx}, \sum_{i=1}^m \lambda_i h_{a_i}} = \sum_{i=1}^m \lambda_i \braket{x\mapsto e^{ikx}, h_{a_i}}
$$

Taking the square modulus of $c_k$ gives

$$
\begin{align*}
	|c_k|^2 =& \overline{c_k} c_k = \sum_{j=1}^m \overline{\lambda_j \braket{ x\mapsto e^{ikx}, h_{a_j}}} \sum_{l=1}^m \lambda_i \braket{ x\mapsto e^{ikx}, h_{a_l} } \\
	=& \sum_{j=1}^m \sum_{l=1}^m \overline{\lambda_j} \lambda_l \braket{ h_{a_j}, x\mapsto e^{ikx} } \braket{ x\mapsto e^{ikx}, h_{a_l} }
\end{align*}
$$

Summing over $k = -n,\dots,0,\dots,n$ gives

$$
	\sum_{k=-n}^n |c_k|^2 = \sum_{i,l=1}^m \overline{\lambda_j} \lambda_l \left(\braket{ h_{a_j}, x\mapsto e^{ikx} } \braket{x\mapsto e^{ikx}, h_{a_l}} \right)
$$

By Proposition $\ref{proposition-6}$, we have $\sum_{k=-\infty}^\infty \ket{x\mapsto e^{ikx}}\bra{x\mapsto e^{ikx}} = \mathbf{1}$.From Lemma $\ref{lemma-3}$, we know that Parseval's identity holds for each $h_{a_i}$. Thus, in the limit $n\to\infty$ we obtain

$$
\begin{align*}
	\sum_{k=-\infty}^\infty |c_k|^2 =& \sum_{i,l=1}^m \overline{\lambda_j} \lambda_l \left(\braket{ h_{a_j}, x\mapsto e^{ikx} } \braket{x\mapsto e^{ikx}, h_{a_l}} \right) \\
	=& \sum_{i,l=1}^m \overline{\lambda_j} \lambda_l \braket{ h_{a_j}, h_{a_l} } \\
	=& \Braket{ \sum_{j=1}^m \lambda_j h_{a_j}, \sum_{l=1}^m \lambda_l h_{a_l}} \\
	=& \braket{g, g} = \norm{g}^2
\end{align*}
$$
</details>
</MathBox>

<MathBox title="Square integrable simple periodic functions are dense" boxType="proposition" tag="4">
The set of simple $2\pi$-periodic functions $\mathcal{S}_{2\pi} (\R,\mathbb{C})$ is dense in $L_{2\pi} (\R, \mathbb{C})$. That is, for $f\in\mathcal{S}_{2\pi} (\R,\mathbb{C})$ and $\epsilon > 0$, there is a $2\pi$-periodic simple function $g:\R\mathbb{C}$ with $\norm{ f - g } < \epsilon$.

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>

Let $\epsilon > 0$ and suppose $f:[-\pi,\pi]\to\mathbb{C}$ is square integrable. Then there is a continuous function $g:[-\pi,\pi]$ with $\norm{ f - g } < \epsilon$. Since the domain $[-\pi,\pi]$ is compact, if follows that $g$ is uniformly continuous. Thus, for $\epsilon > 0$, there is $\delta > 0$ such that

$$
	|x - y| \implies |g(x) - g(y)| < \epsilon
$$

Next, we decompose $[-\pi,\pi]$ into $N\in\N$ open intervals $I_j$ with $\operatorname{length}(I_j) < \delta$. Taking the supremum for each interval $I_j$, i.e. $c_j := \sup\set{g(x)|x\in I_j}$, we can define a simple step function $h(x) = c_j$ for $x\in I_j$. It follows that for $x\in I_j$

$$
	|g(x) - h(x)| = |g(x) - g(y)| < \epsilon ,\; y\in \overline{I}_j
$$

because $|x - y| < \delta$. In total we have

$$
	\norm{ f - h } \leq \norm{ f - g } + \norm{ g - h } = \underbrace{\norm{ f - g }}_{<\epsilon} + \left(\int_{-\pi}^\pi \smash[b]{\underbrace{|g(x) - h(x)|^2}_{<\epsilon}} \;\d x \right)^{1/2} < C\epsilon
$$

where $C\in\R$ is a constant.
</details>
</MathBox>

<MathBox title="Parseval's identity" boxType="theorem" tag="1">
Let $f\in L_{2\pi}^2 (\R,\mathbb{C})$ have complex Fourier series 

$$
	\mathcal{F}_n (f) = \sum_{k=-n}^n \braket{ x\mapsto e^{ikx}, f} e^{ikx} = \sum_{k=-n}^n c_k e^{ikx}
$$

with respect to the inner product

$$
	\braket{ f, g } = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi \overline{f(x)} g(x) \;\d x,\; f,g\in f,g\in L_{2\pi}^2 (\R,\mathbb{C})
$$

Then

$$
	\lim_{n\to\infty} \norm{ f - \mathcal{F}_n (f) }_2 = 0
$$

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>

Let $\epsilon > 0$ and choose $h \in\mathcal{S}_{2\pi} (\R,\mathbb{C})$ with $\norm{ f - h } < \epsilon$. Applying the triangle inequality and Proposition $\ref{proposition-4}$ we get

$$
\begin{align*}
	\norm{ f - \mathbb{F}_n (f) } =& \norm{ f + h - h - \mathcal{F}_n (f) + \mathcal{F}_n (h) - \mathcal{F}_n (h) } \\
	\tleq& \norm{ (f - h) - \mathcal{F}_n (f - h) } + \underbrace{\norm{ h - \mathcal{F}_n (h) }}_{\xrightarrow{n\to\infty 0}} 
\end{align*}
$$

Note that $\mathcal{F}_n (f - h)$ is the orthogonal projection of $(f - h)$ onto $\operatorname{span}(\set{x\mapsto e^{ikx}}_{k\in\Z})$. The Pythagorean theorem implies that

$$
	\norm{ (f - h) - \mathcal{F}_n (f - h) }^2 = \norm{ \mathcal{F}_n (f - h)}^2 + \norm{ f - h }^2
$$

from which it follows that

$$
	\norm{ (f - h) - \mathcal{F}_n (f - h) } \leq \lVert f - h \rVer < \epsilon
$$

Hence, by the sandwhich theorem it follows that

$$
	\lim_{n\to\infty} \norm{ f - \mathcal{F}_n (f) } = 0
$$

## Convergence

<MathBox title="Dirichlet kernel" boxType="definition">
The Dirichlet kernel is a continuous function $\mathcal{D}_n : \R\to\R$, with $n\in\N$, given by

$$
\begin{equation*}
\begin{split}
	\mathcal{D}_n (x) =& \sum_{k=-n}^n e^{ikx} = 1 \ 2\sum_{k=1}^n \cos(kx) \\
	=& \frac{\sin\left(\left(n + \frac{1}{2}\right) x \right)}{\sin\left(\frac{1}{2}x\right)}
\end{split}
\tag{\label{equation-4}}
\end{equation*}
$$

In terms of the Dirichlet kernel, a Fourier series can be rewritten as

$$
\begin{align*}
	\mathcal{F}_n (f)(x) =& \sum_{k=-n}^n c_k e^{ikx} = \sum_{k=-n}^n \left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{-\pi}^\pi e^{-iky} f(y)\;\d y \right) e^{ikx} \\
	=& \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi f(y) \sum_{k=-n}^n e^{ik(x - y)} \;\d y \\
	=& \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi f(y) \mathcal{D}_n (\underbrace{x - y}_{:=z}) \;\d y \\
	=& \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi \mathcal{D}_n (z) f(x - z)  \;\d z \\
	=& \braket{\mathcal{D}_n, f(x - \cdot)} = \frac{1}{2\pi} (\mathcal{D}_n * f)(x) \\
\end{align*}
$$
</MathBox>

<MathBox title="Properties of the Dirichlet kernel" boxType="proposition">
The Dirichlet kernel satisfies the following properties
1. $\mathcal{D}_n$ has exactly $2n$ zeroes inside the interval $[-\pi, \pi]$-
2. $\int_{-\pi}^\pi \mathcal{D}_n (x) \;\d x = 2\pi \implies \braket{D_n,1} = 1$
3. $\lim_{n\to\infty} \int_{-\pi}^\pi |\mathcal{D}_n (x)| \;\d x = \infty$

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>

**(3):**

For all $x > 0$ we have

$$
\begin{align*}
	|\mathcal{D}_n (x)| =& \frac{\left|\sin\left(\left(n + \frac{1}{2}\right) x \right)\right|}{\left|\sin\left(\frac{1}{2}x\right)\right|} \\
	\geq& \frac{\left|\sin\left(\left(n + \frac{1}{2}\right) x \right)\right|}{x}
\end{align*}
$$

such that

$$
\begin{align*}
	\int_{-\pi}^\pi |\mathcal{D}_n (x)| \;\d x =& 2\int_0^\pi |\mathcal{D}_n (x)| \;\d x \geq 2 \int_0^\pi \frac{\left|\sin\left(\left(n + \frac{1}{2}\right) x \right)\right|}{x} \;\d x \\
	=& 2\int_0^{(n + 1/2)n} \frac{|\sin(y)|}{y} \;\d y \geq 2\int_0^{n\pi} \frac{|\sin(y)|}{y} \;\d y \\
	=& 2\sum_{k=1}^n \int_{(k-1)\pi}^{k\pi} \frac{|\sin(y)|}{y} \;\d y \\
	=& 2\sum_{k=1}^n \int_{(k-1)\pi}^{k\pi} \frac{|\sin(y)|}{k\pi} \;\d y \\
	=& 2\sum_{k=1}^n \frac{1}{k\pi} \underbrace{\int_{(k - 1)\pi}^{k\pi} |\sin(y)| \d y}_{=1} \\
	=& \frac{2}{\pi} \sum_{k=1}^n \frac{1}{k} \xrightarrow{n\to\infty} \infty
\end{align*}
$$
</details>
</MathBox>

<MathBox title="Uniform converge of Fourier series" boxType="theorem">
Let $f:\R\to\mathbb{C}$ be a $2\pi$-periodic continuous function that is piecewise $C^1$. That is, there exists a finite partition $a_0 = -\pi < a_1 < \cdots < a_m < a_{m+1} = \pi$ such that $f|_{[a_j, a_{j+1}]} \in C^1$ for all $j\in\set{0,1,\dots,m}$. Then the Fourier series of $f$, denoted $\mathcal{F}_n (f)$, converges uniformly to $f$ on $[-\pi,\pi]$, i.e.  $\mathcal{F}_n (f) \xrightarrow{n\to\infty} f$ uniformly.

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>

Define the derivative function

$$
	\tilde{f}(x) := \begin{cases}
		0,\quad& x\in\set{a_0,a_1,\dots,a_{m+1}} \\
		f'(x),\quad& \text{otherwise}
	\end{cases}
$$

Since $f$ is piecewise $C^1$, it follows that $\tilde{f}$ is a piecewise continuous function in $L_{2\pi}^2 (\R, \mathbb{C})$. By Parseval's identity (Theorem $\ref{theorem-1}$), we have

$$
	\norm{\tilde{f}}^2 = \sum_{k=-\infty}^\infty |\braket{e_k, \tilde{f}}^2 < \infty,\; e_k (x) = e^{ikx}
$$

where the Fourier coefficients of $f$ are

$$
	c_k := \braket{e_k, f} = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi e^{-ikx} f(x) \;\d x,\; k\neq 0
$$

Integrating $c_k$ by parts with $u = f(x)$ and $v = -\frac{1}{ik}e^{-ikx}$ yields

$$
\begin{align*}
	\frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi e^{-ikx} f(x) \;\d x =& \frac{1}{2\pi} \left(\frac{1}{-ik}\left[ e^{-ikx} f(x) \right]_{-\pi}^\pi - \frac{1}{-ik}\int_{-\pi}^\pi e^{-ikx} f'(x) \;\d x \right) \\
	=& \frac{1}{i2\pi k} \int_{-\pi}^\pi e^{-ikx} \tilde{f}(x) \;\d x \\
	=& \frac{1}{ik} \braket{e_k, \tilde{f}}
\end{align*}
$$

Using the inequality $ab \leq (a^2 + b^2) / 2$ for $a,b\in\R$, we obtain

$$
	|c_k| = \frac{1}{k} |\braket{e_k, \tilde{f}}| \leq \frac{1}{2} \left(\frac{1}{k^2} + |\braket{e_k, \tilde{f}}|^2 \right)
$$

Summing over $k\neq 0$, we get

$$
	\sum_{k=-\infty, k\neq 0}^\infty |c_k| \leq \sum_{k=-\infty, k\neq 0}^\infty \frac{1}{k^2} + \sum_{k=-\infty, k\neq 0}^\infty |\braket{e_k, \tilde{f}}|^2 < \infty
$$

since both series on the right converge. For $f_k = c^k e^{ikx}$, it follows that $|f_k (x)| \leq M_k =: |c_k|$ and $\sum_{k=-\infty}^\infty M_k < \infty$. By the Weierstrass M-test $\sum_{k=-\infty}^\infty f_k$ converges uniformly to a continuous function $h:[-\pi,\pi] \to\mathbb{C}$. That is,

$$
	\norm{\mathcal{F}_n (f) - h}_\infty \xrightarrow{n\to\infty} 0
$$

On the other hand, Parseval's identity implies that

$$
	\norm{\mathcal{F}_n (f) - f}_2 \xrightarrow{n\to\infty} 0
$$

By the triangle inequality, it follows that

$$
	\norm{f - h}_2 \leq \norm{f - \mathcal{F}_n (f)}_2 + \norm{\mathcal{F}_n (f) - h}_2 \leq \norm{\mathcal{F}_n (f) - h}_\infty \xrightarrow{n\to\infty} 0
$$

Hence $\norm{f - h}_2 = 0$, and since both $f$ and $h$ are continuous on a compact interval, it follows that $f = h$.
</details>
</MathBox>

<MathBox title="Pointwise convergence of Fourier series" boxType="theorem" tag="2">
Let $f\in L_{2\pi}^2 (\R,\mathbb{C})$, and fix a point $\hat{x} \in[-\pi,\pi]$. Suppose that the left and right limits of $f$ at $\hat{x}$ exists:

$$
\begin{align*}
	f(\hat{x}^-) :=& \lim_{\substack{\epsilon\to 0 \\ \epsilon > 0}} f(\hat{x} - \epsilon) \\
	f(\hat{x}^+) :=& \lim_{\substack{\epsilon\to 0 \\ \epsilon > 0}} f(\hat{x} + \epsilon)
\end{align*}
$$

and that the one-sided derivatives at $\hat{x}$ also exist:

$$
\begin{align*}
	\lim_{\substack{h\uparrow 0}} \frac{f(\hat{x} + h) - f(\hat{x})}{h} \\
	\lim_{\substack{h\downarrow 0}} \frac{f(\hat{x} + h) - f(\hat{x})}{h} \\ 
\end{align*}
$$

Then the partial sums $\mathcal{F}_n (f)(\hat{x})$ of the Fourier series of $f$ converge pointwise at $\hat{x}$ to the arithmetic mean of the left and right limits:

$$
	\mathcal{F}_n (f) (\hat{x}) \xrightarrow{n\to\infty} \frac{1}{2}\left(f(\hat{x}^+) f(\hat{x}^-)\right) =: M
$$

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>

Using the Dirichlet kernel $\mathcal{D}_n$ given in $\eqref{equation-4}$ we write $\mathcal{F}_n (f) (\hat{x}) = \braket{\mathcal{D}_n, f(\hat{x} - \cdot)}$ and $\braket{\mathcal{D}_n, M} = M$. By symmetry of $\mathcal{D}_n$, we obtain

$$
\begin{align*}
	\braket{\mathcal{D}_n, f(\hat{x} - \cdot)} =& \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi \mathcal{D}_n (x) f(\hat{x} - x) \;\d x \\
	=& \frac{1}{2\pi} \left(\int_{-\pi}^0 \mathcal{D}_n (x) f(\hat{x} - x) \;\d x + \int_0^\pi \mathcal{D}_n (x) f(\hat{x} - x) \;\d x \right)
	=& \frac{1}{2\pi} \left(\int_0^\pi \mathcal{D}_n (y) f(\hat{x} + y) \;\d y + \int_0^\pi \mathcal{D}_n (x) f(\hat{x} - y) \;\d y \right) \\
	=& \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_0^\pi \mathcal{D}_n (y) (f(\hat{x} + y) + f(\hat{x} - y)) \;\d y
\end{align*}
$$

The pointwise limit is given by

$$
\begin{align*}
	\mathcal{F}_n (f)(\hat{x}) - M =& \braket{\mathcal{D}_n, f(\hat{x} - \cdot)} - \braket{\mathcal{D}_n, M} \\
	=& \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_0^\pi \mathcal{D}_n (y) (f(\hat{x} + y) + f(\hat{x} - y)) \;\d y - \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_0^\pi \mathcal{D}_n (y) \underbrace{2M}_{f(\hat{x}_+) + f(\hat{x}_-)} \;\d y \\
	=& \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_0^\pi \mathcal{D}_n (y) (f(\hat{x} + y) - f(\hat{x}_-) + f(\hat{x} - y) - f(\hat{x}_-)) \;\d y
	=& \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_0^\pi \sin\left(\left(n + \frac{1}{2}\right)x \right) \underbrace{\frac{f(\hat{x} + y) - f(\hat{x}_-) + f(\hat{x} - y) - f(\hat{x}_-)}{\sin\left(\frac{1}{2}y \right)}}_{g(y)} \;\d y
\end{align*}
$$

To show that $g \in \mathcal{L}_{2\pi}^2$, we write

$$
	g(y) = \begin{cases}
		\frac{f(\hat{x} + y) - f(\hat{x}_-)}{\sin\left(\frac{1}{2}y \right)} + \frac{f(\hat{x} - y) - f(\hat{x}_-)}{\sin\left(\frac{1}{2}y \right)},\quad& y\in(0,\pi) \\
		0,\quad& y\in [-\pi, 0]
	\end{cases}
$$

Since $\sin\left(y/2 \right) \geq y/4$ for $y\in(0,\pi)$, we get

$$
	\left|\frac{f(\hat{x} \pm y) - f(\hat{x}_\pm)}{\sin\left(\frac{1}{2}y \right)} \right| \leq 4\left| \frac{f(\hat{x} \pm y) - f(\hat{x}_\pm)}{y} \right| \xrightarrow{y\to 0_\pm} 4 |C_\pm|
$$

which follows because

$$
\begin{align*}
	\lim_{\substack{h\to 0 \\ h > 0}} \frac{f(\hat{x} + h) - f(\hat{x})}{h} :=& C_+
	\lim_{\substack{h\to 0 \\ h < 0}} \frac{f(\hat{x} + h) - f(\hat{x})}{h} :=& C_-
\end{align*}
$$

Hence $g \in L_{2\pi}^2$.

Using Euler's formula we rewrite 

$$
\begin{align*}
	frac{1}{2\pi} \int_0^\pi \sin\left(\left(n + \frac{1}{2}\right)x \right) =& \frac{1}{2i} (e^{i(n + 1/2)y} - e^{-i(n + 1/2)y} e^{-iy/2}) \\
	=& \frac{1}{2i} (e^{iny} e^{iy/2} - e^{-iny} e^{-iy/2})
\end{align*}
$$

Since $g\in\mathcal{L}_{2\pi}^2$, we get by Bessel's inequality that

$$
	\frac{1}{2\pi} \int_0^\pi \sin\left(\left(n + \frac{1}{2}\right)x \right) g(y) \;\d y = \braket{e_{-n}, g_1} + \braket{e_{-n}, g_2} \xrightarrow{n\to\infty} 0
$$
</details>
</MathBox>

<MathBox title="" boxType="example">
Consider $2\pi$-periodic function $f:\R\to\R$ defined by

$$
	f(x) = \begin{cases}
		0,\quad& x\in[-\pi,0) \\
		\pi - x,\quad& x\in[0,\pi]
	\end{cases}
$$

The complex Fourier coefficients of $f$ are 

$$
\begin{align*}
	c_k :=& \braket{e_k, f} = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_0^\pi e^{-ikx} (\pi - x) \;\d x \\
	=& \begin{cases}
		\frac{\pi}{4},\quad& k=0 \\
		\frac{\pi}{2\pi} \left(-\frac{1}{k^2} [(-1)^k - 1] - i\frac{\pi}{k} \right),\quad& k\neq 0
	\end{cases}
\end{align*}
$$

while the real coefficients are

$$
\begin{align*}
	a_k =& c_k + c_{-k} = \frac{1}{\pi} \frac{1}{k^2} [1 - (-1)^k] \\
	b_k =& i(c_k - c_{-k}) = \frac{1}{k}
\end{align*}
$$

By Theorem $\ref{theorem-2}$, we have $\mathcal{F}_n (f)(0) \xrightarrow{n\to\infty} \frac{\pi}{2}$, giving

$$
	\frac{\pi}{2} =& \frac{\pi}{4} + \sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{1}{\pi} \frac{1 - (-1)^k}{k^2} \\
	\implies \frac{\pi^2}{4} =& \sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{1 - (-1)^k}{k^2} 
$$

### Gibbs phenomenon

<MathBox title="Overshoot estimate of the step function" boxType="example">
Consider the $2\pi$-periodic function $f:\R\to\mathbb{C}$ defined by

$$
  f(x) = \begin{cases}
    1,\quad& x\in (-\pi, 0) \\
    0,\quad& x\in [0,\pi]
  \end{cases}
$$

The Fourier series of $f$ is given by

$$
  \mathcal{F}_n (f) (x) = \frac{1}{2} + \sum_{\substack{k=1 \\ k\text{ odd}}}^n \left(-\frac{2}{\pi} \right) \frac{1}{k} \sin(kx)
$$

To estimate the overshot of $\mathcal{F}_n (f)$ near the jump discontinuity at $x = 0$, we analyze the partial sum

$$
  S_n (x) := \sum_{\substack{k=1 \\ k\text{ odd}}}^n \frac{\sin(kx)}{k}
$$

Using

$$
  \frac{\sin(kx)}{k} = \int_0^x \cos(kt)\;\d t
$$

we write

$$
\begin{equation*}
  S_n (x) = \int_0^x \sum_{\substack{k=1 \\ k\text{ odd}}}^n \cos(kt)\;\d t
\tag{\label{equation-5}}
\end{equation*}
$$

The cosine sum can be evaluated explicitly:

$$
\begin{align*}
  \sum_{\substack{k=1 \\ k\text{ odd}}}^n \cos(kt) =& \frac{1}{2} \sum_{\substack{k= -n \\ k\text{ odd}}}^n e^{ikt} = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{\substack{m=0 \\ m\text{ even}}}^{2n} e^{i(m-n)t} \\
  =& \frac{1}{2} e^{-int} \sum_{\substack{m=0 \\ m\text{ even}}}^{2n} e^{imt} \\
  =& \frac{1}{2} e^{-int} \sum_{l=0}^n (\underbrace{e^{i2t}}_{=q})^l \\
  =& \frac{1}{2} e^{-int} \frac{1 - q^{n+1}}{1 - q} \\
  =& \frac{1}{2} \frac{\sin[(n+1) t]}{\sin(t)},\; t \notin \pi\Z \tag{\label{equation-6}}
\end{align*}
$$

Plugging $\eqref{equation-6}$ into $\eqref{equation-5}$ yields

$$
  S_n (x) = \frac{1}{2} \int_0^x \frac{\sin[(n+1)t]}{\sin(t)}\;\d t
$$

For small $t > 0$, we use the inequality $\sin(t) \leq t$, which implies

$$
  \frac{1}{\sin(t)} \geq \frac{1}{t}
$$

Thus,

$$
  S_n (x) \geq \frac{1}{2} \int_0^x \frac{\sin[\overbrace{(n+1)t}^{y}]}{t} \;\d t
$$

Substituting $y = (n+1)t$ gives

$$
\begin{equation*}
  S_n (x) \geq \frac{1}{2} \int_0^{(n+1)x} \frac{\sin(y)}{y} \;\d y = \frac{1}{2} \operatorname{Si}[(n+1)x]
\tag{\label{equation-7}}
\end{equation*}
$$

To probe the behaviour of $\mathcal{F}_n (f)$ at $x = 0$, we set $x = \pi/(n+1)$. Using the bound $\eqref{equation-7}$, we finally get

$$
\begin{align*}
  \mathcal{F}_n (f)\left(\frac{\pi}{n+1} \right) =& \frac{1}{2} - \frac{2}{\pi} S_n \left(\frac{k\pi}{n+1} \right) \\
  \leq \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{\pi} \operatorname{Si}(\pi) \approx -0.089
\end{align*}
$$
</MathBox>

## Fourier series in $L^1$

To extend Fourier series to integrable functions $\mathcal{L}_{2\pi}^1$, we note that $\mathcal{L}_{2\pi}^2 (\R, \mathbb{C}) \subseteq \mathcal{L}_{\2pi}^1 (\R, \mathbb{C})$.

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>

Let $f\in \mathca{L}_{2\pi}^2$. Using the inner product structure $\eqref{equation-8}$ and applying the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality, we obtain

$$
  \left|\frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi |f(x)| \;\d x \right| = |\braket{|f|, 1}| \leq \norm{f}_{L^2} \cdot \norm{1}_{L^2}
$$

Since

$$
  \norm{1}_{L^2} = \left(\frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi 1 \;\d x \right)^{1/2} = 1
$$

we get

$$
\begin{align*}
  \norm{f}_{L^1} =& \frac{1}{2} \int_{-\pi}^\pi |f(x)|\;\d x \\
  \leq& \norm{f}_{L^2} < \infty
\end{align*}
$$

Hence, $f \in L_{2\pi}^1$.
</details>

Let $e_k (x) := e^{ikx}$ for $k\in\Z$. For any $f\in L_{2\pi}^1 (\R,\mathbb{C})$, define the Fourier coefficients by

$$
  \hat{f}(k) = \braket{e_k, f} := \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi e^{-ikx} f(x) \;\d x
$$

Since $|e^{-ikx} f(x)| = |f(x)|$, this integral exists. Hence

$$
  \mathcal{F}_n (f) = \sum_{k=-n}^n e_k \braket{e_k, f}
$$

is well defined for $f\in L_{2\pi}^1$.

<MathBox title="Riemann-Lebesgue lemma" boxType="proposition">
If $f\in \mathcal{L}_{2\pi}^1 (\R, \mathbb{C})$ is $2\pi$-periodic integrable function, its Fourier coefficients

$$
  \hat{f}(k) := \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi e^{-ikx} f(x) \;\d x,\; k\in\Z
$$

satisfy

$$
  \lim_{|k|\to\infty} \hat{f}(k) = 0
$$

Equivalently, the Fourier transform defines a map $\hat{\cdot}: \mathcal{L}_{2\pi}^1 (\R, \mathbb{C}) \to c_0 (\Z, \mathbb{C})$, where

$$
  c_0 (\Z, \mathbb{C}) := \Set{(a_k)_{k\in\Z} : a_k \in \mathbb{C}, \lim_{|k|\to\infty} a_k = 0}
$$

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>

If $f\in \mathcal{L}_{2\pi}^2$, then by Parseval's identity

$$
  \sum_{k\in\Z} |\hat{f}(k)|^2 = \norm{f}_{L^2}^2 < \infty
$$

so $(\hat{f}(k))_{k\in\Z} \in \ell^2 (\Z, \mathbb{C})$, and in particular $\lim_{|k|\to\infty} \hat{f}(k) = 0$.

Take $f\in L_{2\pi}^1 (\R, \mathbb{C})$ and for $N\in\N_0$ define the truncations $f_N : \R\to\mathbb{C}$ by

$$
 f_N (x) := \begin{cases}
  f(x),\quad& |f(x)| \leq N \\
  N,\quad& |f(x)| > N
 \end{cases}
$$

Since $|f_N (x)| \leq N$ for any $N\in\N$, it follows that $f_N L^\infty \subset \in L_{2\pi}^2$. Applying Lebesgue's dominated convergence theorem, we have $|f_N (x)| \overset{\text{a.e.}}{\leq} |f(x)|$ for $x\in\R$ and $\lim_{N\to\infty} f_N (x) \eqae f(x)$ for $x\in\R$. This implies

$$
  \lim_{N\to\infty} \int_{-\pi}^\pi |f_N (x) - f(x)|\;\d x = 0
$$

and for all $k\in\Z$, we obtain

$$
\begin{align*}
  |\hat{f}_N (k) - \hat{f}(k)| = \left|\frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi e^{-ikx} (f_N(x) - f(x)) \right| \\
  \tleq& \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi |f_N (x) - f(x)|\;\d x \xrightarrow{N\to\infty} 0
\end{align*}
$$

Fix $\epsilon > 0$. Since $f_N \in L_{2\pi}^2$, there exists $K\in\N$ such that

$$
  |k| \geq K \implies |\hat{f}_N (k)| < \frac{\epsilon}{2}
$$

Choose $N$ large enough so that

$$
  |f_N (x) - f(x)| < \frac{\epsilon}{2}
$$

Then for all $k\in\Z$

$$
  |\hat{f}_N (k) - \hat{f}(k)| < \frac{\epsilon}{2}
$$

Hence, for all $|k| \geq K$

$$
\begin{align*}
  |\hat{f}(k)| =& |\hat{f}(k) - \hat{f}_N (k) + \hat{f}_N (k)| \\
  \tleq& |\hat{f}(k) - \hat{f}_N (k)| + |\hat{f}_N (k)| \\
  <& \frac{\epsilon}{2} + \frac{\epsilon}{2} = \epsilon
\end{align*}
$$

This implies $\lim_{|k|\to\infty} \hat{f}(k) = 0$.
</details>
</MathBox>

# Fourier transform

The Fourier transform integral is the limit of Fourier series as the periodicity goes to infinity. Consider a Fourier series of a function $f \in L^2\left([-L/2, L/2] \right)$

$$
\begin{gather*}
	f(x) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^\infty c_n e^{\frac{i2\pi n x}{L}} \\
	c_n = \frac{1}{L}\braket{ f(x), e_n } = \frac{1}{L}\int_{-L/2}^{L/2} f(x)e^{-\frac{i2\pi n x}{L}}\;\d x
\end{gather*}
$$

Defining $\xi = \frac{n}{L}$ and $\Delta\xi = \frac{1}{L}$. Taking the limit $L\to\infty \iff \Delta\xi\to 0$ we get

$$
\begin{align*}
	f(x) =& \lim_{\Delta\xi \to 0} \sum_{n=-\infty}^\infty \Delta\xi \int_{-\frac{1}{2\xi}}^{\frac{1}{2\xi}} f(\xi) e^{-i2\pi \xi x}\;\d\xi e^{i2\pi n \Delta\xi x} \\
	=& \lim_{\Delta\xi\to 0}\sum_{n=-\infty}^\infty \Delta\xi \braket{ f(x), e^{i2\pi \xi x} } e^{i 2\pi n \Delta\xi x}
\end{align*}
$$

Defining $\omega = \frac{n\pi}{L}$ and $\Delta\omega = \frac{\pi}{L}$. Taking the limit $L\to\infty$ or $\Delta\omega\to 0$ we get

$$
\begin{align*}
	f(x) =& \lim_{\Delta\omega\to 0} \sum_{n=-\infty}^\infty \frac{\Delta\omega}{2\pi} \int_{-\frac{\pi}{\Delta\omega}}^{\frac{\pi}{\Delta\omega}} f(\xi) e^{-i\omega\xi}\;\d\xi e^{in\Delta\omega x} \\
	=& \lim_{\Delta\omega\to 0}\sum_{n=-\infty}^\infty \frac{\Delta\omega}{2\pi}\braket{ f(x), e_i\omega x } e^{in\Delta\omega x}
\end{align*}
$$

In this limit, the summation with weight $\Delta\xi$ becomes a Riemann integral. The Fourier transform of $f$, denoted $\mathcal{F}\left[ f(x) \right] = \hat{f}(\omega)$ is defined as the inner product $\braket{f(x), e^{i2\pi\xi x}}$ resulting in

$$
\begin{align*}
	f(x) &= \mathcal{F}^{-1}\left[ \hat{f}(\xi) \right] = \int_{-\infty}^\infty \hat{f}(\xi) e^{i2\pi\xi x}\;\d\xi \\
	\hat{f}(\xi) &= \mathcal{F}\left[ f(x) \right] = \int_{-\infty}^\infty f(x) e^{-i2\pi\xi x}\;\d x
\end{align*}
$$

Both integrals converge as long as $f$ and $\hat{f}$ are integrable, i.e. $f, \hat{f}\in L^1 (-\infty, \infty)$. When the independent variable $x$ represents time, denoted $t\in\R$, the transform variable $\xi$ represents frequency, denoted $f\in\R$. In terms of angular frequency $\omega = 2\pi f$, the Fourier transform takes the form

$$
	\hat{f}(\omega) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x) e^{-i \omega x}\;\d x \quad \forall \omega\in\R
$$

<MathBox title='Fourier transforms' boxType="definition">
The Fourier transform of an integrable function $f\in L^1 (\R^n, \mathbb{C})$, is a function $\hat{f}:\R^n \to\mathbb{C}$ defined by

$$
\begin{equation*}
  \hat{f}(\boldsymbol{\omega}) := \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}} \int_{\R^n} e^{-i\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\mathbf{x}} f(\mathbf{x})\;\d\mathbf{x}
\tag{\label{equation-9}}
\end{equation*}
$$
</MathBox>

The Fourier transform of $f\in L^1 (\R^n, \mathbb{C})$, denoted $\hat{f}$, is a continous bounded function, i.e. $\hat{f} \in C_\text{b} (\R^n, \mathbb{C})$.

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>

Taking the supremum norm of $\hat{f}$ gives

$$
\begin{align*}
  \norm{\hat{f}}_\infty =& \sup_{\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\R^n} |\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{\omega})| \\
  =& \sup_{\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\R^n} \left|\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}} \int_{\R^n} e^{-i\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\mathbf{x}} f(\mathbf{x})\;\d\mathbf{x} \right| \\
  \tleq& \sup_{\boldsymbol{\omega}\in\R^n} \int_{\R^n} |e^{-i\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\mathbf{x}} f(\mathbf{x})|\;\d\mathbf{x} \\
  =& \int_{\R^n} |f(\mathbf{x})|\;\d\mathbf{x} = \norm{f}_{L^1} < \infty
\end{align*}
$$

showing that $\hat{f}$ is bounded.

To show that $\hat{f}$ is continuous, consider a sequence $(\boldsymbol{\omega}_k) \subset \R^n$ with $\boldsymbol{\omega}_k \xrightarrow{k\to\infty} \boldsymbol{\omega}$ and define

$$
  g_k (\mathbf{x}) := \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}} e^{-i\boldsymbol{\omega}_k \cdot\mathbf{x}} \xrightarrow{k\to\infty} \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}} e^{-i\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\mathbf{x}} =: g(\mathbf{x})
$$

and $|g_k (\mathbf{x}) f(\mathbf{x})| \overset{\text{a.e.}}{\leq} |f(\mathbf{x})|$ for all $\mathbf{x}\in\R^n$. Then by Lebesgue's dominated convergence theorem

$$
  \hat{f}(\boldsymbol{\omega}_k) = \int_{\R^n} g_k (\mathbf{x}) f(\mathbf{x}) \;\d\mathbf{x} \xrightarrow{k\to\infty} \int_{\R^n} g(\mathbf{x}) f(\mathbf{x})\;\d\mathbf{x} = \hat{f}(\boldsymbol{\omega})
$$
</details>

<MathBox title="Riemann-Lebesgue lemma (continuous version)" boxType="theorem">
If $f \in L^1 (\R^n, \mathbb{C})$, then

$$
  \lim_{\norm{\mathbf{p}}\to\infty} \hat{f}(\mathbf{p}) = 0
$$

Equivalently, for every sequence $(\mathbf{p}_k)_{k\in\N} \subseteq \R^n$ with $\norm{\mathbf{p}_k} \xrightarrow{k\to\infty} \infty$, then 

$$
  \lim_{k\to\infty} \hat{f}(\mathbf{p}_k) = 0
$$

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>

Assume first that $f \in C_c^\infty (\R^n, \mathbb{C})$ is a smooth function with compact support. Since $f$ has compact support, there exists $R > 0$ such that $\operatorname{supp}(f) \subseteq \mathcal{B}_R (\mathbf{0})$. It follows that each partial derivative $\partial_j f \in C_c^\infty (\R^n, \mathbb{C})$ also has compact support. Taking the Fourier transform of $\partial_j f$ and integrating by parts over $B_{R+1} (\mathbf{0})$ yields

$$
\begin{align*}
  \widehat{\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_j}}(p) =& \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}} \int_{\mathcal{B}_{R+1} (\mathbf{0})} e^{-i \mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{x}} \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_j}(\mathbf{x}) \;\d\mathbf{x} \\
  =& \underbrace{\int_{\partial \mathcal{B}_{R+1} (\mathbf{0})} e^{-i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{x}} f(\mathbf{x}) n_j (\mathbf{x}) \;\d S(\mathbf{x})}_{=0} \\
  &- \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}} \int_{B_{R+1}(\mathbf{0})} \frac{\partial}{\partial x_j} e^{-i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{x}} f(\mathbf{x}) \;\d\mathbf{x} \\
  =& -\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}} \int_{B_{R+1}(\mathbf{0})} (-i p_j) e^{-i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{x}} f(\mathbf{x}) \;\d\mathbf{x} \\
  =& i p_j \hat{f}(p)
\end{align*}
$$

where $\unitvec{n} = (n_i,\dots,n_j)$ is the outward unit normal.

Thus, by the triangle inequality for integrals

$$
\begin{align*}
  |p_j| \cdot |\hat{f}(\mathbf{p})| =& | \widehat{\partial_j f} | \\
  \leq& \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}} \norm{\partial_i f}_{L^1} =: C_j
\end{align*}
$$

Now let

$$
  \max_{1 \leq j \leq n} |p_j| \geq \frac{\norm{\mathbf{p}}}{\sqrt{n}}
$$

there exists and index $j\in\set{1,\dots,n}$ such that

$$
  |p_j| \geq \frac{\norm{\mathbf{p}}}{\sqrt{n}}
$$

Hence

$$
  |\hat{f}(\mathbf{p})| \leq \frac{C}{p_j} \leq \frac{\sqrt{n} C}{\norm{\mathbf{p}}} \xrightarrow{\norm{\mathbf{p}}\to\infty} 0
$$

Now let $f \in L^1 (\R^n, \mathbb{C})$. Since $C_c^\infty (\R^n, \mathbb{C})$ is dense in $L^1 (\R^n, \mathbb{C})$, for every $\epsilon > 0$ there exists $g\in C_c^\infty (\R^n, \mathbb{C})$ such that 

$$
  \norm{f - g}_{L^1} = \int_{\R^n} |f(\mathbf{x}) - g(\mathbf{x})| \;\d\mathbf{x} < \epsilon
$$

Then for every $\mathbf{p}\in\R^n$

$$
\begin{align*}
  |\hat{f}(\mathbf{p}) - \hat{g}(\mathbf{p})| =& \left| \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}} \int_{\R^n} e^{-i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{x}} (f(\mathbf{x}) - g(\mathbf{x})) \;\d\mathbf{x} \right| \\
  \leq& \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}} \norm{f - g}_{L^1} < \frac{\epsilon}{(2\pi)^{n/2}}
\end{align*}
$$

Since we have established that $\hat{g}(\mathbf{p}) \xrightarrow{\norm{\mathbf{p}} \to\infty} 0$, there exists $M > 0$ such that

$$
  \norm{\mathbf{p}} > M \implies |\hat{g}(\mathbf{p})| < \frac{\varepsilon}{(2\pi)^{n/2}}
$$

Applying the triangle inequality for $\norm{\mathbf{p}} > M$, we obtain

$$
\begin{align*}
  |\hat{f}(\mathbf{p})| \tleq& |\hat{f}(\mathbf{p}) - \hat{g}(\mathbf{p})| + |\hat{g}(\mathbf{p})| \\
  <& \frac{2\epsilon}{(2\pi)^{n/2}}
\end{align*}
$$

Since $\epsilon > 0$ is arbitrary, it follows that that

$$
  \lim_{\norm{\mathbf{p}}\to\infty} \hat{f}(\mathbf{p}) = 0
$$
</details>
</MathBox>

The Lebesgue-Riemann lemma implies that the Fourier transform defines a linear map $\mathcal{F}: L^1 (\R^n, \mathbb{C}) \to C_0 (\R^n, \mathbb{C})$.

<MathBox title="Schwartz space" boxType="definition">
The Schwartz space on $\R^n$, denoted $\mathcal{S}(\R^n, \mathbb{C})$ is the space of all infinitely differentiable functions whose derivatives decay faster than any inverse polynomial at infinity. It is defined by

$$
	\mathcal{S}\left(\R^n, \mathbb{C}\right) := \Set{ f\in C^\infty (\R^n, \mathbb{C}) : \sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\R^n} \left| \mathbf{x}^\beta D^\alpha f(\mathbf{x}) \right| < \infty,\, \forall \alpha,\beta \in\N_0^n}
$$

where, for multi-indices $\alpha = (\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n)$ and $\beta = (\beta_1,\dots,\beta_n)$, we write $\mathbf{x}^\beta = x_1^{\beta_1} \cdots x_n^{\beta_n}$ and

$$
  D^\alpha = \frac{\partial^{|\alpha|}}{\partial x_1^{\alpha_1} \cdots \partial x_n^{\alpha_n}}
$$

with $|\alpha| = \alpha_1 + \cdots + \alpha_n$.

Equivalently, using the Euclidean norm $\norm{\mathbf{x}}$

$$
	\mathcal{S}\left(\R^n, \mathbb{C}\right) := \Set{ f\in C^\infty (\R^n, \mathbb{C}) : \sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\R^n} \left| \norm{\mathbf{x}}^{2N} \mathcal{D}^\alpha f(\mathbf{x}) \right| < \infty,\, \forall \alpha\in\N_0^n, N\in\N_0}
$$

Thus, for every multi-index $\alpha$ and every $N \geq 0$

$$
  D^\alpha f(\mathbf{x}) = O(\norm{\mathbf{x}}^{-N}),\, \norm{\mathbf{x}} \to \infty
$$

In other words, every derivative of a Schwartz function decays faster than any prescribed inverse power of $\norm{\mathbf{x}}$. In particular, $f$ itself is rapidly decreasing. 

Because Schwartz functions have arbitrarily high polynomial decay, they belong to every $L^p$ space, $\mathcal{S}(\R^n, \mathbb{C}) \subsetneq L^p (\R^n, \mathbb{C})$ for $1\leq p \leq\infty$.

The Fourier transform defines an automorphism on Schwartz spaces, i.e. $f\in\mathcal{S}\left(\R^n, \mathbb{C}\right) \implies \hat{f}\in \mathcal{S}\left(\R^n, \mathbb{C}\right)$
</MathBox>

Compactly supported functions form a subsspace of the Schwartz space, i.e. $C_c^\infty (\R^n, \mathbb{C}) \subseteq \mathcal{S}(\R^n, \mathbb{C})$. Let $f\in C_c^\infty (\R^n, \mathbb{C})$. Since $\operatorname{supp}(f)$ is compact, there exists $R > 0$ such that

$$
  \operatorname{supp}(f) \subseteq \overline{B_R (0)} = \set{\mathbf{x}\in\R^n : \norm{\mathbf{x}} \leq R}
$$

Because $f$ is identically zero outside its support, all of its derivatives also vanish in this region. Thus, for every pair of multi-indices $\alpha,\beta \in\N_0^n$

$$
  \mathbf{x}^\beta D^\alpha f(\mathbf{x}) = 0,\, \mathbf{x} \notin \operatorname{supp}(f)
$$

On the compact set $\operatorname{supp}(f)$, the function $\mathbf{x}^\beta D^\alpha f(\mathbf{x})$ is continuous and therefore bounded. Consequently,

$$
  \sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\R^n} |\mathbf{x}^\beta D^\alpha f(\mathbf{x})| < \infty,\, \forall \alpha, \beta \in\N_0^n
$$

This is precisely the defining condition for $f$ to belong to the Schwartz space.

## Properties

<MathBox title="Linearity of Fourier transforms" boxType="proposition">
The Fourier transform is a linear operator, so that for $f, g\in L^1 (\R^n, \mathbb{C})$ and $\alpha, \beta\in\mathbb{C}$, we have

$$
\begin{gather*}
	\mathcal{F}\left[\alpha f(x) + \beta g(x) \right] = \alpha\mathcal{F}\left[ f(x) \right] + \beta\mathcal{F}\left[g(x) \right] \\
	\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left[\alpha \hat{f}(x) + \beta \hat{g}(x) \right] = \alpha\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left[ \hat{f}(x) \right] + \beta\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left[ \hat{g}(x) \right] \\
\end{gather*}
$$
</MathBox>

<MathBox title="Fourier shifting property" boxType="proposition">
Let $f \in L^1 (\R^n, \mathbb{C})$ be an integrable function. For $\mathbf{a}\in\R^n$, define the translation and modulation operators respectively by

$$
  (T_\mathbf{a} f)(\mathbf{x}) := f(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{a})
$$

and 

$$
  (M_\mathbf{a} f)(\mathbf{x}) := e^{-i\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{a}} f(\mathbf{x})
$$

The Fourier transform $\mathcal{F}: L^1 (\R^n, \mathbb{C}) \to C_0 (\mathbf{R}^n, \mathbb{C})$ satisfies the following properties concerning translations and modulations.

1. **Translation in physical space:** 

Translation of $f$ by $\mathbf{a}\in\R^n$ corresponds to modulation of its Fourier transform

$$
  \mathcal{F}[T_\mathbf{a} f](\boldsymbol{\omega}) = e^{-i\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\mathbf{a}} \hat{f}(\boldsymbol{\omega}) = (M_a \hat{f})(\boldsymbol{\omega})
$$

In particular

$$
  |\mathcal{F}[T_\mathbf{a}](\boldsymbol{\omega})| = |\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{\omega})|
$$

2. **Modulation in physical space:**

Modulation of $f$ by $-\mathbf{b}$ corresponds to translation of its Fourier transform in the opposite direction

$$
  \mathcal{F}[M_\mathbf{b} f](\boldsymbol{\omega}) = \hat{f}(\boldsymbol{\omega} + \mathbf{b}) = T_{-\mathbf{b}} \hat{f}(\boldsymbol{\omega})
$$

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>

**(1):** By definition $\eqref{equation-9}$, we have

$$
  \mathcal{F}\left[ f(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{a}) \right] = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}} \int_{\R^n} e^{-i\operatorname{\omega} \cdot \mathbf{x}} f(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{a}) \;\d\mathbf{x}
$$

Changing variables $\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{x} - \mathbf{a}$ with $\d\mathbf{x} = \d\mathbf{y}$, we obtain

$$
  \mathcal{F}\left[ f(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{a}) \right] =& \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}} \int_{\R^n} e^{-i\operatorname{\omega} \cdot (\mathbf{y} + \mathbf{a})} f(\mathbf{y}) \;\d\mathbf{y} \\
  =& \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}} e^{-i\boldsymbol{\omega} \cdot\mathbf{a}} \int_{\R^n} e^{-i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{y}} f(\mathbf{y}) \d\mathbf{y} \\
  \underbrace{=}_{\eqref{equation-9}}& e^{-i\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\mathbf{a}}
$$

Similarly, 

$$
\begin{align*}
  \mathcal{F}[e^{-i \mathbf{b})\cdot \mathbf{x}} f(\mathbf{x})] =& \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}} \int_{\R^n} e^{-i\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\mathbf{x}} e^{-i\mathbf{b}\cdot\mathbf{x}} f(\mathbf{x}) \;\d\mathbf{x} \\
  =& \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}} \int_{\R^n} e^{-i(\boldsymbol{\omega} + \mathbf{b}) \cdot\mathbf{x}} f(\mathbf{x}) \;\d\mathbf{x} \\
  \underbrace{=}_{\eqref{equation-9}}& \hat{f}(\boldsymbol{\omega} + \mathbf{b})
\end{align*}
$$
</details>
</MathBox>

<MathBox title="Fourier differentiation property" boxType="proposition">
Let $f \in\mathcal{S}(\R^n, \mathbb{C})$ be a Schwartz function. For $j = 1,\dots,n$, define the shorthand $\partial_j := \frac{\partial}{\partial x_j}$ and the component multiplication operator $(M_j f)(\mathbf{x}) = x_j f(\mathbf{x})$. The Fourier transform $\mathcal{F}: \mathcal{S}(\R^n, \mathbb{C}) \to S(\R^n, \mathbb{C})$ satisfies the following differentiation properties

1.
$$
\begin{equation*}
 \mathcal{F}[\partial_j f] = i M_j \hat{f}
\tag{\label{equation-11}}
\end{equation*}
$$

2.
$$
\begin{equation*}
  \partial_j \mathcal{F} [f] = \mathcal{F}(-iM_j f)
\tag{\label{equation-12}}
\end{equation*}
$$

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>

**(1):** Using $\eqref{equation-9}$

$$
  \mathcal{F}[\partial_j f](\boldsymbol{\omega}) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}} \int_{\R^n} e^{-i\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\mathbf{x}} \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_j} (\mathbf{x}) \;\d\mathbf{x} 
$$

Since $f$ is a Schwartz function, it follows that $\partial_j f \in L^1 (\R^n, \mathbb{C}^n)$, so that

$$
\begin{equation*}
  \int_{\R^n} e^{-i\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\mathbf{x}} \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_j} (\mathbf{x}) \;\d\mathbf{x} = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}} \lim_{R\to\infty} \int_{\mathcal{B}_R (0)} e^{-i\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\mathbf{x}} \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_j} (\mathbf{x}) \;\d\mathbf{x}
\tag{\label{equation-10}}
\end{equation*}
$$

For fixed $R$, integration by parts on $B_R (0)$ gives

 $$
\begin{align*}
  \int_{\mathcal{B}_R (0)} e^{-i\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\mathbf{x}} \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_j} (\mathbf{x}) \;\d\mathbf{x} =& \int_{\partial B_R (0)} e^{-i\operatorname{\omega}\cdot\mathbf{x}} f(\mathbf{x}) \nu_j (\mathbf{x}) \;\d\sigma(\mathbf{x}) \\
  -& \int_{B_R (0)} \frac{\partial e^{-i\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\mathbf{x}}}{\partial x_j} f(\mathbf{x}) \;\d\mathbf{x}
\end{align*}
$$

where $\boldsymbol{\nu}$ denotes the outward unit normal to $\partial B_R$ and $\sigma(\mathbf{x})$ is the surface element at $\mathbf{x}$.

Applying the triangle inequality for integrals on the boundary term

$$
\begin{align*}
  \left| \int_{\partial B_R (0)} e^{-i\operatorname{\omega}\cdot\mathbf{x}} f(\mathbf{x}) \unitvec{n}_j (\mathbf{x}) \;\d\sigma(\mathbf{x}) \right| \leq& \int_{\partial B_R (0)} \underbrace{|e^{-i\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\mathbf{x}}|}_{=1} \cdot |f(\mathbf{x})| \cdot \underbrace{|\unitvec{n}_j (\mathbf{x})|}_{\leq 1} \;\d\sigma(\mathbf{x}) \\
  =& \int_{\partial B_R (0)} |f(\mathbf{x})| \;\d\sigma(\mathbf{x})
\end{align*}
$$

Because $f \in S(\R^n)$, for every $N > 0$, there exists $C_N > 0$ such that

$$
  |f(\mathbf{x})| \leq \frac{C_N}{(1 + |\mathbf{x}|)^N}
$$

On $\partial B_R$, we have $|\mathbf{x}| = R$, and therefore

$$
  \int_{\partial B_R (0)} |f(\mathbf{x})| \;\d\sigma(\mathbf{x}) \leq \frac{C_N}{(1 + R)^N} |\mathbb{S}^{n-1}| R^{n-1}
$$

Choosing $N > n - 1$, we obtain

$$
  \lim_{R\to\infty} \frac{R^{n-1}}{(1 + R)^N} = 0
$$

Thus, in the limit $R\to\infty$, the boundary term vanishes and the integral $\eqref{equation-10}$ reduces to

$$
\begin{align}
  \int_{\mathcal{B}_R (0)} e^{-i\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\mathbf{x}} \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_j} (\mathbf{x}) \;\d\mathbf{x} =& - \int_{B_R (0)} \underbrace{\frac{\partial e^{-i\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\mathbf{x}}}{\partial x_j}}_{= (-i \omega_j) e^{-i\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\mathbf{x}}} f(\mathbf{x}) \;\d\mathbf{x} \\
  =& i \omega_j \int_{B_R (0)} e^{-i\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\mathbf{x}} f(\mathbf{x}) \;\d\mathbf{x}
\end{align*}
$$

Multiplying by the normalization factor $(2\pi)^{-n/2}$ leads to

$$
\begin{align*}
  \mathcal{F}[\partial_j f](\boldsymbol{\omega}) =& \frac{i\omega_j}{(2\pi)^{n/2}} \lim_{R\to\infty} \int_{B_R (0)} e^{-i\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot \mathbf{x}} f(\mathbf{x}) \;\d\mathbf{x} \\
  =& i\omega_j \hat{f}(\boldsymbol{\omega}) = i M_j f
\end{align*}
$$

**(2):** Because $f$ is a Schwartz function, $x_j f(\mathbf{x}) \in L^1 (\R^n, \mathbb{C})$, so that integration under the integral sign i justified. Thus, from $\eqref{equation-9}$

$$
\begin{align*}
  \frac{\partial}{\partial \omega_j} \mathcal{F}[f](\boldsymbol{\omega}) =& \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}} \int_{\R^n} \underbrace{\frac{\partial}{\partial \omega_j} \left(e^{-i\boldsymbol{\omega}_j \cdot \mathbf{x}}\right)}_{=-ix_j e^{-i\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\mathbf{x}}} f(\mathbf{x}) \;\d\mathbf{x} \\
  =& \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}} \int_{\R^n} e^{-i\omega\cdot\mathbf{x}} (-ix_j f(\mathbf{x}) \;\d\mathbf{x} \\
  =& \mathcal{F}[-ix_j f](\boldsymbol{\omega}) = \mathcal{F}[-iM_j f](\boldsymbol{\omega})
\end{align*}
$$
</details>
</MathBox>

<MathBox title='Convolution theorem' boxType="theorem">
Let $f, g \in L^1(\R)$ be integrable functions. The Fourier transform of the convolution

$$
	h(x) := (f*g)(x) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty f(y)g(x - y)\;\d y
$$

is given by

$$
	\hat{h}'(\xi) = \hat{f}(\xi)\cdot\hat{g}(\xi)
$$

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>

Taking the inverse Fourier transform of the product of $\hat{f}$ and $\hat{g}$ gives

$$
\begin{align*}
	\mathcal{F}\left[ \hat{f}(\xi) \hat{g}(\xi) \right] &= \int_{-\infty}^\infty \hat{f}(\xi)\hat{g}(\xi)e^{i2\pi\xi x}\;\d\xi \\
	&= \int_{-\infty}^\infty \hat{f}(\xi)e^{i2\pi\xi x}\left[ \int_{-\infty}^\infty g(y) e^{-i2\pi\xi y}\;\d y \right]\;\d\xi \\
	&= \int_{-\infty}^\infty g(y)\hat{f}(\xi) e^{i2\pi\xi(x - y)}\;\d\xi\d y \\
	&= \int_{-\infty}^\infty g(y) \underbrace_{\left[ \int_{-\infty}^\infty \hat{f}(\xi)e^{i2\pi\xi(x - y)}\;\d\xi \right]}_{f(x - y)}\;\d y \\
	&= \int_{-\infty}^\infty g(y)f(x - y)\;\d y \\
	&= g*f = f*g
\end{align*}
$$
</details>
</MathBox>

<MathBox title="Parseval-Plancherel theorem" boxType="theorem">
Let $f, g \in L^1(\R)\cap L^2(\R)$, then the following identity holds (Parseval's theorem)

$$
	\braket{f, g} = \int_{-\infty}^\infty f(x)\overline{g(x)}\;\d x = \int_{-\infty}^\infty \hat{f}(\xi)\overline{\hat{g}(\xi)}\;\d x
$$

In the special case $g = f$, this identity reduces to (Plancherel's theorem)

$$
	\norm{ f}_{L^2}^2 = \int_{-\infty}^\infty |f(x)|^2\;\d x = \int_{-\infty}^\infty \left|\hat{f}(\xi)\right|^2\;\d\xi
$$
</MathBox>

### Fixed point of the Fourier transform

<MathBox title="Gaussian function is a fixed point of the Fourier transform" boxType="proposition" tag="5">
The Gaussian function $f: \R^n \to\mathbb{C}$ given by $f(\mathbf{x}) = \exp(-\norm{\mathbf{x}}^2 /2)$ is a fixed point of the Fourier transform, i.e. $\hat{f}(\mathbf{p}) = \exp(-\norm{\mathbf{p}}^2 /2)$

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>

**The one-dimensional case**

Consider first the one-dimensional Gaussian $g:\R\to\mathbb{C}$ given by $g(x) = e^{-x^2 /2}$. This function satisfies the ordinary differential equation

$$
\begin{equation*}
  g'(x) = -xg(x)
\tag{\label{equation-13}}
\end{equation*}
$$

Applying the Fourier differentiation property $\eqref{equation-11}$, we have 

$$
\begin{equation*}
  \hat{g}' (p) = ip \hat{g}(p)
\tag{\label{equation-14}}
\end{equation*}
$$

Applying $\eqref{equation-12}$ gives

$$
\begin{equation*}
  \widehat{xg}(p) = i \hat{g}'(p)
\tag{\label{equation-15}}
\end{equation*}
$$

Taking the Fourier transform of $\eqref{equation-13}$ using $\eqref{equation-14}$ and $\eqref{equation-15}$ yields

$$
  ip\hat{g}(p) = -i\hat{g}'(p)
$$

or

$$
  \hat{g}'(p) + p\hat{g}(p) = 0
$$

which coincides with $\eqref{equation-13}$. Multiplying with the integrating factor $e^{p^2 /2}$, we obtain

$$
  e^{p^2 /2} \hat{g}' (p) + p e^{p^2 /2} \hat{g} (p) = 0
$$

By the product rule

$$
  \frac{\d}{\d p} \left(e^{p^2 /2} \hat{g}(p) \right) = 0
$$

Integrating both sides results in

$$
  e^{p/2} \hat{g} (p) = C
$$

or

$$
  \hat{g}(p) = Ce^{-p^2 /2}
$$

for some scalar $C \in\mathbb{C}$.

It remains to determine $C$. Setting $p = 0$, we have

$$
  C = \hat{g} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \int_\R \underbrace{e^{-i0\cdot x}}_{=1} e^{-x^2 /2} \;\d x
$$

Using the standard Gaussian integral

$$
  \int_\R e^{-x^2 /2} \;\d x = \sqrt{2\pi}
$$

we find that $C = 1$. Thus $\hat{g}(p) = e^{-p^2 /2}$.

**The $n$-dimensional case**

Now consider $f(\mathbf{x}) = e^{-\norm{\mathbf{x}}^2 /2}$. Since

$$
  \norm{\mathbf{x}}^2 = \sum_{j=1}^n x_j^2
$$

we can write

$$
  f(\mathbf{x}) = \prod_{j=1}^n e^{-x_j^2 /2}
$$

Consequently

$$
\begin{align*}
  \hat{f}(\mathbf{p}) =& \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}} \int_{\R^n} e^{-i\mathbf{p} \cdot \mathbf{x}} e^{-\norm{\mathbf{x}}^2 /2} \;\d\mathbf{x} \\
  =& \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}} \prod_{j=1}^n \int_\R e^{-ip_j x_j} e^{x_j^2 /2} \;\d x_j \\
  =& \prod_{j=1}^n \left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \int_\R e^{-ip_j x_j} e^{-x_j^2 /2} \;\d x_j \right)
\end{align*}
$$

From the one-dimensional result

$$
  \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \int_\R e^{-ip_j x_j} e^{-x_j^2 /2} \;\d x_j = e^{-p_j^2 /2}
$$

Thus

$$
\begin{align*}
  \hat{f}(\mathbf{p}) =& \prod_{j=1}^n e^{-p_j^2 /2} \\
  =& \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2} \sum_{j=1}^n p_j^2 \right) \\
  =& e^{-\norm{\mathbf{p}}^2 /2}
\end{align*}
$$
</details>
</MathBox>

## Inverse Fourier transform

<MathBox title="" boxType="proposition" tag="6">
Let $f\in S(\R^n, \mathbb{C})$ be a Schwartz function. Then its Fourier transform $\hat{f} = \mathcal{F}(f)$ is also a Schwartz function, i.e. $\mathcal{F}(f) \in S(\R^n, \mathbb{C})$.

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>

From $\eqref{equation-11}$, we have $\partial_j \hat{f} = \mathcal{F}(-i x_j f)$, and from $\eqref{equation-12}$ we get $p_j \hat{f} = \mathcal{F}(-\partial_j f)$. Since $f$ is a Schwartz function, multiplication by $x_j$ and partial differentiation with respect to $x_j$ both preserve the Schwartz space. Thus, for every multi-index $\alpha \in\N_0^n$

$$
  D^\alpha \hat{f} = \mathcal{F}\left((-i)^{|\alpha|} \mathbf{x}^{\alpha} f \right)
$$

In particular, $D^\alpha \hat{f}$ exists and is continuous for every $\alpha$.

It remains to show that all the Schwartz seminorms are finite. That is, we need to prove that every pair of multi-indices, $\alpha, \beta \in\N_0^n$

$$
\begin{equation*}
  \sup_{\mathbf{p} \in\R^n} \left| \norm{\mathbf{p}}^\beta D^\alpha \hat{f} (\mathbf{p}) \right| < \infty
\tag{\label{equation-17}}
\end{equation*}
$$

Applying $\eqref{equation-12}$ repeatedly, we obtain

$$
\begin{equation*}
  \mathbf{p}^\beta D^\alpha \hat{f} = (-i)^{|\alpha| + |\beta|} \mathcal{F}(D^\beta (\mathbf{x}^\alpha f)
\tag{\label{equation-16}}
\end{equation*}
$$

Because $f \in S(\R^n, \mathbb{C})$, we have $\mathbf{x}^\alpha f \in S(\R^n, \mathcal{C})$, and therefore $D^\beta (\mathbf{x}^\alpha f) \in S(\R^n, \mathbb{C})$. Every Schwartz function is integrable, so

$$
  D^\beta (\mathbf{x}^\alpha f) \in L^1 (\R^n, \mathbb{C})
$$

For any $h \in L^1 (\R^n, \mathbb{C})$, its Fourier transform is bounded, with

$$
  \norm{\hat{h}}_\infty \leq \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}} \norm{h}_{L^1}
$$

Applying this to $\eqref{equation-16}$, we get

$$
\begin{align*}
  \sup_{\mathbf{p}^\beta D^\alpha \hat{f}(\mathbf{p})} \leq& \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}} \norm{D^\beta (\mathbf{x}^\alpha f)}_{L^1} \\
  \leq& \infty
\end{align*}
$$

This implies that $\eqref{equation-17}$ holds for every pair of multi-indices $\alpha, \beta$. Thus, all Schwartz seminorms of $\hat{f}$ are finite, and it follows that $\hat{f} \in S(\R^n, \mathbb{C})$. Hence, the Fourier transform maps the Schwartz space into itself $\mathcal{F}: S(\R^n, \mathbb{C}) \to S(\R^n, \mathbb{C})$.
</details>
</MathBox>

<MathBox title="Fourier inversion theorem" boxType="theorem">
For a Schwartz function $f \in S(\R^n, \mathbb{C})$, then

$$
  f(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}} \int_{\R^n} e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{x}} \hat{f}(\mathbf{p}) \;\d\mathbf{p}
$$

or

$$
  \mathcal{F}^2 f(\mathbf{x}) = f(-\mathbf{x})
$$

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>

Define the normalized Gaussian

$$
  \Phi(\mathbf{x}) := \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}} e^{-\norm{\mathbf{x}}^2 /2}
$$

with satisfies the properties:
1. $\Phi(\mathbf{x}) \geq 0$ for all $\mathbf{x} \in\R^n$
2. $\hat{\Phi} = \Phi$ (Proposition $\ref{proposition-5}$)
3. $\int_{\R^n} \Phi(\mathbf{x}) \;\d\mathbf{x} = 1$

For $k\in\N$, define a Dirac sequence $\delta_k (\mathbf{x}) := k^n \Phi(k\mathbf{x})$. The functions $\delta_k$ form an approximate identity, satisfying $\delta_k (\mathbf{x}) \geq 0$ for all $k\in\N$ and $\mathbf{x}\in\R^n$. After a change of variables $\mathbf{u} = k\mathbf{x}$, we have

$$
  \int_{\R^n} \delta_k (\mathbf{x}) \;\d\mathbf{x} = \int_{\R^n} \Phi(\mathbf{u}) \;\d\mathbf{u} = 1
$$

Furthermore, for all $\epsilon > 0$, the ball $B_\epsilon (\mathbf{0}) := \set{\mathbf{y}\in \R^n : \norm{\mathbf{y}} < \epsilon}$ satifies

$$
  \lim_{k\to\infty} \int_{\R^n \setminus \mathcal{B}_\epsilon (\mathbf{0})} \delta_k (\mathbf{x}) \;\d \mathbf{x} = 0
$$

because $\Phi\in L^1 (\R^n, \mathbb{C})$.

Since $f$ is a Schwartz function, it is bounded and continuous. Thus, the approximate identity property gives

$$
\begin{equation*}
\begin{split}
  \lim_{k\to\infty} (\delta_k * f)(\mathbf{x}) =& \lim_{k\to\infty} \int_{\R^n} \delta_k (\mathbf{y}) f(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y})\;\d\mathbf{y} \\
  =& f(\mathbf{x}),\, \forall \mathbf{x} \in\R^n
\end{split}
\tag{\label{equation-18}}
\end{equation*}
$$

Since $\hat{\Phi} = \Phi$ by Proposition $\ref{proposition-5}$, we can express $\delta_k$ as

$$
\begin{align*}
  \delta_k (\mathbf{y}) =& k^n \hat{\Phi} (-k\mathbf{y}) \\
  =& \frac{k^n}{(2\pi)^{n/2}} \int_{\R^n} e^{-i(-k\mathbf{y}) \cdot \mathbf{x}} \Phi (\mathbf{x}) \;\d\mathbf{x}
\end{align*}
$$

Making the change of variables $\mathbf{z} = k\mathbf{x}$, with $\d\mathbf{x} = k^{-n} \d\mathbf{z}$, we get

$$
\begin{equation*}
  \delta_k (\mathbf{y}) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}} \int_{\R^n} e^{i\mathbf{y}\cdot\mathbf{z}} \Phi\left(\frac{\mathbf{z}}{k} \right) \;\d\mathbf{z}
\tag{\label{equation-19}}
\end{equation*}
$$

Substituting $\eqref{equation-19}$ into the integral limit of $\eqref{equation-18}$, we obtain

$$
  f(\mathbf{x}) = \lim_{k\to\infty} \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}} \int_{\R^n} \int_{\R^n} e^{i\mathbf{y}\cdot\mathbf{z}} \Phi\left(\frac{\mathbf{z}}{k} \right) f(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y}) \;\d\mathbf{z} \;\d\mathbf{y}
$$

Since $f$ and $\Phi$ are rapidly decreasing, the integrals are absolutely convergent. Thus, by Fubini's theorem we can interchange the order of integration

$$
  f(\mathbf{x}) = \lim_{k\to\infty} \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}} \int_{\R^n} \Phi\left(\frac{\mathbf{z}}{k} \right) \left(\int_{\R^n} e^{i\mathbf{y}\cdot\mathbf{z}} f(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y}) \;\d\mathbf{y} \right) \;\d\mathbf{z}
$$

Now set $\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y}$, or $\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{x} - \mathbf{u}$, so

$$
\begin{align*}
  \int_{\R^n} e^{i\mathbf{y}\cdot\mathbf{x}} f(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y}) \;\d\mathbf{y} =& \int_{\R^n} e^{i(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{u})\cdot\mathbf{z}} f(\mathbf{u}) \;\d\mathbf{u} \\
  =& e^{i\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{z}} \int_{\R^n} e^{-i\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{z}} f(\mathbf{u}) \;\d\mathbf{u}
\end{align*}
$$

By the definition of the Fourier transform

$$
  \int_{\R^n} e^{-i\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{z}} f(\mathbf{u}) \;\d\mathbf{u} = (2\pi)^{n/2} \hat{f}(\mathbf{z})
$$

Thus

$$
\begin{equation*}
  f(\mathbf{x}) = \lim_{k\to\infty} \int_{\R^n} \Phi\left(\frac{\mathbf{z}}{k} \right) e^{i\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{z}} \hat{f}(\mathbf{z}) \;\d\mathbf{z}
\end{equation*}
$$

For each fixed $\mathbf{z}$, we have

$$
  \lim_{k\to\infty} \Phi\left(\frac{\mathbf{z}}{k} \right) = \Phi(\mathbf{0}) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}}
$$

Moreover, since $\Phi$ attains it maximum at $\mathbf{0}$

$$
  0 \leq \Phi\left(\frac{\mathbf{z}}{k} \right) \leq \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}}
$$

so that

$$
  \left| \Phi\left(\frac{\mathbf{z}}{k} e^{i\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{z}} \hat{f}(\mathbf{z}) \right) \right| \leq \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}} |\hat{f}(\mathbf{z})|
$$

Since $\hat{f}$ is also a Schwartz function by Proposition $\ref{proposition-6}$, it follows that $\hat{f} \in L^1 (\R^n)$ is integrable. Thus, the integrand in $\eqref{equation-20}$ is dominated by the integrable function $|\hat{f}(\mathbf{z})|$. The dominated convergence theorem therefore gives
 
$$
\begin{align*}
  f(\mathbf{x}) =& \int_{\R^n} \underbrace{\lim_{k\to\infty} \Phi\left(\frac{\mathbf{z}}{k} \right)}_{=\Phi(0) = (2\pi)^{-n/2}} e^{i\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{z}} \hat{f}(\mathbf{z}) \;\d\mathbf{z} \\
  =& \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}} \int_{\R^n} e^{i\mathbf{x}\cdot \mathbf{z}} \hat{f}(\mathbf{z}) \;\d\mathbf{z}
\end{align*}
$$

Relabelling $\mathbf{z}$ as $\mathbf{p}$, we obtain the Fourier inversion formula

$$
  f(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}} \int_{\R^n} e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{x}} \hat{f}(\mathbf{p}) \;\d\mathbf{p}
$$
</details>
</MathBox>

## Uncertainty principle

<MathBox title="Uncertainty principle" boxType="theorem">
For any Schwartz function $f\in\mathcal{S}(\R)$ and any $x_0, \xi_0 \in\R$, the following inequality holds

$$
	\norm{ f(x)}_{L^2} \leq 4\pi \left\| (x - x_0) f(x) \right\|_{L^2}\cdot\left\| (\xi - \xi_0) \hat{f}(x) \right\|_{L^2}
$$

If $f$ is normalized, i.e. $\norm{ f(x)}_{L^2} = 1$, then the inequality can be written

$$
	\left\| (x - x_0) f(x) \right\|_{L^2}^2 \cdot\left\| (\xi - \xi_0) \hat{f}(x) \right\|_{L^2}^2 \geq \frac{1}{16\pi^2}
$$

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>

Applying integration by parts on $\norm{ f}_{L^2}^2$ gives

$$
\begin{align*}
	\norm{ f}_{L^2}^2 &= \int_{-\infty}^\infty |f(x)|^2\;\d x = \int_{-\infty}^\infty f(x)\overline{f(x)}\;\d x \\
	&= \left. x|f(x)|^2 \right|_{-\infty}^\infty - \int_{-\infty}^\infty x\left[ f'(x)\overline{f(x)} + f(x)\overline{f'(x)} \right]\;\d x \\
	&= - 2\int_{-\infty}^\infty x\Re\left[ f'(x)\overline{f'(x)} \right]\;\d x \\
	&\leq 2 \norm{ x f(x)}_{L_2} \cdot \left\| f'(x) \right\|_{L_2}
\end{align*}
$$

Where we have used that $\lim_{|x|\to\infty} \left| xf(x)^2 \right| = 0$ since $f$ is Schwartz, and the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality. Using Plancherel's identity and the Fourier transform derivation rule give

$$
	\left\| \overline{f'(x)} \right\|_{L_2} = \left\| f'(x) \right\|_{L_2} = \left\| \hat{f}'(\xi) \right\|_{L_2} = \left\| (i2\pi\xi)\hat{f}(\xi) \right\|_{L_2} = 2\pi\left\| \xi\hat{f}(\xi) \right\|_{L_2}
$$

Combining the results gives a special case of the uncertainty principle

$$
	\norm{ f}_{L^2}^2 \leq 4\pi \norm{ x f(x)}_{L^2} \cdot \left\| \xi \hat{f}(\xi) \right\|_{L^2}
$$

To prove the uncertainty principly in its full generality, we define the function

$$
	g(x) = e^{-i2\pi\xi_0 x} f(x + x_0) \;\textrm{ so that }\; \hat{g}(x) = e^{i2\pi x_0 \xi} \hat{f}(\xi + \xi_0)
$$

For this function $g$, we have

$$
\begin{align*}
	\norm{ g}_{L^2}^2 &= \int_{-\infty}^\infty \left| e^{-i2\pi\xi_0 x} f(x + x_0) \right|^2 \;\d x = \int_{-\infty}^\infty \left| f(t) \right|^2 \;\d t = \norm{ f}_{L^2}^2 \\
	\norm{ u g(u)}_{L^2} &= \left( \int_{-\infty}^\infty \left| u e^{-i2\pi\xi_0 u} f(u + x_0) \right|^2 \;\d u \right)^{1/2} \\
	&= \left( \int_{-\infty}^\infty \left| (x - x_0) f(x) \right|^2 \;\d x \right)^{1/2} = \norm{ (x - x_0)f(x)}_{L^2} \\
	\norm{ t\hat{g}(t)}_{L^2} &= \left( \int_{-\infty}^\infty \left| t e^{i2\pi x_0 t} \hat{f}(t + \xi_0) \right|^2 \;\d t \right)^{1/2} \\
	&= \left( \int_{-\infty}^\infty \left| \left(\xi - \xi_0 \right) \hat{f}(\xi_0) \right|^2 \;\d\xi \right)^{1/2} = \left\| \left(\xi - \xi_0\right) \hat{f}(\xi) \right\|_{L^2}
\end{align*}
$$

Substituting $g$ into the specialized uncertainty inequality above yields the desired result.
</details>
</MathBox>

The equality holds in the uncertainty principle if we get an equality when applying the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality. This implies we must have $f'(x) = \lambda xf(x)$ for some scalar $\lambda$. This differential equation has the general solution

$$
	f(x) = \alpha e^{-\frac{\lambda}{2} x^2} \quad \lambda > 0,\; |\alpha|^2 = \sqrt\frac{\lambda}{\pi}\norm{ f}_{L^2}^2
$$

# Discrete Fourier transform

<MathBox title='Discrete Fourier transform (DFT)' boxType="definition">
The discrete Fourier transform of a complex sequence $\Set{ f_n }_{n=0}^N \subseteq\mathbb{C}$ produces a complex sequence $\Set{ F_k }_{k=0}^{N-1}$ where

$$
	\mathcal{F}\Set{ f_k } = F_k = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x_n \cdot e^{-\frac{i2\pi}{N}kn}
$$

The inverse transform is defined by

$$
	f_n = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} X_k \cdot e^{\frac{i2\pi}{N}kn}
$$
</MathBox>

# Short-time Fourier transform

<MathBox title='Short-time Fourier transform (STFT)' boxType="definition">
The short-time Fourier transform of an integrable function $f:\R\to\mathbb{C}$ is defined by

$$
	\mathcal{F}\left[ f(\tau) \right] = \hat{f}_g(t, \omega) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty f(\tau) \overline{g(\tau - t)} e^{-i \omega x}\;\d\tau = \braket{f(\tau), g_{t,\omega}(\tau)}
$$

where $g_{t, \omega}(\tau) = e^{i\omega t}g(\tau - t)$ and $g(t)$ is the kernel (window). The Gabor transform is a special case of the STFT with a Gaussian kernel of the form

$$
	g(t) = e^{-\frac{(t - \tau)^2}{a^2}}
$$

The inverse STFT transform is given by

$$
	f(t) = \mathcal{F}^{-1}\left[ \hat{f}_g (t, \omega) \right] = \frac{1}{2\pi\| g\|^2} \int_{-\infty}^\infty\int_{-\infty}^\infty \hat{f}_g (\tau, \omega)g(t - \tau)e^{i\omega t}\;\d\omega \d t
$$
</MathBox>

## Discrete

The discrete version of the STFT can be formed by discretizing time and frequency

$$
	\tau = j\Delta t \quad \nu = k \Delta\omega \quad j, k \in\N
$$

The discretized kernel function becomes

$$
	g_{j, k} = e^{i2\pi k\Delta\omega t} g(t - k\Delta t)
$$

and the discrete STFT is

$$
	\hat{f}_{j, k} = \braket{f, g_{j,k}} = \int_{-\infty}^\infty f(\tau)\overline{g_{j, k}(\tau)}\;\d\tau
$$

# Wavelet transform

Wavelets extend the concepts in Fourier analysis to more general orthogonal bases, and partially overcome the uncertainty principle by exploiting multi-resolution decomposition. This multi-resolution approach enables different time and frequency fidelities in different frequency bands.

<MathBox title="Wavelet" boxType="definition">

A function $\psi\in L^2(\R)$ is called an orthonormal wavelet if it generates a basis for $L^2(\R)$. The basis is constructed as a family of functions by means of translations and dilations of $\psi$

$$
	\Set{\psi_{a,b}(t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{a}}\left( \frac{t - b}{a} \right) | b \in\R,\; a\R^+ }
$$

In terms of dyadic translations and dilations with $a = 2^{-j}$ and $b = k2^{-j}$ for $j, k \in \Z$, we get

$$
	\braket{\psi_{jk}, \psi_{lm}} = \int_{-\infty}^\infty \psi_{jk}(x)\overline{\psi_{lm}(x)}\;\d x = \delta_{jl}\delta_{km}
$$
</MathBox>

<MathBox title='Wavelet transform' boxType="definition">
Suppose $\psi\in L^2 (\R)$ is an orthonormal wavelet satisfying the boundedness property

$$
	C_\psi = \int_{-\infty}^\infty \frac{\left| \hat{\psi}(\omega) \right|^2}{|\omega |}\d\omega < \omega
$$

The wavelet transform of a function $f\in L^1(\R$ is defined as

$$
	\mathcal{W}_\psi \left[ f(t) \right](a, b) = \braket{f, \psi_{a, b}} = \int_{-\infty}^\infty f(t)\overline{\psi_{a,b}(t)}\;\d t = \frac{1}{\sqrt{|a|}}\int_{-\infty}^\infty f(t)\overline{\psi\left(\frac{t - b}{a}\right)}\;\d t
$$

The inverse wavelet transform is

$$
	f(t) = \frac{1}{C_\psi} \int_0^\infty \int_{-\infty}^\infty \mathcal{W}_\psi [f](a, b) \psi_{a, b}(t)\frac{1}{a^2}\;\d a\d b
$$

Every function $f\in L^2(\R)$ may be expanded in the wavelet basis as (using dyadic translations and dialtions)

$$
	f(t) = \sum_{j=-\infty}^\infty \sum_{k=-\infty}^\infty \psi_{jk}(t)
$$

where the wavelet coefficients $c_{jk}$ are given by

$$
	c_{jk} = \braket{f(t), \psi_{j, k}} = \mathcal{W}_\psi[f(t)]\left(2^{-j}, k2^{-j} \right)
$$
</MathBox>
