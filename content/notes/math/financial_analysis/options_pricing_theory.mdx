# Options

An option is a financial derivative that gives its holder an asymmetrical exposure to the performance of an underlying asset (the underlier). Specifically, an option is contract that grants its holder the right, but not the obligation, to trade the underlier at a predetermined *strike price* $K$. For a European-style option, this right may by exercised only at the expiration time $T$, whereas for an American-style option, it may be exercised at any time up to and including $T$. In exchange for this right, the option holder pays the seller (or writer) a premium.

There are many types of options, but call and put options are the fundamental ones. A call option gives its holder the right, but not the obligation to buy the underlier at the strike price $K$. At expiration $T$, its payoff is

$$
  \Pi_T^\text{call} = N (S_T - K)^+
$$

where $S_T$ denotes the price of the underlier at $T$, and $N$ denotes the number of units of the underlier covered by the option.

Similarly, a put option gives it holder the right, but not the obligation to sell the underlier at the strike price $K$. Its payoff at expiration $T$

$$
  \Pi_T^\text{put} = N (K - S_T)^+
$$

A general distinction goes between vanilla and exotic options. Vanilla options are standard option contracts with conventional payoff structures. The fundamental vanilla options are calls and puts. Options with non-standard or more complex payoff structures are called exotic options.

Options have a wide range of applications, including:
- **Hedging:** Options can be used to protect existing exposures in the underlier. For example, an investor with a long position in the underlier can purchase a put option to establish a floor on the value of that position. Conversely, a short position in the underlier can be hedged by purchasing a call option, which limits the loss that can arise from an increase in the underlier's price.
- **Leverage:** A call option can provide exposure to increases in the underlier's price while requiring an initial cash outlay that is typically smaller than the cost of purchasing the underlier outright. For example, rather than investing $N S_0$ to acquire $N$ units of an asset, an investor may purchase a call on $N$ units by paying a premium that may be substantially smaller than $N S_0$. This creates leveraged exposure, but the two investments are not equivalent: the call has a nonlinea
- **Directional exposure with limited downside:** Suppose an investor has a bearish view on the underlier over a given horizon. A short position in the underlier or a short forward position generates a payoff that decreases as $S_T$ increases and therefore carries potentially substantial losses if the market moves against the position. By contrast, purchasing a put option provides bearish exposure while limiting the holder's maximum loss to the premium paid (ignoring financing and transaction costs). The put therefore combines directional exposure with explicit downside protection.
- **Expressing specific market view:** Because options have nonlinear payoffs, combinations of calls, puts, and positions in the underlier can be constructed to express particular views about the direction, magnitude, or distribution of future price movements. Such combinations, commonly referred to as option strategies or option spreads, allow an investor to tailor the payoff profile to a specific market scenario and investment horizon.

## Option value

Let $S_t$ denote the price of the underlier at time $t$. An the market-to-market (MtM) value of an option, or simply option price, is the current market value at which the option can be bought or sold prior to its expiration. At inception, the option's market price is called the option premium. 

For a standard vanilla option, its market price can be conceptually decomposed into two components

$$
  \text{option price} = \text{intrinsic value} + \text{time value}
$$

**Intrinsic value**
The intrinsic value of an option at time $t$ is the value that would be obtained by exercising the option immediately, assuming immediate exercise is permitted. For a call and put, respectively, the intrinsic value is given by

$$
\begin{align*}
  I_t^\text{call} =& (S_t - K)^+ \\
  I_t^\text{put} =& (K - S_t)^+
\end{align*}
$$

For a European-style option, which cannot be exercised before its expiration, intrinsic value should be interpreted as the immediate-exercise value rather than as an amount that can actually be realized at time $t$.

**Time value**
The time value (or extrinsic value) of an option is the portion of its market price that exceeds its intrinsic value. Denoting the market price of the optiona at time $t$ by $V_t$, its time value is

$$
  \mathrm{TV}_t = V_t - I_t
$$

Time value reflects, among other factors, the possibility that the option may become more valuable before expiration as the underlier price, volatility, interest rates, or other relevant market variables evolve. For a standard option in a frictionless setting with no arbitrage, the time value vanishes at expiration, so that

$$
\begin{align*}
  V_T^\text{call} =& (S_T - K)^+ \\
  V_T^\text{pu} =& (K - S_T)^+
\end{align*}
$$

### Option sensitivities

The dynamics of an option's value can be described either in terms of calendar time $t$ or time to maturity $\tau = T - t$, where $T$ is the expiration time. Since $T$ is fixed, time to maturity decreases one-for-one with the passage of calendar time, i.e. $\d\tau = -\d t$.

The delta of an option measures the sensitivity of its value to changes in the price of the underlier. Let $V(S, t)$ denote the value of an option as a function of the underlier price $S$ and calendar time $t$. The delta at time $t$ is defined as 

$$
\begin{equation*}
  \Delta_t = \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} (S_t, t)
\tag{\label{equation-3}}
\end{equation*}
$$

Thus, for a sufficiently small change $\Delta S$ in the underlier price, and all other model inputs fixed, the corresponding first-order change in the option value is $\Delta V \approx \Delta_t \Delta S$.

The theta of an option measures the sensitivity of its value to the passage of time, holding the other model inputs fixed. Let $V(S,\tau)$ denoted the value of an option as a function of the underlier price $S$ and time to maturity $\tau$. The sensitivity to time maturity is given by

$$
  \frac{\partial V}{\partial\tau} (S_t, \tau_t)
$$

Because $\d\tau = -\d t$, the corresponding sensitivity to calendar time is

$$
  \frac{\partial V}{\partial\tau} (S_t, \tau_t) = -\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} (S_t, t)
$$

If we define theta with respect to calendar time as

$$
  \theta_t = \frac{\partial V}{\partial t} (S_t, t) = -\frac{\partial V}{\partial\tau} (S_t, \tau_t)
$$

then a small increment $\Delta t$ in calendar time produces the first-order change $\Delta V \approx \Theta_t \Delta t$, provided all other model inputs are held fixed.

An alternative convention is to define theta in terms of time to maturity

$$
  \theta_t = \frac{\partial V}{\partial \tau} (S_t, \tau_t) = -\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} (S_t, t)
$$

which differ only by a minus sign.

### Moneyness

The moneyness of an options is classified as follows
- **In-the-money (ITM):** The option has strictly positive intrinsic value, i.e. $S_t > K$ for a call and $S_t < K$ for a put.
- **At-the-money (ATM):** The option has zero intrinsic value and the underlier price equals the strike, $K = S_t$.
- **Out-of-the-money (OTM):** The option has zero intrinsic value and is not AMT, i.e. $S_t < K$ for a call and $S_t > K$ for a put.

Moneyness is therefore time-dependent: an option can move between ITM, ATM and OTM as the priceof the underlier changes.

At inception, it is also common to classify the strike relative to a reference price. Two commonly used conventions are at-the-money spot (ATMS) and at-the-money forward (ATMF).
- an option is ATMS if $K = S_0$
- an option is ATMF if $K = F_{0,T}$, where $F_{0,T}$ denotes the forward price of the underlier for maturity $T$, observed at inception

In general $F_{0,T} \neq S_0$, because the forward price incorporates relevant financing, carry, income, and other market-specific effects of the period from $0$ to $T$. Consequently, an option may be ATMF without being ATMS, and vice versa.

The terms ITM and OTM can also be used to describe the option's moneyness at inception. Specifically, an option is ITM at inception if 
- $K < S_0$ for a call
- $K > S_0$ for a put

and OTM at inception if
- $K > S_0$ for a call
- $K < S_0$ for a put

When $K = S_0$, the option is ATM spot at inception.

### Notional amount

The notional amount of an option determines the scale of its payoff. The precise convention depends on the type of option and the market in which it is traded.

A common convention is to specify the notional amount as a number of units of the underlier. If $N$ denotes the number of units, the terminal payoff is

$$
\begin{align*}
  \Pi_T^\text{call} =& N (S_T - K)^+ \\
  \Pi_T^\text{put} =& N (K - S_T)^+ \\
\end{align*}
$$

Alternatively, particularly in structured products, the notional may be specified as a monetary amount. In this convention, the payoff is often expressed in terms of the return of the underlier rather than in units of the underlying asset. For example, a monetary notional $N$ applied to an ATMS call may give the terminal payoff

$$
  \Pi_T^\text{call} = N \left(\frac{S_T}{S_0} - 1 \right)^+
$$

Here, $N$ has units of currency, whereas in the first convention $N$ represents a number of units of the underlier. The two conventions therefore generate different monetary payoffs unless the respective notionals are appropriately related.

## Vanilla options

Vanilla options are standard, relatively simple option contracts with conventional payoff structures. The fundamental vanilla options are calls and puts, typically written on equities, equity indices, FX rates, commodities, or interest rates. The term vanilla is not, however, a strict synonym for liquid: an option may have a standard payoff structure without being particularly liquid.

For many actively traded vanilla options, market prices or implied volatilities are quoted using conventions based on a standard pricing framework. In the case of equity and some other asset classes, the Black–Scholes–Merton model provides the classical reference framework. In practice, however, market-standard pricing models and volatility conventions differ across asset classes; for example, FX and interest-rate options commonly use conventions and models adapted to their respective markets.

### Equity options

Vanilla equity options are call and put options of either European or American style written on a single stock or an equity index. Equity index options are actively traded on many exchanges and generally have substantial trading volumes. Single-stock options are also exchange-listed in several market, although trading activity varies considerably across stocks and regions.

Settlement conventions depend on the particular contract. For example, many exchange-listed single-stock options in the United Stated are American-style and physically settled, meaning that exercise results in the delivery of the underlying shares. Equity index optoins are typically cash-settled, since an index itself cannot be physically delivered. The precise exercise and settlement conventions are contract-specific.

Equity options are generally quoted by specifying the strike and expiration. Depending on the market convention, the strike may be expressed in absolute price terms or relative to a reference level. For index options and structured equity products, it is also common to describe strikes in terms of their moneyness, for example as a percentage of the spot or forward level.

### Foreign exchange (FX) options

A vanilla foreign exchange option is a call or put option on an exchange rate. Let $X_t$ denote the number of units of currency $2$ ($\text{ccy}2$) per unit of currency $1$ ($\text{ccy}1$), so that the FX quotation is expressed as 

$$
\begin{equation*}
  X_t = \frac{\text{ccy}1}{\text{ccy}2}
\tag{\label{equation-1}}
\end{equation*}
$$

A call on $\text{ccy}1$ gives the holder the right to buy currency $1$ and pay for it in currency $2$ at the strike exchange rate $K$. If the option has a notional $N$ units of currency $1$, its terminal payoff, expressed in currency $2$, is

$$
  \Pi_T^\text{call, ccy1} = N (X_T - K)^+
$$

Similarly, a put on currency $1$ gives the holder the right to sell $N$ units of currency $1$ in exchange for currency $2$ at strike $K$, with terminal payoff

$$
  \Pi_T^\text{put, ccy2} = N (K - X_T)^+
$$

**FX option duality**

FX options exhibit a quotation duality arising from the fact that an exchange rate can be quoted in either direction. The inverse exchange rate of $\eqref{equation-1}$ is

$$
\begin{equation*}
  Y_t = \frac{1}{X_t} = \frac{\text{ccy}1}{\text{ccy}2}
\tag{\label{equation-2}}
\end{equation*}
$$

Consider an $N$-unit call on $\text{ccy}1$, with strike $K$ in units of $\text{ccy}2$ per unit of $\text{ccy}1$. Its payoff in currency $2$ is $N ( X_T - K)^+$. Using $\eqref{equation-2}$, the terminal payoff can be expressed as

$$
  N (X_T - K)^+ = N K X_T \left(\frac{1}{K} - Y_T \right)
$$

so, after accounting for the appropriate currency conversion, the same economic exposure can be represented as a put on the inverse FX rate with strike $1/K$. Specifically, the corresponding option is a put on $\text{ccy}2$, with notional $KN$ in currency $2$ and strike $1/K$ in units of $\text{ccy}1$ per unit of $\text{ccy}2$.

Thus, an option to buy $N$ units of $\text{ccy}1$ against $KN$ units of $\text{ccy}2$ at exchange rate $K$ can equivalently be described, under the inverse quotation, as an option to sell $KN$ units of $\text{ccy}2$ against $N$ units of $\text{ccy}1$ at exchange rate $1/K$.

**FX option quotation**

In the FX options market, vanilla options are commonly quoted in terms of delta rather than directly by strike. Delta is calculated using the relevant FX option pricing convention, typically based on a Black–Scholes/Garman–Kohlhagen framework. Standard market quotations often include:

- ATMF (at-the-money forward);
- $25\%$-delta options; and
- $10\%$-delta options.

The precise definition of FX delta—such as spot delta, forward delta, or premium-adjusted delta—is market- and currency-pair-dependent, so the quotation convention must be specified when interpreting an FX volatility quote.

### Commodity options

Depending on the commodity and market convention, a vanilla commodity option may be written directly on the spot price of the commodity or, more commonly in exchange-traded markets, on a futures contract. The precise underlying, exercise style, and settlement mechanism are contract-specific.

For an option written with notional $N$ on a futures price $F_t$, the terminal payoff for strike $K$ is

$$
  \Pi_T^\text{call} = N (F_T - K)^+
$$

### Interes rate options

Two fundamental classes of vanilla interest-rate options are caps/floors and swaptions.

#### Caps and floors

Consider an accrual period $(T_{i-1}, T_i)$ with year fraction $\tau_i$ and a reference interest rate $R_i$ applying to that period. A caplet is a call option on the reference interest rate. With notional $N$ and strike $K$, its payoff, typically paid at $T_i$ is

$$
  \Pi_{T_i}^\text{caplet} = N\tau_i (R_i - K)^+
$$

Thus, the caplet holder receives a payment when the reference rate exceeds the strike $K$. The exact definition of $R_i$, its fixing date, and the payment date depend on the underlying rate and market convention.

An interest-rate cap is a portfolio of caplets covering a sequence of accrual periods. If all caplets have the same strike $K$, its cash flow in period $i$ is

$$
  \Pi_{T_i}^\text{cap} = N\tau_i (R_i - K)
$$

and the collection cash flows is given by

$$
  \Pi^\text{cap} = N \sum_{i=1}^m \tau_i (R_i - K)^+
$$

Similarly, a floorlet is a put option on the reference interest rate. It payoff at $T_i$ is

$$
  \Pi_{T_i}^\text{floorlet} = N \tau_i (K - R_i)^+
$$

An interest-rate floor is a portfolio of floorlets with cash flow at $T_i$ given by

$$
  \Pi_{T_i}^\text{floor} = N \tau_i (K - R_i)^+
$$

The colloction of cash flows is the sum

$$
  \Pi^\text{floor} = N \sum_{i=1}^m \tau_i (K - R_i)^+
$$

Hence, the cap and floor can be viewed as collections of individual options on the relevant reference rates, each associated with a particular accrual period. Market quotations for caps and floors are commonly expressed in terms of strike and maturity, although conventions can vary by currency and reference rate.

#### Swaption

A swaption is an option that gives the holder the right, but not the obligation to enter into and interest-rate swap (IRS) at a specified future date. Consider a swaption expiring at $T$, where the underlying interest-rate swap begins at $T$ and matures at $T_m$. The holder has the right to enter into this swap at the predermined fixed rate $K$.

Let $s(T, T_m)$ denote the par swap rate at the option epiration date $T$, and let $A(T, T_m)$ denote the present value at $T$ of one unit of the fixed-leg annuity of the underlying swap. For a payer swaption, the value at expiration is

$$
  \Pi_T^\text{payer} = N A(T, T_m) (s(T, T_m) - K)^+
$$

For a receiver swaption, the corresponding payoff is

$$
  \Pi_T^\text{receiver} = N A(T, T_m) (K - s(T, T_m))^+
$$

The factor $A(T, T_m)$ converts the difference between the market swap rate and the strike into the present value, at $T$, of the corresponding stream of fixed-versus-floating interest payments.

Here $N$ is the notional of the underlying interest-rate swap. The option tenor is the period from inception to the expiration date $T$, while the underlying swap tenor is the period from $T$ to $T_m$. Thus, it is useful to distinguish the swaptions' option maturity from the maturity of the underlying swap.

Swaptions are typically quoted by specifying the option expiry, the underlying swap tenor, and the relevant strike or volatility measure. For example, a quotation may refer to a swaption with a 5-year option expiry into a 10-year underlying swap. The precise quotation convention—strike, premium, or implied volatility—depends on the market and currency.

### Option pricing and hedging

**Option hedging**

In an idealized complete, frictionless market, the no-arbitrage value of a contingent claim can be interpreted as the cost of dynamically replicating its payoff. Thus, the option's price is equal to the initial cost of a self-financing trading strategy that reproduces the option's terminal payoff.

A delta-hedging strategy dynamically adjusts the position in the underlier according to the option's delta $\eqref{equation-3}$. For example, if the option has delta $\Delta_t$, a position of $-\Delta_t$ units of the underlier, together with the appropriate position in the risk-free asset, locally offsets the option's first-order exposure to movement in the underlier price.

It is important to distinguish the option price from its hedging cost in a general market. The equality between the two is a consequence of the no-arbitrage and completeness assumptions underlying the replication argument; in real markets, transaction costs, discrete rebalancing, liquidity constraints, model risk, and imperfect hedging mean that an option's market price need not equal the realized cost of hedging it ex post.

**Vanilla option pricing**

The Black-Scholes-Merton (BSM) model is the classical framework for the pricing and delta hedging of European options. Under this model, the underlier price follows a geometric Browian motion 

$$
  \frac{d S_t}{S_t} = \mu \d t + \sigma \d W_t
$$

where $\mu$ is the drift of the underlier under the chosen probability measure, $\sigma > 0$ is its constant volatility, and $W_t$ is a standard Wiener process. Under the risk-neutral measure used for pricing, and in the absence of dividends, the drift becomes the continuously compounded risk-free rate $r$

$$
  \frac{d S_t}{S_t} = r \d t + \sigma \d W_t^\mathbb{Q}
$$

The BSM model applies to vanilla equity options, but has been adapted to other asset classes. In particular, the Garman-Kohlhagen model is the standard extension for FX options, while the Black model is widely used for options on forwards and futures, including many interest-rate derivatives.

For a European vanilla, the classical Black-Scholes (BS) formulas give the no-arbitrage call and put prices under the assumptions of the BSM model. For a dividend-free underlier, with current spot price $S_0$, strike $K$ and time to maturity $T$, the call and put prices are

$$
\begin{equation*}
\begin{split}
  c =& S_0 N(d_1) - K e^{-rT} N(d_2) \\
  p =& K e^{-rT} N (-d_2) - S_0 N(-d_1)
\end{split}
\tag{\label{equation-4}}
\end{equation*}
$$

where

$$
  d_1 = \frac{\ln(S_0/K) + (r + \sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}
$$

and

$$
  d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T} = \frac{\ln(S_0/K) + (r - \sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}
$$

Here $N(\cdot)$ denotes the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal distribution

$$
  N(z) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \int_{-\infty}^z \exp\left(-\frac{x^2}{2} \right) \;\d x
$$

The formulas $\eqref{equation-4}$ assumes zero dividends or other carry on the underlier. If the underlier pays a continuous dividend yield $q$, the corresponding formulas become

$$
\begin{equation*}
\begin{split}
  c =& S_0 e^{-qT} N(d_1) - K e^{-rT} N(d_2) \\
  p =& K e^{-rT} N (-d_2) - S_0 e^{-qT} N(-d_1)
\end{split}
\tag{\label{equation-4}}
\end{equation*}
$$

with 

$$
  d_1 = \frac{\ln(S_0/K) + (r - q + \sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}
$$

and $d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T}$. Thus, in the BSM framework, the option value depends on the spot price $S_0$, strike $K$, time to maturity $T$, interest rate $r$, and volatility $\sigma$, together with any relevant carry such as a dividend yield $q$.

Among these parameters, volatility is particularly important for option traders and risk managers, because it is not directly observable in the same way as spot or interest rates and has a substantial effect on option values. For fixed $S_0$, $K$, $T$, and $r$, the price of a standard European call or put is a strictly increasing function of volatility. Consequently, there is a one-to-one relationship between the option price and its implied volatility. The implied volatility is the value of $\sigma$ that, when substituted into the relevant pricing model, reproduces the observed market price.

### Implied volatility

Implied volatility is the value of the volatility parameters $\sigma$ that, with all other relevant model inputs held fixed, makes a pricing model reproduce the observed market price of an option. In the Black-Scholes-Merton framework, the implied volatility $\sigma_\text{i}$ is defined implicitly by

$$
  V^\text{BSM} (S_0, K, T, r, \sigma_\text{i}) = V^\text{market}
$$

where $V^\text{market}$ is the observed market price.

It is important to distinguish realized volatility, which describes the historical or future variability of the underlier, from implied volatility, which is a model-dependent quantity inferred from option prices.

In the basic BSM model, a single constant volatility parameter $\sigma$ is assumed for a given underlier. Consequently, the model would imply the same volatility for all strikes and maturities. In observed markets, however, the implied volatility inferred from option prices generally varies with both strike and maturity. Thus, implied volatility should more generally be viewed as a function $\sigma_\text{i} (K, T)$.

For a fixed maturity, the dependence of implied volatility on strike is known as the volatility smile or, more generally, the volatility skew/smile. Across maturities, the corresponding dependence is referred to as the volatility term structure.

#### Volatility smile 

There are several complementary explanations for the observed strike dependence of implied volatility

- **Limitations of the BSM model.** The BSM model assumes that the underlier follows a geometric Brownian motion with constant volatility. Under this assumption, the terminal price is lognormally distributed. Empirical equity-return distributions, however, typically exhibit features that are inconsistent with this assumption, including heavier tails and, in many cases, significant skewness. A single constant volatility therefore cannot simultaneously reproduce market prices across all strikes. By allowing volatility to vary with strike, practitioners can reproduce a broader cross-section of observed option prices.

  This adjustment should not be interpreted as implying that the resulting volatility parameter represents the true future volatility of the underlier. Rather, it is a model-implied parameter that summarizes the market prices of options with different strikes. In particular, simply replacing the constant BSM volatility by a strike-dependent implied volatility does not, by itself, define a complete stochastic model for the dynamics of the underlier or its volatility. Consequently, such a procedure is generally insufficient for consistently pricing path-dependent derivatives. More sophisticated models, such as local-volatility or stochastic-volatility models, are typically required when the dynamics of the entire implied-volatility surface are relevant.

- **Supply and demand.** Option prices also reflect the balance of supply and demand across strikes. For example, institutional investors with substantial long equity exposure may demand OTM put options to protect against large downward moves in equity markets. Strong demand for such protection can increase the prices of OTM puts and therefore their implied volatilities. This mechanism is one contributing factor to the typically higher implied volatility observed for lower-strike equity index puts.

The shape of the smile is not universal, however. Single-stock options can exhibit different patterns from broad equity-index options, and the skew can vary substantially across stocks, maturities, and market conditions. In some markets or for some individual securities, implied volatility can even increase with strike over part of the relevant range.

#### Dynamics of the volatility smile

The dynamics of the implied-volatility surface describe how implied volatility changes as the underlier price and other market variables evolve. Two simplified descriptions are commonly used to characterize the movement of the smile when the underlier price changes while the overall level of expected volatility is assumed to remain approximately stable.
- **Sticky strike.** The implied volatility associated with each fixed strike remains approximately unchanged as the underlier price moves. In other words,

$$
  \sigma_\text{i} (K, S_t) \approx \sigma_\text{i} (K, S_0)
$$

for fixed $K$. The smile is therefore anchored to absolute strike levels. This assumption can be a useful approximation in certain market conditions, although it is not a fundamental property of option prices.

- **Sticky delta.** The implied volatility associated with a given option delta remains approximately unchanged as the underlier price moves. In this case, the smile effectively moves with the underlier, since the strike corresponding to a given delta changes as the spot or forward price changes. This convention is particularly natural in markets such as FX, where volatility surfaces are frequently quoted in delta space.

Neither assumption is universally correct. They are simplified rules for describing smile dynamics, and the actual evolution of the volatility surface is an empirical property of the market.

#### Forward volatility and variance additivity

Suppose $0 = t_0 < t_1 < t_2$. If $\sigma_1$ and $\sigma_2$ denote the corresponding constant-equivalent implied volatilities for maturities $t_1$ and $t_2$, then, under the assumption that total variance is additive across non-overlapping time intervals

$$
  \sigma_2^2 t_2 = \sigma_1^2 t_1 + \sigma^2 (t_1, t_2) (t_2 - t_1)
$$

where $\sigma(t_1, t_2)$ is the forward volatility associated with the interval $[t_1, t_2]$. Hence

$$
\begin{equation*}
  \sigma^2 (t_1, t_2) = \frac{\sigma_2^2 t_2 - \sigma_1^2 t_1}{t_2 - t_1}
\tag{\label{equation-5}}
\end{equation*}
$$

The key object being interpolated or extrapolated here is total variance, not volatility itself. This follows from the fact that, under the usual constant-volatility diffusion framework, variance accumulates linearly with time.

Forward volatility is useful, for example, in pricing forward-starting options, whose strike or moneyness is determined at a future date.

If $t_1 < t < t_2$, a simple piecewise-constant-forward-volatility interpolation assumes that the forward volatility over $[t_1, t]$ is equal to the forward volatility over the entire interval $[t_1, t_2]$. The total variance accumulated up to $t$ is then

$$
  \sigma^2 (t) t = \sigma_1^2 t_1 + \sigma^2 (t_1, t_2) (t - t_1)
$$

Substituting in $\eqref{equation-5}$ gives

$$
  \sigma^2 (t) t = \sigma_1^2 t_1 + \frac{t - t_1}{t_2 - t_1} (\sigma_2^2 t_2 - \sigma_1^2 t_1)
$$

Equivalently,

$$
  \sigma^2 (t) t = [1 - w(t)] \sigma_1^2 t_1 + w(t) \sigma_2^2 t_2
$$

where

$$
  w(t) = \frac{t - t_1}{t_2 - t_1}
$$

Thus, this interpolation scheme performs a linear interpolation of total variance between two maturities. The resulting implied volatility is then recovered as

$$
  \sigma(t) = \sqrt{\frac{[1 - w(t)] \sigma_1^2 t_1 + w(t) \sigma_2^2 t_2}{t}}
$$

This is only one possible interpolation method. In practice, volatility surfaces are typically interpolated using conventions designed to preserve desirable properties such as smoothness and, where required, absence of static arbitrage.

derived (with other parameters fixed) from an option price quoted in the market. For a given underlier, although its volatility has a unique value in the BSM model, its implied volatility may be different for different tenors and option strikes. For a given tenor, the implied volatilities from the options with different strike prices show a pattern known as volatility smile.

#### FX volatility quotations

In the FX options market, volatility is commonly quoted using delta-based conventions rather than directly specifying strikes. For a given maturity, a typical quotation includes the at-the-money-forward (ATMF) volatility together with risk reversals and butterflies.

The ATMF volatility, denoted $\sigma_\text{ATMF}$, corresponds to a strike equal to the forward ATM strike, subject to the relevant FX market convention.

A 25-delta risk reversal is defined as

$$
  25\Delta\text{RR} = \sigma_{25\Delta\text{call}} - \sigma_{25\Delta\text{put}}
$$

It measures the assymmetry of implied volatility between the 25-delta call and put.

A 25-delta butterfly (or 25-delta strangle) is defined by

$$
  25\Delta\text{BF} = \frac{1}{2} (\sigma_{25\Delta\text{call}} + \sigma_{25\Delta\text{put}}) - \sigma_\text{ATMF}
$$

Given these three market quotes, the two wing volatilities can be recovered as

$$
\begin{align*}
  \sigma_{25\Delta\text{call}} =& \sigma_\text{ATMF} + 25\Delta\text{BF} + \frac{1}{2} 25\Delta\text{RR} \\
  \sigma_{25\Delta\text{put}} =& \sigma_\text{ATMF} + 25\Delta\text{BF} - \frac{1}{2} 25\Delta\text{RR}
\end{align*}
$$

The corresponding implied volatilities at other delta levels can then be obtained by interpolation according to the market's specified volatility-surface convention.

For fixed spot, forward, maturity, interest rates, and other model inputs, there is a one-to-one relationship between an option's strike and its delta under the standard Black-type pricing framework. Consequently, a volatility quoted as a function of delta can be transformed into an implied-volatility representation as a function of strike.

### Put-call parity

Let $c_t (T, K)$ and $p_t (T-K)$ denote, respectively, the time-$t$ prices of a European call and put with common strike $K$ and maturity $T$. Assume that the two options have identical contractual terms other than the right to buy or sell the underlier.

In the case of a zero dividend underlier, the put-call parity states that

$$
  c_t (T, K) - p_t (T, K) = S_t^\text{fwd} (T) - Ke^{-r(T - t)}
$$

where $S_t^\text{fwd} (T)$ denotes the relevant forward price of the underlier for maturity $T$. Equivalently, if $F_t (T)$ denotes the forward price

$$
  c_t (T, K) - p_t (T, K) = e^{-r(T - t)} (F_t(T) - K)
$$

More generally, it is useful to define the value of a foward contract with delivery price $K$ by

$$
  f_t (T, K) = e^{-r(T - t)} (F_t (T) - K)
$$

Then put-call parity can be written compactly as

$$
  c_t (T, K) - p_t (T, K) = f_t (T, K),\, t \leq T
$$

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>

The result follows directly from the terminal payoff. At maturity, we have

$$
\begin{equation*}
  (S_T - K)^+ - (K - S_T)^+ = S_T - K,\, \forall T > 0
\tag{\label{equation-7}}
\end{equation*}
$$

Thus, a long call combined with a short put has exactly the same terminal payoff as a forward contract to buy the underlier for $K$ at $T$. By the no-arbitrage principle, two portfolios with identical cash flows must have the same value at every earlier time $t$.
</details>

If put-call parity were violated, we could construct an arbitrage by taking a long position in the cheaper portfolio and a short position in the more expensive portfolio, with the initial cash flows offsetting and the terminal cash flows cancelling exactly.

For a non-dividend-paying stock, if the relevant repo or financing carry is zero, the forward price is $F_t = S_t - e^{-r(T-t)} K$, so that $f_t (T, K) = S_t - Ke^{-r(T - t)}$. In this case, the put-call parity becomes

$$
  c_t (T, K) - p_t (T, K) = S_t - K e^{-r(T - t)}
$$

or, equivalently

$$
  c_t (T, K) + Ke^{-r(T - t)} = p_t (T, K) + S_t
$$

This latter form has a direct replication interpretation: a call combined with a zero-coupn bond paying $K$ at $T$ has the same payoff as a put combined with one unit of the underlier. If the underlier pays a continuous dividend yield $q$, the corresponding relation becomes

$$
\begin{equation*}
  c_t - p_t = S_t e^{-q(T-t)} - Ke^{-r(T-t)}
\tag{\label{equation-6}}
\end{equation*}
$$

More generally, the appropriate carry and discounting conventions depend on the asset class.

**Implication for implied volatility**

Put–call parity imposes a strong consistency condition on the implied volatilities of European calls and puts. Under a given arbitrage-free pricing model, if a call and put have the same strike $K$, maturity $T$, and underlying, and are priced using the same market inputs for rates and carry, then they must have the same implied volatility. Indeed, the difference between their prices $\eqref{equation-6}$ contains no volatility-dependent component.

This also implies that the vega of the call and put must be identical. For example, in the Black-Scholes-Merton model with continuous dividend yield $q$ the call and put vega are

$$
\begin{align*}
  \text{Vega}_\text{call} =& \frac{\partial c}{\partial\sigma} = S_t e^{-q(T - t)} \phi(d_1) (T - t)^{1/2} \\
  \text{Vega}_\text{put} =& \frac{\partial p}{\partial\sigma} = S_t e^{-q(T - t)} \phi(d_1) (T - t)^{1/2}
\end{align*}
$$

so that $\text{Vega}_\text{call} = \text{Vega}_\text{put}$.

Put–call parity therefore provides a particularly clear example of option decomposition: although a call and a put are individually nonlinear contingent claims, their difference eliminates the optionality,

$$
  \text{call} - \text{put} = \text{forward}
$$

because their nonlinear payoff components cancel as shown in $\eqref{equation-7}$.

For interest-rate derivatives, the analogous relationship is given by

$$
  \text{cap} - \text{floor} = \text{payer swap}
$$

provided the cap and floor have matching notionals, accrual periods, strikes, and reference rates. More precisely, this is an equality of the corresponding streams of cash flows: each caplet minus the corresponding floorlet produces the cash flow of a payer swap for that accrual period

$$
  N\tau_i (R_i - K)^+ - N \tau_i (K - R_i)^+ = N\tau_i (R_i - K)
$$

Summing across all periods gives the cash flows of the corresponding payer interest-rate swap.

### European option strategies

European options can be used to express specific views on the future price of an underlier. More generally any sufficiently regular European payoff at a fixed maturity can be represented, or approximated arbitrarily well, using a portfolio of vanilla calls, puts, and a position in the underlier and/or a risk-free asset.

The basic building blocks in option strategies are call and put payoffs. For a unit notional $N = 1$, their terminal payoffs are

$$
  \Pi_T^\text{call} = (S_T - K)^+,\quad \Pi_T^\text{put} = (K - S_T)^+
$$

As functions of $S_T$, these are piecewise-linear functions with slopes $\pm 1$ on their respective in-the-money regions and slope $0$ elsewhere. By combining options with different strikes and notionals, we can construct a wide range of piecewise-linear payoff profiles. In the limit, sufficiently regular nonlinear payoff can be represented using a continuum of vanilla options. This observation underlies the static replication of European contingent claims.

Below are some of the most common vanilla option strategies

#### Call spread

A call spread, or vertical bull call spread, consists of
- long one call with strike $K_1$
- short one call with strike $K_2$ 

with $K_1 < K_2$. Its payoff is

$$
  \Pi_T = (S_T - K_1)^+ - (S_T - K_2)^2
$$

Equivalently,

$$
  \Pi_T = \begin{cases}
    0,\quad& S_T \leq K_1 \\
    S_T - K_1,\quad& K_1 < S_T < K_2 \\
    K_2 - K_1,\quad& S_T \geq K_2
  \end{cases}
$$

The strategy provides a bullish exposure over the interval $[K_1, K_2]$, while limiting both its maximum profit and its cost relative to a standalone long call. When the two strikes are close, the value of the spread is particularly sensitive to the relative implied volatilities at the two strikes, and therefore to the local shape of the volatility smile or skew, rather than simply to the overall volatility level.

#### Put spread

A put spread, or vertical bear put spread, consists of
- long one put with strike $K_1$
- short one put with strike $K_2$

with $K_1 > K_2$. Its payoff is

$$
  \Pi_T = (K_1 - S_T)^+ - (K_2 - S_T)^+
$$

The position provides bearish exposure over the interval $[K_2, K_1]$, while limiting the maximum profit and reducing the premium relative to purchasing the $K_1$ put outright

#### Risk reversal

A risk reversal combines options with opposite directions and typically different strikes. A common structure is
- long one OTM call with strike $K_\text{call}$
- short one OTM put with strke $K_\text{put}$

with $K_\text{put} < S_0 < K_\text{call}$. The strikes are often selected so that the initial new premium is approximately zero. The resulting position provides asymmetric directional exposure: it benefits from a sufficiently large upward move and loses value if the underlier falls sufficiently far.

#### Butterfly

A standard call butterfly consists of
- long one call at $K_1$
- short two calls at $K_2$
- long one call at $K_3$

where $K_1 < K_2 < K_3$ and

$$
  K_2 = \frac{K_1 + K_3}{2}
$$

Its payoff is

$$
  \Pi_T = (S_T - K_1)^+ - 2(S_T - K_2)^+ + (S_T - K_3)^+
$$

The payoff is maximized when $S_T = K_2$ and vanishes when $S_T \leq K_1$ or $S_T \geq K_3$. Thus, a long butterfly profits when the underlier finishes near the middle strike $K_2$, relative to the initial cost of the strategy.

#### Collar

A collar typically consists of 
- a long position in the underlier
- a long OTM put
- a short ITM call

with the put strike below the current underlier price and the call strike above it. The put establishes a downside floor, while the short call limits the upside beyond the call strike. The premium received from selling the call can partially or fully offset the cost of the protective put.

#### Straddle

A long straddle consists of
- one long call
- one long put

with the same strike $K$ and maturity $T$. The strike is typically chosen close to the ATM level. Its payoff is

$$
\begin{align*}
  \Pi_T =& (S_T - K)^+ + (K - S_T)^+ \\
  =& |S_T - K|
\end{align*}
$$

The strategy therefore benefits from a sufficiently large move in either direction and is primarily an expression of a view that the realized movement of the underlier will exceed that implied by the option premiums.

#### Strangle

A long strangle consists of
- one long OTM call
- one long OTM pu

with the same maturity, but different strikes $K_\text{put} < S_0 < K_\text{call}$. Its payoff is

$$
  \Pi_T = (K_\text{put} - S_T)^+ + (S_T - K_\text{call})^+
$$

#### Call ratio spread

A call ratio spread combines calls in unequal quantities. A common $1\times 2$ structure consists of
- long one call at $K_1$
- short two calls at $K_2$

with $K_1 < K_2$. Its payoff is

$$
  \Pi_T = (S_T - K_1)^+ - 2(S_T - K_2)^+
$$

Unlike a standard call spread, the short-call notional exceeds the long-call notional. Consequently, the strategy can have unlimited downside for sufficiently large increases in the underlier price. This feature is important when assessing the risk of ratio spreads.

#### Covered call

A covered call, or buy/write, consists of
- a long position in the underlier
- a short call on the same underlier, typically with an OTM strike

The strategy generates premium income from selling the call in exchange for giving up some of the upside beyond the call strike. It is commonly used when the investor has a neutral to moderately bullish view and is willing to cap the upside in exchange for option premium.

The short call is economically covered by the long underlier position: if the call is exercised, the investor can deliver the underlying asset. This reduces the need for additional collateral relative to an uncovered short call, although the precise collateral and margin treatment depends on the market and counterparty arrangement.

#### Calendar spread

A calendar spread, or horizontal spread, consists of otherwise similar options with different expiration dates. For example, a call calendar spread may involve
- buying a longer-dated call
- selling a shorter-dated call

typically with the same strike. Because the two options have different maturities, the position is particularly sensitive to the term structure of implied volatility and to the evolution of volatility over time. Calendar spreads can therefore be used to express views on the relative pricing of different maturities and, in appropriate settings, on forward volatility.

The term horizontal comes from representing maturity on the horizontal axis of a diagram, in contrast to a vertical spread, where the maturity is fixed and the strikes differ.

#### Directional trading with options

Market participants, including hedge funds, may use slightly OTM calls or puts to obtain directional exposure to an underlier. Relative to an ATM option, an OTM option generally requires a lower upfront premium, although this does not necessarily mean that it provides “cheaper” directional exposure in an economically equivalent sense. Its delta, gamma, probability of finishing ITM, and sensitivity to volatility and time decay are all different.

In many trading strategies, the option position is intended to express a view over a finite horizon rather than to be held until expiration. The position may therefore be unwound, rolled, or otherwise offset before maturity. For this reason, liquidity and transaction costs are important considerations when selecting an option. Highly liquid standardized options are generally easier to enter and exit at competitive prices than bespoke OTC or exotic options, whose secondary-market liquidity can be limited.

Thus, the choice between a vanilla and an exotic option is not determined solely by the desired terminal payoff. It also depends on the liquidity, hedging requirements, transaction costs, model risk, and expected holding period of the position.

### American options

An American option may be exercised by its holder at any time to and including its expiration date $T$. If the option can be exercised only at a specified finite set of dates (e.g., every month), it is called a Bermudan option. However, in some market contexts, options with discrete exercise opportunities are sometimes informally called American options.

For an American option, the holder must determine the optimal exercise time. At any exercise opportunity t, immediate exercise is optimal when the exercise value is at least as large as the value of retaining the option. More formally, if $E_t$ denotes the immediate exercise value and $V_t$ the value of continuing to hold the options, exercise is optimal whenever $E_t \geq V_t^\text{cont}$, where $V_t^\text{cont}$ denotes the value conditional on not exercising at $t$.

**American versus European options**

Let $c_t$ and $p_t$ denote the time-$t$ price of the European call option and put option with strike $K$ and maturity $T$, while $C_t$ and $P_t$ denote the corresponding American option prices. Due to its exercise right at the discretion of option holder, an American option's price should be at least the price of its counterpart European options, i.e.

$$
\begin{equation*}
  C_t \geq c_t,\quad P_t \geq p_t
\tag{\label{equation-8}}
\end{equation*}
$$

The inequality follows directly from the fact that the holder of the American option can always choose to exercise only at $T$. Thus, the set of admissible exercise strategies for the American option contains the European exercise strategy as a special case.

**Early exercise of an American call**

The price of an American call is equal to the price of the corresponding European call under the following conditions:
- dividend and repo rate are all zero for the underlier during the tenor of the option
- the continuously compounded risk-free interes rate $r$ is non-negative.

Under these conditions early exercise of the call is never optimal and therefore

$$
  C_t = c_t
$$

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>

We first show that the European call prices has the lower bound

$$
  c_t \geq (S_t - K e^{-r(T-t)})^+,\, \forall t < T
$$

Define two portfolios at time $t < T$
- Portfolio $A$: Long one European call $c_t$ together with a zero-coupon bond with the value $Ke^{-r(T-t)}$, so that the bond pays $K$ at $T$
- Portfolio $B$: Long one share $S_t$ with zero dividend yield

At maturity $T$, the porfolio payoffs are
- Portfolio $A$: $(S_T - K)^+ + K = \max\set{S_T, K}$
- Portfolio $B$: $S_T$

Since $\max\set{S_T, K} \geq S_T$, Portfolio $A$ dominates Portfolio $B$ at maturity. By the no-arbitrage principle

$$
  c_t + K e^{-r(T-t)} \geq S_t
$$

or, because $c_t \geq 0$

$$
\begin{equation*}
  c_t \geq (S_t - Ke^{-r(T-t)})^+
\tag{\label{equation-9}}
\end{equation*}
$$

Because $r \geq 0$, it follows that $e^{-r(T-t)} \leq 1$ and therefore

$$
  S_T - Ke^{-r(T-t)} \geq S_t - K
$$

Taking positive parts gives

$$
  (S_T - Ke^{-r(T-t)})^+ \geq (S_t - K)^+
$$

From the inequalities $\eqref{equation-8}$ and $\eqref{equation-9}$

$$
  C_t \geq c_t \geq (S_t - Ke^{-r(T-t)})^+ \geq (S_t - K)^+
$$

Because the early exercise value of the American call is $(S_t - K)^+$, exercising the call before maturity cannot be optimal. Since early exercise provides no additional value, the American and European calls have the same value, i.e.

$$
  C_t = c_t,\, \forall t < T
$$
</details>

The economic intuition is straightforward. Exercising the call early requires paying the strike $K$ before maturity and gives up the remaining optionality. If the stock pays no dividends, there is no benefit from owning the stock earlier. Moreover, when $r \geq 0$, postponing the payment of $K$ has non-negative financing value. If the holder wishes to monetize an appreciated call position before maturity, selling the option is therefore preferable to exercising it: selling preserves the remaining time value, whereas exercise destroys it.

For a given underlying, strike, and maturity, an American option provides at least as much value to its holder as the corresponding European option because it offers a larger set of exercise opportunities. Consequently,

$$
  V_t^\text{American} \geq V_t^\text{European}
$$

However, this does not imply that an American option is always economically preferable at the same quoted premium. In a frictionless market, the premium should reflect the additional value associated with the early-exercise right. Moreover, the possibility of terminating a European option before maturity should not generally be described as an “unwind” at a value below intrinsic value: the market value of a European option is itself constrained by no-arbitrage bounds and, under standard assumptions, is at least its intrinsic value. An OTC unwind may involve transaction costs, bid–ask spreads, funding costs, or other contractual adjustments, but these are separate from the fundamental distinction between American and European exercise rights.

Thus, the essential advantage of an American option is not that it guarantees a higher liquidation value at any time, but that it gives the holder additional exercise rights. The economic value of those rights is reflected in the American option's price relative to the corresponding European option.

## Exotic options
