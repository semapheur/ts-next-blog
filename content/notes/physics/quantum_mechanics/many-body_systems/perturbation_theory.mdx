# Projection operators

Let $\mathcal{H}$ be a Hilbert space and let $\set{\ket{\Phi}_i}$ denote an orthonormal complete eigenbasis of the unperturbed Hamiltonian

$$
\begin{equation*}
  \hat{H}_0 \ket{\Phi_i} = E_i^{(0)} \ket{\Phi_i},\, \braket{\Phi_i|\Phi_j} = \delta_{ij}
\tag{\label{equation-15}}
\end{equation*}
$$

We single out the reference state $\ket{\Phi_0}$ and define the projection operators 

$$
  \hat{P} = \ket{\Phi_0} \bra{\Phi_0}
$$

and

$$
  \hat{Q} = \hat{I} - \hat{P} = \sum_{i\neq 0} \ket{\Phi_i} \bra{\Phi_i}
$$

We introduce primed summation to denote omission of the $i=0$ term, i.e. $\sum'_i := \sum_{i\neq 0}$. 

These projection operators are linear, Hermitian and idempotent. For $\hat{P}$

$$
  \hat{P}^2 = \ket{\Phi_0} \braket{\Phi_0|\Phi_0} \bra{\Phi_0} = \ket{\Phi_0} \bra{\Phi_0} = \hat{P}
$$

whereas

$$
  \hat{P}^\dagger = (\ket{\Phi_0} \bra{\Phi_0})^\dagger = \ket{\Phi_0}\bra{\Phi_0} = \hat{P}
$$

For $\hat{Q}$

$$
  \hat{Q}^2 = (\hat{I} - \hat{P})^2 = \hat{I} - \hat{P} - \hat{P} + \hat{P}^2 = \hat{I} - \hat{P} = \hat{Q}
$$

Moreover,

$$
  \hat{P}\hat{Q} = \hat{P}(\hat{I} - \hat{P}) = \hat{P} - \hat{P}^2 = 0
$$

and
 
$$
  \hat{Q}\hat{P} = (\hat{I} - \hat{P})\hat{P} = \hat{P} - \hat{P}^2 = 0
$$

Thus, $\hat{P}$ and $\hat{Q}$ are complementary orthogonal projection operators satisfying

$$
\begin{align*}
  \hat{P}^2 =& \hat{P} \\
  \hat{Q}^2 =& \hat{Q} \\
  \hat{P}\hat{Q} = \hat{Q}\hat{P}
\end{align*}
$$

The property 

$$
\begin{equation*}
  \hat{P} + \hat{Q} = \hat{I}
\tag{\label{equation-17}}
\end{equation*}
$$

is called the resolution of the identity associated with the decomposition $\mathcal{H} = \operatorname{ran}(\hat{P}) \oplus \operatorname{ran}(\hat{Q})$.

## Projection operators for a nonorthogonal basis

The orthonormality of the eigenbasis $\set{\ket{\Phi_i}}$ is not required to construct a projector onto a subspace. Suppose instead that $\set{\ket{\Phi_i}}_{i\neq 0}$ is a linearly independent, generally nonorthogonal set spanning a subspace $Q$, while $\ket{\Phi_0}$ is orthogonal to that subspace. Then the projector onto the reference state is

$$
  \hat{P} = \ket{\Phi_0} \frac{1}{\braket{\Phi_0|\Phi_0}} \bra{\Phi_0}
$$
  
To express the projector onto the complementary subspace, introduce the column vetor of basis kets

$$
  \ket{\boldsymbol{Phi}} = \begin{bmatrix} \ket{\Phi_1} \\ \ket{\Phi_2} \\ \vdots \end{bmatrix}
$$

with corresponding row vector

$$
  \bra{\boldsymbol{\Phi}} = \begin{bmatrix} \ket{\Phi_1} & \ket{\Phi_2} & \cdots \end{bmatrix}
$$

The overlap matrix is 

$$
  S_{ij} = \braket{\Phi_i|\Phi_j},\, i, j \neq 0
$$

Provided the basis functions are linearly independent, $\mathbf{S}$ is invertible, and the projector onto their span is

$$
  \hat{Q} =& \sum_{i,j \neq 0} \ket{\Phi_i} (S^{-1})_{ij} \bra{\Phi_j} = \ket{\boldsymbol{\Phi}} \mathbf{S}^{-1} \bra{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}
$$

We can verify idempotency directly

$$
\begin{align*}
  \hat{Q}^2 =& \sum_{i,j,k,l} \ket{\Phi_i} (S^{-1})_{ij} \braket{\Phi_j|\Phi_k} (S^{-1})_{kl} \bra{\Phi_l} \\
  =& \sum_{i,l} \ket{\Phi_i} (S^{-1} SS^{-1})_{il} \bra{\Phi_l} \\
  =& \hat{Q}
\end{align*}
$$

## Projector actions

Consider a state $\ket{\Psi} \in\mathcal{H}$ expanded in the orthonormal eigenbasis as

$$
  \ket{\Psi} = \sum_i a_i \ket{\Phi_i}
$$

Applying $\hat{P}$ gives

$$
\begin{align*}
  \hat{P}\ket{\Psi} =& \sum_i a_i \bra{\Phi_0} \braket{\Phi_0|\Phi_i} \\
  =& \sum_i a_i \ket{\Phi_0} \delta_{0i} = a_0 \ket{\Phi_0}
\end{align*}
$$

showing that $\hat{P}$ extracts the component of $\ket{\Psi}$ along $\ket{\Phi_0}$. Similarly,

$$
\begin{align*}
  \hat{Q}\ket{\Psi} =& (\hat{I} - \hat{P})\ket{\Psi} \\
  =& \ket{\Psi} - a_0 \ket{\Phi_0} = \sum'_i a_i \ket{\Phi_i}
\end{align*}
$$

showing that $\hat{Q}$ removes the component of $\ket{\Psi}$ parallel to $\ket{\Phi_0}$ and projects it onto the orthogonal complement of the reference state.

Using the resolution of the identity, every state can be decomposed uniquely as

$$
\begin{align*}
  \ket{\Psi} =& \hat{I} \ket{\Psi} = (\hat{P} + \hat{Q})\ket{\Psi} \\
  =& \hat{P}\ket{\Psi} + \hat{Q}\ket{\Psi}
\end{align*}
$$

## Decomposition of an operator

Similarly, the resolution of the identity allows any operator on $\mathcal{H}$ to be resolved into four components

$$
\begin{align*}
  \hat{A} =& (\hat{P} + \hat{Q}) \hat{A} (\hat{P} + \hat{Q}) \\
  =& \underbrace{\hat{P}\hat{A}\hat{P} + \hat{Q}\hat{A}\hat{Q}}_{\hat{A}_\text{D}} + \underbrace{\hat{P}\hat{A}\hat{Q} + \hat{Q}\hat{A}\hat{Q} + \hat{Q}\hat{A}\hat{P}}_{\hat{A}_\text{X}}
\end{align*}
$$

where $\hat{A}_\text{D} = \hat{P}\hat{A}\hat{P} + \hat{Q}\hat{A}\hat{Q}$ is the block-diagonal, or even, part of $\hat{A}$ and $\hat{A}_\text{X} = \hat{P}\hat{A}\hat{Q} + \hat{Q}\hat{A}\hat{P}$ is the off-diagonal, cross, or odd part.

If $\hat{A} = \hat{A}_\text{D} + \hat{A}_\text{X}$ and $\hat{B} = \hat{B}_\text{D} + \hat{B}_\text{X}$, then

$$
\begin{align*}
  (\hat{A}\hat{B})_\text{D} =& \hat{A}_\text{D} \hat{B}_\text{D} + \hat{A}_\text{X} \hat{B}_\text{X} \\
  (\hat{A}\hat{B})_\text{X} =& \hat{A}_\text{D} \hat{B}_\text{X} + \hat{A}_\text{X} \hat{B}_\text{D}
\end{align*}
$$

## Commutation with the unperturbed Hamiltonian

The projection operators $\hat{P}$ and $\hat{Q}$ commute with $\hat{H}_0$. To see this, not that since $\ket{\Phi_0}$ is an eigenstate of $\hat{H}_0$ satisfying $\eqref{equation-15}$, we have

$$
\begin{align*}
  \hat{H}_0 \hat{P} =& \hat{H}_0 \ket{\Phi_0} \bra{\Phi_0} \\
  =& E_0^{(0)} \ket{\Phi_0} \bra{\Phi_0} \\
  =& E_0^{(0)} \hat{P}
\end{align*}
$$

Likewise

$$
\begin{align*}
  \hat{P}\hat{H}_0 =& \ket{\Phi_0} \bra{\Phi_0} \hat{H}_0 \\
  =& E_0^{(0)} \ket{\Phi_0} \bra{\Phi_0} \\
  =& E_0^{(0)} \hat{P}
\end{align*}
$$

where we have used the Hermiticity of $\hat{H}_0$

$$
  \bra{\Psi_0} \hat{H}_0 = E_0^{(0)} \bra{\Psi_0}
$$

Thus, $[\hat{P}, \hat{H}_0] = 0$. Since $\hat{Q} = \hat{I} - \hat{P}$ it follows immediately that 

$$
\begin{align*}
  [\hat{Q}, \hat{H}_0] =& [\hat{I} - \hat{P}, \hat{H}_0] \\
  =& -[\hat{P}, \hat{H}_0] = 0
\end{align*}
$$

Hence, $[\hat{P}, \hat{H}_0] = [\hat{Q}, \hat{H}_0] = 0$.

# Time-independent perturbation theory

To derive Rayleigh-Schrödinger perturbation theory we decompose the Hamiltonian $\hat{H}$ into an exactly solvable reference Hamiltonian $\hat{H}_0$ and a perturbation $\hat{V}$

$$
\begin{equation*}
  \hat{H} = \hat{H}_0 + \hat{V}
\tag{\label{equation-4}}
\end{equation*}
$$

For bookkeeping purposes, it is often convenient to introduce a formal perturbation parameter $\lambda$

$$
  \hat{H}(\lambda) = \hat{H}_0 + \lambda\hat{V}
$$

where $\lambda$ serves only to classify terms according to their perturbative oerder. It is not a physical parameter and is set to $\lambda = 1$ after the perturbation expansion has been carried out.

The exact eigenstates and eigenvalues satisfy the stationary Schrödinger equation

$$
\begin{equation*}
  \hat{H}\ket{\Psi_n} = E_n \ket{\Psi_n}
\tag{\label{equation-3}}
\end{equation*}
$$

whereas the unperturbed eigenvalue problem is defined by

$$
\begin{equation*}
  \hat{H}_0 \ket{\Phi_n} = E_n^{(0)} \ket{\Phi_n}
\tag{\label{equation-5}}
\end{equation*}
$$
owhe the eigenstates chosen to be orthonormal, $\braket{\Phi_m|\Phi_n} = \delta_{mn}$.

Assuming that the unperturbed eigenvalue $E_n^{0}$ is nondegenerate, the exact eigenvalues and vector can be labelled continuously such that

$$
\begin{align*}
  \lim_{\lambda\to 0} \ket{\Psi_n (\lambda)} =& \ket{\Phi_n} \\
  \lim_{\lambda\to 0}  E_n (\lambda) =& E_n^{(0)}
\end{align*}
$$

We define the quantum state and energy corrections by

$$
\begin{equation*}
\begin{split}
  \ket{\chi_n} =& \ket{\Psi_n} - \ket{\Phi}_n \\
  \Delta E_n =& E_n - E_n^{(0)}
\end{split}
\tag{\label{equation-8}}
\end{equation*}
$$

Substituting $\ket{\Psi_n} = \ket{\Phi_n} + \ket{\chi_n}$ into the exact Schrödinger equation $\eqref{equation-3}$ gives

$$
  \hat{H}(\ket{\Phi_n} + \ket{\chi_n}) = E_n (\ket{\Phi_n} + \ket{\chi_n})
$$

Using $\eqref{equation-4}$ and $\eqref{equation-5}$ and rearranging, we obtain

$$
\begin{equation*}
  (\hat{H} - E_n) \ket{\chi_n} = (\Delta E_n - \hat{V})\ket{\Phi_n}
\tag{\label{equation-1}}
\end{equation*}
$$

If the exact energy $E_n$ were known, then this would become  an inhomogeneous linear differential equation for the unknown correction $\ket{\chi_n}$. As for any linear operator equation, its general solution is the sum of a particular solution and an arbitrary solution of the associated homogeneous equation

$$
\begin{equation*}
  (\hat{H} - E_n)\ket{f} = 0
\tag{\label{equation-2}}
\end{equation*}
$$

To see this, consider the general equation

$$
  \hat{A}\ket{f} = \ket{g}
$$

where $\hat{A}$ is a linear operator and $\ket{g}$ is known. If $\ket{f_0}$ is a particular solution, $\hat{A}\ket{f_0} = \ket{g}$, and $\ket{h}$ satisfies the corresponding homogeneous equation $\hat{A}\ket{h} = 0$, then

$$
  \ket{f} = \ket{f_0} + c\ket{h}
$$

where $c$ is an arbitrary scalar, is also a solution since

$$
\begin{align*}
  \hat{A}\ket{f} =& \hat{A} \ket{f_0} + c\hat{A} \ket{h} \\
  =& \ket{g}
\end{align*}
$$

The homogeneous equation $\eqref{equation-2}$ is simply the eigenvalue equation for the exact Hamiltonian. Since $E_n$ is assumed to be a nondegenaret eigenvalue, the null space of the operator $\hat{H} - E_n$ is one-dimensional and is therefore spanned by the exact eigenstate $\ket{\Psi_n}$. Consequently, if $\ket{\chi_n}$ is a particular solutino of $\eqref{equation-1}$, then $\ket{\chi_n} + c\ket{\Psi_n}$ is also a solution for any scalar $c$. Thus the decomposition $\ket{\Psi_n} = \ket{\Phi_n} + \ket{\chi_n}$ is not unique. This freedom can be removed by imposing the orthogonality condition

$$
  \braket{\chi_n|\Phi_n} = 0
$$

Geometrically, this decomposes the exact eigenstate into a component parallel to the reference state $\ket{\Phi_n}$ and a component lying entirely in its orthogonal complement. Thus, $\ket{\chi_n}$ contains only the portion of the exact wavefunction that cannot be absorbed into an overall rescaling of the unperturbed state.

Since the normalization of an eigenvector is arbitrary, it is convenient to adopt the intermediate normalization convention

$$
\begin{equation*}
  \braket{\Phi_n|\Psi_n} = 1
\tag{\label{equation-7}}
\end{equation*}
$$

Together with

$$
  \braket{\Phi_n|\Phi_n} = 1,\quad \braket{\Phi_n|\chi_n} = 0
$$

this immediately gives

$$
  \braket{\Phi_n|\Psi_n} = \braket{\Phi_n|\Phi_n} + \braket{\Phi_n + \chi_n} = 1
$$

Notice that the exact state is not normalized under this convention. Indeed,

$$
\begin{align*}
  \braket{\Psi_n|\Psi_n} =& \braket{\Phi_n + \chi_n|\Phi_n + \chi_n} \\
  =& 1 + \braket{\chi_n|\chi_n}
\end{align*}
$$

since the cross terms vanish by orthogonality. Intermediate normalization therefore fixes the otherwise arbitrary component of the exact eigenstate along $\ket{\Psi_n}$ while leaving the overall norm of $\ket{\Psi_n}$ unconstrained. This convention greatly simplifies the subsequent perturbation expansion and is the standard normalization used in Rayleigh-Schrödinger perturbation theory.

From the intermediate normalization condition $\eqref{equation-7}$, we obtain a simple expression for the exact energy. Left-multiplying the exact Schrödinger equation $\erqef{equation-3}$ by $\bra{\Phi_n}$ gives

$$
  \braket{\Phi_n|\hat{H}|\Psi_n} = E_n \underbrace{\braket{\Phi_n|\Psi_n}}_{=1} = E_n
$$

Similarly, left-multiplying the unperturbed eigenvalue equation $\eqref{equation-5}$ by $\bra{\Psi_n}$ and using the Hermiticity of $\hat{H}_0$, we have

$$
\begin{align*}
  \braket{\Phi_n|\hat{H}_0|\Psi_n} =& \braket{\hat{H}_0 \Psi_n|\Psi_n} = E_n^{(0)} \underbrace{\braket{\Phi_n|\Psi_n}}_{=1} = E_n^{(0)}
\end{align*}
$$

Subtracting these two expressions yields

$$
\begin{align*}
  \Delta E_n =& E_n - E_n^{(0)} \\
  =& \braket{\Phi_n|\hat{H} - \hat{H}_0|\Psi_n} \\
  =& \braket{\Phi_n|\hat{V}|\Psi_n}
\end{align*}
$$

Thus

$$
  \Delta = \braket{\Phi_n|\hat{V}|\Psi_n} \\
$$

and

$$
\begin{equation*}
  E_n = \braket{\Phi_n|\hat{H}|\Psi_n}
\tag{\label{equation-6}}
\end{equation*}
$$

Equation $\eqref{equation-6}$ is called the transition matrix element form (or projected form) of the energy. If $\ket{\Psi_n}$ is the exact eigenstate of $\hat{H}$, then the energy obtained from this expression is exact. However, unlike the Rayleigh quotient, $\eqref{equation-6}$ is not variational. Consequently, if an approximate eigenstate

$$
  \ket{\tilde{\Psi}_n} = \ket{\Psi_n} + O(\epsilon)
$$

is substituted into $\eqref{equation-6}$, the resulting energy error is generally of first order

$$
  \tilde{E}_n - E_n = O(\epsilon)
$$

In contrast, the variatonal energy

$$
\begin{align*}
  E_\text{var} = \frac{\braket{\tilde{\Psi}_n}|\hat{H}|\tilde{\Psi}_n}{\braket{\tilde{\Psi}_n|\tilde{\Psi}_n}}
\end{align*}
$$

is stationary with respect to the first-order variation of the eigenstate. As a consequency, provided the approximate wavefunction is properly normalized, the corresponding energy error is second order

$$
  E_\text{var} = E_n + O(\epsilon^2)
$$

This distinction is one of the principal differences between the projected energy and the variational energy expressions.

An important consequence of $\eqref{equation-6}$ arises when $\ket{\Psi_n}$ is expanded as a full configuration interaction wavefunction with reference determinant $\ket{\Phi_n}$

$$
  \ket{\Psi_n} = c_0 \ket{\Phi_n} + \sum_I c_I \ket{\Phi_I}
$$

Because the Hamiltonian is a two-body operator, the Slater-Condon rules imply that

$$
  \braket{\Phi_n|\hat{H}|\Phi_I} = 0
$$

whenever $\ket{\Phi_I}$ differs from the reference determinant by more than two spin orbitals. Thus, the projected energy depends only on the reference coefficient together with the coefficients of the singly and doubly excited determinants. Although the exact FCI wavefunction contains excitations of all orders, the coefficients of triple and higher excitations do not contribute directly to the projected energy: their influence is only indirect through coupling to the lower-excitation coefficients.

## Order-by-order expansion

Expanding the eigenstate $\ket{\Psi_n}$ and eigenvalue $E_n$ in powers of the pertubation parameter $\lambda$ gives

$$
\begin{align*}
  \ket{\Psi_n} =& \ket{\Phi_n} + \chi_n = \sum_{j=1}^\infty \lambda^j \ket{\Psi_n^{(j)}} \\
  E_n =& E_n^{(0)} + \Delta E_n = \sum_{j=1}^\infty \lambda^j E_n^{(j)} \\
\end{align*}
$$

Substituting these expansions into the Schrödinger equation $(\hat{H} - E_n) \ket{\Psi_n} = 0$ with $\hat{H} = \hat{H}_0 + \lambda\hat{V}$, we get

$$
  \left(\hat{H}_0 + \lambda\hat{V} - \sum_{j=1}^\infty \lambda^j E_n^{(j)} \right) \left( \sum_{k=1}^n \lambda^k \ket{\Psi_n^{(k)}} \right) = 0
$$

Equating coefficients of powers of $\lambda$ for $m\geq 1$ yields

$$
  (\hat{H}_0 - E_n^{(0)}) \ket{\Psi_n^{(m)}} = (E_n^{(1)} - \hat{V}) \ket{\Psi_n^{(m-1)}} + \sum_{l=0}^{m-2} E_n^{(m-l)} \ket{\Psi_n^{(l)}}
$$

Separating the term with $l = m$ and rearranging yields the $m$th order perturbation equation

$$
\begin{equation*}
  (E_n^{(0)} - \hat{H}_0) \ket{\Psi_n^{(m)}} = \hat{V} \ket{\Psi_n^{(m-1)}} - \sum_{l=0}^{m-1} E_n^{(m-l)} \ket{\Psi_n^{(l)}}
\tag{\label{equation-9}}
\end{equation*}
$$

In order to get the $m$th order energy correction $E_n^{(m)}$, we project this equation onto the unperturbed state $\bra{\Phi_n}$ to each equation and integrate

$$
  \underbrace{\braket{\Phi_n |E_n^{(0)} - \hat{H}_0 | \Psi_n^{(m)}}}_{=0} = \braket{\Phi_n |\hat{V}|\Psi_n^{(m-1)}} - \sum_{l=0}^{m-1} E_n^{(m-l)} \underbrace{\Phi_n |\Psi_n^{(l)}}_{\delta_{l0}}
$$

giving

$$
\begin{equation*}
  E_n^{(m)} = \braket{\Phi_n |\hat{V} |\Psi_n^{(m-1)}}
\tag{\label{equation-11}}
\end{equation*}
$$

## Expansion in zero-order functions

The inhomogeneous differential equations for the higher-order corrections $\ket{\Psi_n^{(m)}}$ are generally difficult to solve directly, although they are more tractable than the original eigenvale problem. A convenient approach is to expand each correction in the complete set of eigenstates $\set{\ket{\Phi_k}}_{k=1}^\infty$ of the unperturbed Hamiltonian $\hat{H}_0$

$$
  \ket{\Psi_n^{(m)}} =& \sum_k a_{kn}^{(m)} \braket{\Phi_k|\Psi_n^{(m)}}
$$

where the expansion coefficients area $a_{kn}^{(m)} = \braket{\Phi_k |\Psi_n^{(m)}}$. Projecting the $m$-th order equation $\eqref{equation-9}$ onto $\ket{\Phi_k}$ and integrating

$$
\begin{align*}
  \braket{\underbrace{\Phi_k |E_n^{(0)} - \hat{H}_0 |}_{=(E_n^{(0)} - E_k^{(0)}\bra{\Phi_k}} \Psi_n^{(m)}} =& \underbrace{\braket{\Phi_k |\hat{V}| \Psi_n^{(m-1)}}}_{\sum_j \braket{\Phi_k |\hat{V}| \Psi_j}\braket{\Phi_j |\Psi_n^{(m-1)}}} \\
  &- \sum_{l=0}^{m-1} E_n^{(m-l)} \underbrace{\braket{\Phi_k |\Psi_n^{(l)}}}_{a_{kn}^{(l)}}
\end{align*}
$$

Thus, the recursion relation for the expansion coefficients is

$$
\begin{equation*}
  (E_n^{(0)} - E_k^{(0)}) a_{kn}^{(m)} = \sum_j V_{kj} a_{jn}^{(m-1)} - \sum_{l=0}^{m-1} E_n^{(m-l)} a_{kn}^{(l)}
\tag{\label{equation-10}}
\end{equation*}
$$

where we introduce the matrix elements $V_{kj} := \braket{\Phi_k |\hat{V}|\Phi_j}$.

For $m=0$, the unperturbed state is $\ket{\Psi_n^{(0)} = \ket{\Phi_n}$, so 

$$
  a_{kn}^{(0)} = \braket{\Phi_k |\Phi_n} = \delta_{kn}
$$

An important feature of $\eqref{equation-10}$ is that, for $k = n$, its left-hand side vanishes

$$
  (E_^{(0)} - E_n^{(0)}) a_{nn}^{(m)} = 0
$$

Thus, the projected equation does not determine $a_{nn}^{(m)}$. This reflects the fact that the inhomogeneous equatin for $\ket{\Psi_n^{(m)}}$ determines the correction only up to addition of an arbitrary multiple of the homogeneous solution $\ket{\Phi_n}$. This freedom can be fixed by imposing intermediate normalization

$$
\begin{align*}
  \braket{\Phi_n |\Psi_n^{(0)}} =& 1 \\
  \braket{\Phi_n |\Psi_n^{(m)}} =& 0,\, m > 0
\end{align*}
$$

so that $a_{nn}^{(m)} = \delta_{m0}$. 

### First-order eigenstate

For $m=1}$ the recursion relation $\eqref{equation-9}$ becomes

$$
  (E_n^{(0)} - E_k^{(0)}) a_{kn}^{(1)} =& \sum_j V_{kj} a_{jn}^{(0)} - E_n^{(1)} a_{kn}^{(0)}
$$

Since $a_{kn}^{(0)} = \delta_{kn}$, this equation reduces to

$$
  (E_n^{(0)} - E_k^{(0)}) a_{kn}^{(1)} =& V_{kn} - E_n^{(1)} a_{kn}^{(0)}
$$

For $n\neq k$, the second term vanishes, giving

$$
\begin{equation*}
  a_{kn}^{(1)} = \frac{V_{kn}}{E_n^{(0)} - E_k^{(0)}}
\tag{\label{equation-13}}
\end{equation*}
$$

Together with $a_{nn}^{(0)}$, the first-order correction to the eigenstate is therefore

$$
  \ket{\Psi_n^{(1)}} = \sum'_k \frac{V_{kn}}{E_n^{(0)} - E_k^{(0)}} \ket{\Phi_k}
$$

where $\sum'_k := \sum_{k, k\neq n}$. Using $\eqref{equation-11}$, we get the second-order energy

$$
\begin{align*}
  E_n^{(2)} =& \braket{\Phi_n |\hat{V}|\Psi_n^{(1)}} = \sum_k a_{kn}^{(1)} V_{nk} \\
  =& \sum'_k \frac{V_{nk} V_{kn}}{E_n^{(0)} - E_k^{(0)}}
\end{align*}
$$

Since $\hat{V}$ is Hermitian, $V_{nk} = V_{kn}^*$ and $V_{nk} V_{kn} = |V_{kn}|^2$, resulting in

$$
  E_n^{(2)} = \sum'_k \frac{|V_{kn}|^2}{E_n^{(0)} - E_k^{(0)}}
$$

### Second-order wavefunction

For $m=2$, the recursion relation $\eqref{equation-2}$ becomes

$$
  (E_n^{(0)} - E_k^{(0)}) a_{kn}^{(2)} = \sum'_j V_{kj} a_{jn}^{(1)} - E_n^{(1)} a_{kn}^{(1)} - E_n^{(2)} a_{kn}^{(0)}
$$

For $k \neq n$, we have $a_{kn}^{(0)} = 0$, and therefore

$$
\begin{equation*}
  a_{kn}^{(2)} =& \frac{E_n^{(0)} - E_k^{(0)}} \left[ \sum'_j V_{kj} a_{jn}^{(1)} - E_n^{(1)} a_{kn}^{(1)} \right] \\
  \underbrace{=}_{\eqref{equation-13}}& \sum'_j \frac{V_{kj} V_{jn}}{(E_n^{(0)} - E_k^{(0)}) (E_n^{(0)} - E_j^{(0)})} - \frac{V_{kn} V_{nn}}{(E_n^{(0)} - E_k^{(0)})^2}
\end{equation*}
$$
 
where we have used the first-order energy correction $E_n^{(1)} = V_{nn}$.

## Wigner's $2m + 1$ rule

Wigner's $2m+1$ rule states that knowledge of eigenstate corrections $\ket{\Psi_n^{(l)}}$ for $l=1,\dots,m$ is sufficient to determine the energy corrections $E_n^{(l)}$ for all $l = 1,\dots, 2m + 1$. This result can be established by a series of isomorphic transformations of the $m$th order energy contribution $E_n^{(m)} = \braket{\Phi_n |\hat{V} |\Psi_n^{(m-1)}}$. Using $\eqref{equation-9}$ for the $m$th order correction $\ket{\Psi_n^{(m)}}$, allows us raise the perturbative order of the dual space (bra) part while lowering the order of the state space (ket), repeatedly until the two orders are equal (if $m$ is odd) or differ by unity (if $m$ is even).

Consider the first step of this recursive procedure. Using the Hermiticity of the perturbative Hamiltonian, $\eqref{equation-11}$ can be written
  
$$
\begin{equation*}
  E_n^{(m)} = \braket{\hat{V} \Phi_n |\Psi_n^{(m-1)}}
\tag{\label{equation-12}}
\end{equation*}
$$

From $\eqref{equation-9}$, the first-order pertrubation equation can be written

$$
  \hat{V} \Phi_n = (E_n^{(0)} - \hat{H}_0) \ket{\Psi_n^{(1)}} + E_n^{(1)} \ket{\Phi_n}
$$

Substituting this into $\eqref{equation-12}$ yields

$$
  E_n^{(m)} = \braket{(E_n^{(0)} - \hat{H}_0) \Psi_n^{(1)} |\Psi_n^{(m-1)}} + E_n^{(1)} \braket{\Phi_n |\Psi_n^{(m-1)}}
$$

For $m > 1$, the last term vanishes under intermediate normalization. From $\eqref{equation-9}$, the $(m-1)$th order perturbation equation gives

$$
  (E_n^{(0)} - \hat{H}_0) \ket{\Psi_n^{(m-1)}} = \hat{V} \ket{\Psi_n^{(m-2)}} - \sum_{l=0}^{m-2} E_n^{(m-1-l)} \ket{\Psi_n^{(l)}}
$$

Substituting this into $\eqref{equation-13}$ and noting that

$$
  \braket{\Psi_n^{(1)}|\Psi_n^{(0)}} = \braket{\Psi_n^{(1)}|\Phi_n} = 0
$$

we obtain


$$
  E_n^{(m)} = \braket{\Psi_n^{(1)}|\hat{V}|\Psi_n^{(m-2)}} - \sum_{l=1}^{m-2} E_n^{(m-l-1)} \braket{\Psi_n^{(1)}|\Psi_n^{(l)}}
$$

Repeating this procedure $k$ times, we get

$$
  E_n^{(m)} = \braket{\Psi_n^{(k)}|\hat{V}|\Psi_n^{(m-k-l)}} - \sum_{j=1}^k \sum_{l=1}^{m-k-1} E_n^{(m-j-l)} \braket{\Psi_n^{(j)}|\Psi_n^{(l)}}
$$

For an even-order energy correction, $E_n^{(2m)}$, take $k = m - 1$. Then

$$
  E_n^{(2m)} = \braket{\Psi_n^{(m-1)}|\hat{V}|\Psi_n^{(m)}} - \sum_{j=1}^{m-1} \sum_{l=1}^m E_n^{(2m-j-l)} \braket{\Psi_n^{(j)}|\Psi_n^{(l)}}
$$

For an odd-order correction, $E_n^{(2m+1)}$, take $k = m$, giving

$$
  E_n^{(2m+1)} =& \braket{\Psi_n^{(m)}|\hat{V}|\Psi_n^{(m)}} - \sum_{j=1}^m \sum_{l=1}^m E_n^{(2m-j-l+1)} \braket{\Psi_n^{(j)}|\Psi_n^{(l)}}
$$

## The Hylleraas variation principle

Instead of expanding $\ket{\Psi_n^{(1)}}$ in the eigenbasis $\set{\ket{\Phi_k}}_{k=1}^\infty$ of the unperturbed Hamiltonian, the first-order wave function and the second-order energy can also be obtained simultaneously by a variational method due to Hylleraas. We begin by projecting the first-order equation $\eqref{equation-9}$ onto $\bra{\Psi_n^{(1)}}$, giving

$$
  \braket{\Psi_n^{(1)}|\hat{H}_0 - E_n^{(0)}|\Psi_n^{(1)}} = \braket{\Psi_n^{(1)}|E_n^{(1)} - \hat{V}|\Phi_n}
$$

or

$$
  0 = \braket{\Psi_n^{(1)}|\hat{V} - E_n^{(1)}|\Phi_n} + \braket{\Psi_n^{(1)}|\hat{H}_0 - E_n^{(0)}|\Psi_n^{(1)}}
$$

Adding the equation for the second-order energy

$$
  E_n^{(2)} = \braket{\Phi_n |\hat{V} - E_n^{(1)}|\Psi_n^{(1)}}
$$

gives

$$
\begin{align*}
  E_n^{(2)} =& \braket{\Psi_n^{(1)}|\hat{V} - E_n^{(1)}|\Phi_n} \\
  &+ \braket{\Phi_n |\hat{V} - E_n^{(1)}|\Psi_n^{(1)}} \\
  &+ \braket{\Psi_n^{(1)}|\hat{H}_0 - E_n^{(0)}|\Psi_n^{(1)}} \\
  =& 2\Re \brekt{\Psi_1^{(1)}|\hat{V} - E_n^{(1)}|\Phi_n} \\
  &+ \braket{\Phi_n^{(1)}|\hat{H}_0 - E_n^{(0)}|\Psi_n^{(1)}}
\end{align*}
$$

This motivates the definition of the Hylleraas functional

$$
  J_2 [\Psi] = 2\Re \braket{\Psi|\hat{V} - E_n^{(1)}|\Phi_n} + \braket{\Psi|\hat{H}_0 - E_n^{(0)}|\Psi}
$$

such that by construction $J_2 [\Psi_n^{(1)}] = E_n^{(2)}$. The first-order wavefunction can therefore be characterized as a stationary point of $J_2 [\Psi]$. For an arbitrary variation $\ket{\delta\Psi}$, the first variation is

$$
\begin{align*}
  \delta J_2 [\Psi] =& \braket{\delta\Psi|\hat{V} - E_n^{(1)}|\Phi_n} + \braket{\Phi_n |\hat{V} - E_n^{(1)}|\delta\Psi} \\
  &+ \braket{\delta\Psi|\hat{H}_0 - E_n^{(0)}|\Psi} + \braket{\Psi|\hat{H}_0 - E_n^{(0)}|\delta\Psi}
\end{align*}
$$

Requiring that $\delta J_2 [\Psi] = 0$ for any $\ket{\delta\Psi}$ results in the Euler-Lagrange equation

$$
  (\hat{V} - E_n^{(1)})\ket{\Phi_n} + (\hat{H}_0 - E_n^{(0)}) \ket{\Psi} = 0
$$

which is precisely the first-order perturbation equation. Thus, $\ket{\Psi} = \ket{\Psi_n^{(1)}}$ is a stationary point of $J_2$.

Because $(\hat{H}_0 - E_n^{(0)}) \ket{\Phi_n} = 0$ and

$$
  \braket{\Phi_n |(\hat{V} - E_n^{(1)}| \Phi_n} = E_n^{(1)} - E_n^{(1)} = 0
$$

it follows that for any scalar $c$

$$
  J_2 [\Psi + c\Phi_n] = J_2 [\Psi]
$$

This degree of freedom is fixed by imposing $\braket{\Psi|\Phi_n} = 0$. On this constrained subspace, the $E_n^{(1)}$ term drops out of the functional $\eqref{equation-14}$, so that

$$
  J_2 [\Psi] = 2\Re \braket{\Psi|\hat{V}|\Phi_n} + \braket{\Psi|\hat{H}_0 - E_n^{(0)}|\Psi}
$$

Next, we show that if $E_n^{(0)}$ is the lowest eigenvalue of $\hat{H}_0$, then $J_2 [\Psi]$ is an upper bound for $E_n^{(2)}$. Let $\ket{\Psi} = \ket{\Psi_n^{(1)}} + \ket{\chi}$, where $\ket{\chi}$ is any admissible correction satisfying $\braket{\Phi_n|chi} = 0$.

Expanding $J_2 [\Psi]$ about the exact first-order wavefunction give

$$
\begin{align*}
  J_2 [\Psi] =& J_2 [\Psi_n^{(1)}] + 2\Re \braket{\chi|\hat{V} - E_n^{(1)}|\Phi_n} \\
  &+ 2\Re \braket{\chi|\hat{H}_0 - E_n^{(0)}|\Psi_n^{(1)}} + \braket{\chi|\hat{H}_0 - E_n^{(0)}|\chi}
\end{align*}
$$

Using the first-order equation from $\eqref{equation-9}$, we find that the cross terms cancel so that we have

$$
  J_2 [\Psi] = E_n^{(2)} + \braket{\chi\hat{H}_0 - E_n^{(0)}|\chi}
$$

If $E_n^{(0)}$ is the lowest eigenvalue of $\hat{H}_0$ then the integral $\braket{\chi|\hat{H}_0 - E_n^{(0)}|\chi}$ is nonnegative and is zero if, and only if, $\ket{\chi}$ is the corresponding eigenfunction. Thus

$$
  J_2 [\Psi] \geq E_n^{(2)}
$$

Thus, $J_2 [\Psi]$, with an arbitrary trial function $\ket{\Psi}$ containing adjustable parameters, can be used in a variational approach for finding approximations to the first-order wavefunction and second-order energy, and this provides an upper bound to $E_n^{(2)}$ in the case of a state having the lowest zero-order energy (provided that $\ket{\Phi_0}$ is an exact eigenfunction of $\hat{H}_0$).

## Resolvent expansion

Consider the stationary Schrödinger equation

$$
\begin{equation*}
  (\hat{H}_0 + \hat{V}) \ket{\Psi} = E\ket{\Psi}
\tag{\label{equation-16}}
\end{equation*}
$$

where the reference state $\ket{\Phi_0}$ satisfies

$$
  \hat{H}_0 \ket{\Phi_0} = E_0^{(0)} \ket{\Phi_0}
$$

We chose the phase and normalization of the exact state according to intermediate normalization 

$$
\begin{equation*}
  \braket{\Phi_0|\Psi} = 1
\tag{\label{equation-21}}
\end{equation*}
$$

Projecting $\eqref{equation-16}$ onto $\bra{\Phi_0}$ gives

$$
  \braket{\Phi_0 |\hat{H}_0 |\Psi} + \braket{\Phi_0 |\hat{V}|\Psi} = E \braket{\Phi_0 |\Psi}
$$

Since $\hat{H}_0$ is Hermitian, $\bra{\Phi_0} \hat{H}_0 = E_0^{(0)} \bra{\Phi_0}$, and therefore, using $\eqref{equation-21}$

$$
  E_0^{(0)} + \braket{\Phi_0 |\hat{V}|\Psi} = E
$$

Defining the energy shift $\Delta E = E - E_0^{(0)}$, we get the relation $\Delta E = \braket{\Phi_0 |\hat{V}|\Psi}$. 

**Orthogonal decomposition**

Introduce the projection operators $\hat{P} = \ket{\Phi_0} \bra{\Phi_0}$ and $\hat{Q} = \hat{I} - \hat{P}$. Applying the resolution of identity $\eqref{equation-17}$, the exact state $\ket{\Psi}$ decomposes as

$$
\begin{align*}
  \ket{\Psi} =& \hat{P} \ket{\Psi} + \hat{Q} \ket{\Psi} \\
  =& \ket{\Phi_0} \underbrace{\braket{\Phi_0 |\Psi}}_{=1} + \sum'_i \ket{\Phi_i} \braket{\Phi_i |\Psi} \\
  =& \ket{\Phi_0} + \ket{\chi}
\end{align*}
$$

where $\ket{\chi} = \hat{Q} \ket{\Psi}$.

Rearranging $\eqref{equation-16}$ as

$$
  -\hat{H}_0 \ket{\Psi} = (\hat{V} - E) \ket{\Psi}
$$

we may add an arbitrary scalar $\zeta$ to both sides, leading to

$$
\begin{equation*}
  (\zeta - \hat{H}_0) \ket{\Psi} = (\hat{V} - E + \zeta)\ket{\Psi}
\tag{\label{equation-22}}
\end{equation*}
$$

The parameter $\zeta$ is introduced purely for convenience; different choices of $\zeta$ lead to different, but algebraically equivalent, forms of the perturbation expansion. Particular choices are 
- $\zeta = E$ (Brillouin-Wigner perturbation theory)
- $\zeta = E_0^{(0)}$ (Rayleigh-Schrödinger perturbation theory)

Applying $\hat{Q}$ to both sides $\eqref{equation-22}$, we obtain

$$
\begin{equation*}
  \hat{Q} (\zeta - \hat{H}_0) \ket{\Psi} = \hat{Q} (\hat{V} - E + \zeta) \ket{\Psi}
\tag{\label{equation-18}}
\end{equation*}
$$

Using idempotency, $\hat{Q}^2 = \hat{Q}$ and commutativity, $[\hat{Q}, \hat{H}_0] = 0$, the operator on the left-hand side can be written as

$$
\begin{align*}
  \hat{Q}(\zeta - \hat{H}_0)\ket{\Psi} =& \hat{Q}^2 (\zeta - \hat{H}_0) \ket{\Psi} \\
  =& \hat{Q}(\zeta - \hat{H}_0) \hat{Q} \ket{\Psi} \tag{\label{equation-17}}
\end{align*}
$$

The operator $\hat{Q}(\zeta - \hat{H}_0) \hat{Q}$ acts entirely within the $Q$-space and in terms of the zero-order functions has the expansion

$$
  \hat{Q}(\zeta - \hat{H}_0) \hat{Q} = \sum'_{i,j} \ket{\Phi_i} \braket{\Phi_i |\zeta - \hat{H}_0|\Phi_j} \bra{\Phi_j}
$$

If the unperturbed eigenstates $\set{\ket{\Phi_i}}$ form an orthonormal basis, $\hat{H}_0 \ket{\Phi_i} = E_i^{(0)}$, and the this expansion simplifies to

$$
  \hat{Q}(\zeta - \hat{H}_0) \hat{Q} = \sum'_i \ket{\Phi_i} (\zeta - E_i^{(0)}) \bra{\Phi_i}
$$

Substituting $\eqref{equation-17}$ into $\eqref{equation-18}$ gives

$$
\begin{equation*}
  \hat{Q} (\zeta - \hat{H}_0) \hat{Q} \ket{\Psi} = \hat{Q} (\hat{V} - E + \zeta) \ket{\Psi}
\tag{\label{equation-20}}
\end{equation*}
$$

We require $\zeta$ to to lie outside the spectrum of $\hat{H}_0$ restricted to $Q$-space. In the discrete case considered here, this implies $\zeta \neq E_i^{(0)}$ for all $i \neq 0$. Under this condition the inverse of $\hat{Q}(\zeta - \hat{H}_0) \hat{Q}$ exists in $Q$-space. This inverse is written in the form

$$
\begin{equation*}
\begin{split}
  \hat{R}_0 (\zeta) =& \frac{\hat{Q}}{\zeta - \hat{H}_0} \\
  \equiv& \sum'_{i,j} \ket{\Phi_i} \braket{\Phi_i (\zeta - \hat{H}_0)^{-1}|\Phi_j} \bra{\Phi_j}
\end{split}
\tag{\label{equation-19}}
\end{equation*}
$$

and is called the resolvent of $\hat{H}_0$. In the eigenbasiss of $\hat{H}_0$, it can be expressed as

$$
\begin{align*}
  \hat{R}_0 (\zeta) =& \sum'_{i,j} \ket{\Phi_i} \braket{\Phi_i |(\zeta - E_j^{(0)})^{-1}| \Phi_j} \bra{\Phi_i} \\
  =& \sum'_i \frac{\ket{\Phi_i} \bra{\Phi_i}}{(\zeta - E_i^{(0)})}
\end{align*}
$$

which rationalizes the notation

$$
  \frac{\hat{Q}}{\zeta - \hat{H}_0}
$$

Applying $\eqref{equation-19}$ to both sides of $\eqref{equation-20}$, we get

$$
  \hat{Q}\ket{\Psi} = \hat{R}_0 (\zeta) (\hat{V} - E + \zeta) \ket{\Psi}
$$

and thus, substituting in $\ket{\Psi} = \ket{\Phi_0} + \hat{Q}\ket{\Psi}$, we obtain the exact equation

$$
  \ket{\Psi} = \ket{\Phi_0} + \hat{R}_0 (\zeta) (\hat{V} - E + \zeta) \ket{\Psi}
$$

This can be seen as an iterative equation for $\ket{\Psi}$. Define $\hat{K} (\zeta, E) = \hat{R}_0 (\zeta) (\hat{V} - E + \zeta)$, we can express the equation as

$$
  \ket{\Psi} = \ket{\Psi_0} + \hat{K}(\zeta, E) \ket{\Psi}
$$

Iterating $N$ times results in

$$
  \ket{\Psi} = \sum_{m=0}^{N-1} \hat{K}^m \ket{\Psi_0} + \hat{K}^N \ket{\Psi}
$$

If the iteration converges in an appropriate operator topology so that

$$
  \lim_{N\to\infty} \hat{K}^N \ket{\Psi} = 0
$$

then

$$
\begin{equation*}
  \ket{\Psi} = \sum_{m=0}^\infty \left[\hat{R}_0 (\zeta) (\hat{V} - E + \zeta) \right]^m \ket{\Phi_0}
\tag{\label{equation-23}}
\end{equation*}
$$

Note that the $m$th term in this sum does not coincide with the $m$th order perturbative correction to the wavefunction, since $E$ contains contributions from all perturbative orders. In any case, using $\Delta E = \braket{\Phi_0 |\hat{V}|\Psi}$ we have, formally

$$
  \Delta E = \sum_{m=0}^\infty \Braket{\Phi_0 | \hat{V} \left[\hat{R}_0 (\zeta) (\hat{V} - E + \zeta) \right]^m |\Phi_0}
$$

which is an implicit nonlinear equation for the exact energy $E = E_0^{(0)} + \Delta E$.

### Brillouin-Wigner perturbation theory

Chosing $\zeta = E$ produces Brillouin-Wigner perturbation theory, which simplifies $\eqref{equation-23}$ and $\eqref{equation-24}$ to

$$
\begin{align*}
  \ket{\Psi} =& \sum_{m=0}^\infty \left[\hat{R}_0 (E) \hat{V} \right]^m \ket{\Phi_0} \\
  \Delta E =& \sum_{m=0}^\infty \Braket{\Phi_0 |\hat{V} \left[\hat{R}_0 (E) \hat{V} \right]^m | \Phi_0}
\end{align*}
$$

In the eigenbasis of $\hat{H}_0$, the resolvent becomes

$$
\begin{align*}
  \hat{R}_0 (E) =& \frac{\hat{Q}}{E - \hat{H}_0} \\
  =& \sum'_i \ket{\Phi_i} \frac{1}{E - E_i^{(0)}} \bra{\Phi_i}
\end{align*}
$$

and the second-order energy becomes

$$
\begin{align*}
  E^{(2)} =& \braket{\Phi_0 |\hat{V} \hat{R}_0 (E) \hat{V}|\Phi_0} \\
  =& \sum'_i \braket{\Phi_0 |\hat{V}|\Phi_i} (E - E_i^{(0)})^{-1} \braket{\Phi_i |\hat{V}|\Phi_0} \\
  =& \sum'_i \frac{V_{0i} V_{0i}}{E - E_i^{(0)}}
\end{align*}
$$

which is similar to the Rayleigh-Schrödinger perturbation theory except for the presence of $E$ rather than $E_0^{(0)}$ in the denominator.

The form of these equation is simpler than that of the Rayleigh-Schrödinger pertubation theory (ignoring the problem of the unknown $E$ in the denominators), because the absence of the so-called renormalization terms, the additional sums $\sum_{l=0}^{m-1}$ involving the lower-order energies in Rayleight-Schrödinger perturbation theory. However, it does not provide a true order-by-order expansion for the energy (because of the presence of the infinite-order $E$), and realted to this is a very fundamental problem with BWPT, its lack of extensivity when stopped at any finite order. In fact, BWPT resembles CI in many respects, and successive order of the BWPT calculations are closely related to the successive iteration used to solve the matrix eigenvalue problem in some CI procedures.
