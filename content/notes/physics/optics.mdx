# Polarization

Consider an electromagnetic wave propagating through a homogeneous, isotropic, linear, and non-conducting medium with refractive index $n$. The electric field $\mathbf{E}$ satisfies the wave equation

$$
\begin{equation*}
  \left(\nabla^2 - \frac{1}{v^2} \frac{\partial^2}{\partial t^2}\right) \mathbf{E} = 0
\tag{\label{equation-1}}
\end{equation*}
$$

where $v = \frac{c}{n}$ is the phase velocity of the wave. For a monochromatic plane wave, the electric field takes the form

$$
\begin{equation*}
  \mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r}, t) = \mathbf{E}_0 e^{i(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r} - \omega t)}
\tag{\label{equation-2}}
\end{equation*}
$$

where $\mathbb{E}_0 \in \mathbb{C}^3$ is the amplitude, $\omega$ is the angular frequency, and $\mathbf{k}$ is the wave vector. Substituting $\eqref{equation-2}$ into the wave equation $\eqref{equation-1}$ gives the dispersion relation $k^2 = \omega^2 /v^2$, so that

$$
  k = \norm{\mathbf{k}} = \frac{\omega}{v} = \frac{n\omega}{c}
$$

For a homogeneous isotropic medium, Gauss's law requires that $\mathbf{E}$ must be divergence-free, i.e.

$$
  \nabla\cdot\mathbf{E} = i\mathbf{k} \cdot\mathbf{E}_0 e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r} - \omega t} = 0
$$

implying that $\mathbf{k}\cdot \mathbf{E}_0 = 0$. Consequently, the electric field is transverse to the direction of propagation. After specifying the direction of $\mathbf{k}$, the electric-field amplitude can have arbitrary components in the two-dimensional plane perpendicular to $\mathbf{k}$.

Let $\unitvec{e}_1$ and $\unitvec{e}_2$ be real orthonormal unit vectors spanning the transverse plane of $\unitvec{k} = \mathbf{k}/k$, chosen such that $\unitvec{e}_1 \cdot \unitvec{e}_2 = 0$ and $\unitvec{e}_1 \times \unitvec{e}_2 = \unitvec{k}$. Then $(\unitvec{e}_1, \unitvec{e}_2, \unitvec{k})$ forms a right-handed orthonormal basis, and the electric field can be expressed as

$$
\begin{equation*}
  \mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r}, t) = (E_1 \unitvec{e}_1 + E_2 \unitvec{e}_1) e^{i(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r} - \omega t)}
\tag{\label{equation-3}}
\end{equation*}
$$

The complex amplitudes $E_1, E_2 \in\mathbb{C}$ can be written polar form as

$$
\begin{equation*}
\begin{split}
  E_1 =& A e^{i\delta_1} \\
  E_2 =& B e^{i\delta_2}
\end{split}
\tag{\label{equation-4}}
\end{equation*}
$$

Introducing $\phi = \mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r} - \omega t$ the physical electric field as given by the real part of $\eqref{equation-3}$

$$
\begin{equation*}
  \Re[\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r}, t)] = A \cos(\phi + \delta_1) \unitvec{e}_1 + B \cos(\phi + \delta_2) \unitvec{e}_2
\tag{\label{equation-34}}
\end{equation*}
$$

Denoting the instantaneous real tranverse field components by $E_{1r}$ and $E_{2r}$, we obtain the coefficient equations

$$
\begin{align*}
  \frac{E_{1r}}{A} =& \cos(\phi + \delta_1) \tag{\label{equation-21}} \\
  \frac{E_{2r}}{B} =& \cos(\phi + \delta_2) \tag{\label{equation-22}}
\end{align*}
$$

Using trigonometric identities, these equations satisfy

$$
\begin{equation*}
  \left(\frac{E_{1r}}{A} \right)^2 + \left(\frac{E_{2r}}{B} \right)^2 - 2 \frac{E_{1r} E_{2r}}{AB} \cos(\delta) = \sin^2 (\delta)
\tag{\label{equation-33}}
\end{equation*}
$$

where $\delta = \delta_2 - \delta_1$. For $A, B > 0$ and $\sin(\delta) \neq 0$, this equation describes an ellipse in the $e_1$-$e_2$ plane. Consequently, a general monochromatic plan wave is elliptically polarized. The polarization state is determined entirely by the amplitude ratio $B/A$ and the relative phase $\delta$.

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>

Rewriting $\phi + \delta_2 = (\phi + \delta_1) + \delta$, and using the cosine addition formula for $\cos[(\phi + \delta_1) + \delta]$ in $\eqref{equation-22}$, we obtain

$$
\begin{equation*}
  \frac{E_{2r}}{B} = \cos(\phi + \delta_1) \cos(\delta) - \sin(\phi + \delta_1) \sin(\delta)
\tag{\label{equation-31}}
\end{equation*}
$$

Applying the Pythagorean identity to $\eqref{equation-21}$ we get

$$
\begin{equation*}
  \sin(\phi + \delta_1) = \sqrt{1 - \frac{E_{1r}^}{A^2}}
\tag{\label{equation-32}}
\end{equation*}
$$

Substituting $\eqref{equation-21}$ and $\eqref{equation-32}$ into $\eqref{equation-31}$ yields

$$
  \frac{E_{2r}}{B} = \frac{E_{1r}}{A} \cos(\delta)a - \sqrt{1 - \frac{E_{1r}^2}{A^2}} \sin(\delta)
$$

Rearranging, we have

$$
  \frac{E_{1r}}{A} \cos(\delta) - \frac{E_2r}{B} = \sqrt{1 - \frac{E_{2r}^2}{B^2}} \sin(\delta)
$$

Squaring both sides gives

$$
  \frac{E_{1r}^2}{A^2} \underbrace{\cos^2(\delta)}_{=1 - \sin^2 (\delta)} - 2\frac{E_{1r} E_{2r}}{AB}\cos(\delta) + \frac{E_{2r}^2}{B^2} = \left(1 - \frac{E_{1r}^2}{A^2} \right) \sin^2 (\delta)
$$

Cancelling terms, we arrive at

$$
  \left(\frac{E_{1r}}{A} \right)^2 + \left(\frac{E_{2r}}{B} \right)^2 - 2 \frac{E_{1r} E_{2r}}{AB} \cos(\delta) = \sin^2 (\delta)
$$
</details>

## Elliptical polarization

To determine the orientation of the polarization ellipse, we diagonalize the quadratic form associated with $\eqref{equation-10}$

$$
  \begin{bmatrix} E_{1r} & E_{2r} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} A^{-2} & -\frac{\cos(\delta)}{AB} \\ -\frac{\cos(\delta)}{AB} & B^{-2} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} E_{1r} \\ E_{2r} \end{bmatrix} = \sin^2 (\delta)
$$

The principal axes are obtained by rotating the transverse coordinate system so that the quadratic form diagonal. The ration angle $\tau$ satisfies

$$
  \tan(2\tau) = \frac{2AB \cos(\delta)}{A^2 - B^2}
$$

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>

The principal axes of the ellipse are the directions in which this quadratic form is diagonal. Thus, we seek a rotation of coordinates that eliminates the $E_{1r} E_{2r}$ cross term in $\eqref{equation-10}$. 

Let the new coordinates $(E'_{1r}, E'_{2r})$ denote coordiantes in a system rotated through $\tau$, with

$$
\begin{align*}
  E_{1r} =& E'_{1r} \cos(\tau) - E'_{2r} \sin(\tau) \\
  E_{2r} =& E'_{1r} \sin(\tau) + E'_{2r} \cos(\tau)
\end{align*}
$$

Substituting this into $\eqref{equation-10}$ gives

$$
\begin{align*}
  \sin^2 (\delta) =& \frac{1}{A^2} [E'_{1r} \cos(\tau) - E'_{2r} \sin(\tau)]^2 \\
  &+ \frac{1}{B^2} [E'_{1r} \sin(\tau) + E'_{2r} \cos(\tau)]^2 \\
  &- \frac{2 \cos(\delta)}{AB} [E'_{1r} \cos(\tau) - E'_{2r} \sin(\tau)] [E'_{1r} \sin(\tau) + E'_{2r} \cos(\tau)]
\end{align*}
$$

Expanding the three terms, the coefficient of $E'_{1r} E'_{2r}$ is

$$
  -\left(\frac{1}{B^2} - \frac{1}{A^2} \right) 2\cos(\tau) \sin(\tau) - \frac{2\cos(\delta)}{AB} [\cos^2 (\tau) - \sin^2 (\tau)]
$$

For the rotated coordinate system to coincide with the principal-axis system, this coefficient must vanish. Using $2\cos(\tau) \sin(\tau) = \sin(2\tau)$ and $\cos^2 (\tau) - \sin^2 (\tau) = \cos(2\tau)$, we obtain

$$
  \left(\frac{1}{A^2} - \frac{1}{B^2} \right) \sin(2\tau) - \frac{2\cos(\delta)}{AB} \cos(2\tau) = 0
$$

Multiplying by $A^2 B^2$ gives

$$
  (B^2 - A^2) \sin(2\tau) = 2AB \cos(\delta) \cos(2\tau)
$$

resulting in

$$
  \tan(2\tau) = \frac{2AB \cos(\delta)}{B^2 - A^2}
$$
</details>

The second principal axis perpendicular to the first and therefore has orientation $\tau + \pi/2$. The squared semiaxis amplitudes are given by the eigenvalues of the symmetric covariance matrix

$$
  \mathbf{C} = \begin{bmatrix} A^2 & AB\cos(\delta) \\ AB\cos(\delta) & B^2 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Its eigenvalues are obtained from the characteristic equation $\det(\mathbf{C} - \lambda\mathbf{I}_2) = 0$, which gives

$$
\begin{equation*}
  \lambda_\pm = \frac{1}{2} \left[A^2 + B^2 \pm \sqrt{(A^2 - B^2)^2 + 4A^2 B^2 \cos^2 (\delta)} \right]
  \tag{\label{equation-8}}
\end{equation*}
$$

The larger eigenvalue corresponds to the squared semimajor axis, $a^2 = \lambda_+$ and the smaller eigenvalue to the squared semiminor axis, $b^2 = \lambda_-$, with $a \geq b \geq 0$.

The ellipticity can be defined as the ratio of the semiminor to semimajor axis

$$
  \varepsilon := \frac{b}{a},\, 0 \leq \epsilon \leq 1
$$ 

From $\eqref{equation-8}$, the ellipticity is given by

$$
  \varepsilon = \sqrt{\frac{A^2 + B^2 - \sqrt{(A^2 - B^2)^2 + 4A^2 B^2 \cos^2 (\delta)}}{A^2 + B^2 + \sqrt{(A^2 - B^2)^2 + 4A^2 B^2 \cos^2 (\delta)}}}
$$

The ellipticity is related to the eccentricity $e$ via

$$
  e = \sqrt{1 - \frac{b^2}{a^2}} = \sqrt{1 - \epsilon^2}
$$

The ellipticity and orientation of the ellipse depend on the phase difference $\delta$ and the amplitude ratio $B/A$. In particular:
- If $A \neq B$ and $\delta = \pm\pi/2$ the field traces an ellipse whose axes are aligned with $\unitvec{e}_1$ and $\unitvec{e}_2$
- If $A = B$ and $\delta \neq 0, \pi, \pm\pi/2$, the field traces a rotated ellipse

Linear polarization occurs when $\varepsilon = 0$, while circular polarization occurs when $\varepsilon = 1$. The helicity, or handedness of polarized light is as follows
- left-handed: $0 < \delta < \pi$
- right-handed: $\pi < \delta < 2\pi$

## Linear polarization

The wave is linearly polarization if the two components are either in phase or out of phase, i.e. $\delta = m\pi$ for $m\in\Z$ if both components are nonzero, $A, B \neq 0$ (horizontal/vertical polarization). Linear polarization also occurs if one of the components vanishes, $A = 0$ or $B = 0$ (diagonal polarization).

## Circular polarization

The polarization ellipse described by $\eqref{equation-33}$ simplifies to a circle when the electric field components have equal amplitude $A = B = A_0$ and are $\pi/2$ out of phase, i.e. 

$$
\begin{equation*}
  \delta = m\pi/2, m\in \Z_+
\tag{\label{equation-35}}
\end{equation*}
$$

Without loss of generalization, the common phase may be chosen such that $\delta_1 = 0$. Writing $\phi = \mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r} - \omega t$, the real electric field becomes $\eqref{equation-34}$ with $\delta_1 = 0$ in the form

$$
  \Re[\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r}, t)] = A_0 [\unitvec{e}_1 \cos(\phi) + \unitvec{e}_2 \cos(\phi + \delta)]
$$

The condition $\eqref{equation-35}$ implies that $\cos(\delta) = 0$ and $\sin(\delta) = \pm 1$. From the cosine addition formula, we obtain

$$
  \cos(\phi + \delta) = -\sin(\phi) \sin(\delta) = \mp \sin(\phi)
$$

The choice of sign produces two distinct fields

$$
\begin{equation*}
  \Re[\mathbf{E}_\pm (\mathbf{r}, t)] = \frac{A_0}{\sqrt{2}} [\unitvec{e}_1 \cos(\omega t) \pm \unitvec{e}_2 \sin(\omega t)]
\tag{\label{equation-36}}
\end{equation*}
$$

where $\mathbf{E}_+$ describes counterclockwise (left) circular polarization and $\mathbf{E}_-$ clockwise (right) circular polarization.

### Circular polarization basis

A circular-polarization basis can be introduced by forming complex conjugate pairs of the transverse bases vectors $\unitvec{e}_1$ and $\unitvec{e}_2$

$$
\begin{equation*}
  \unitvec{e}_\pm = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} (\unitvec{e}_1 \pm i \unitvec{e}_2)
\tag{\label{equation-7}}
\end{equation*}
$$

<details>
<summary>Proof</summary>

To show the orthonormality of the basis $\set{\unitvec{e}_+, \unitvec{e}_+}$, we use the Hermitian inner product $\braket{\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}} = \mathbf{a}^* \mathbf{b}$

Since the Cartesian basis vectors are orthonormal, $\unitvec{e}_i \cdot \unitvec{e}_j = \delta_{ij}$, we obtain

$$
\begin{align*}
  \unitvec{e}_\pm^*\unitvec{e}_\pm =& \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} (\unitvec{e}_1 \mp i\unitvec{e}_2) \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} (\unitvec{e}_1 \pm i\unitvec{e}_2) \\
  =& \frac{1}{2} (\underbrace{\unitvec{e}_1 \cdot\unitvec{e}_1}_{=1} \pm i\underbrace{\unitvec{e}_1 \cdot\unitvec{e}_2}_{=0} \mp i\underbrace{\unitvec{e}_2 \cdot\unitvec{e}_1}_{=0} - i^2 \underbrace{\unitvec{e}_2 \cdot\unitvec{e}_2}_{=1}) \\
  =& 1
\end{align*}
$$

Furthermore

$$
\begin{align*}
  \unitvec{e}_\pm^*\unitvec{e}_\mp =& \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} (\unitvec{e}_1 \mp i\unitvec{y}) \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} (\unitvec{x} \mp i\unitvec{y}) \\
  =& \frac{1}{2} (\underbrace{\unitvec{e}_1 \cdot \unitvec{e}_1}_{=1} \mp 2i\underbrace{\unitvec{e}_1 \cdot\unitvec{e}_2}_{=0} + i^2 \underbrace{\unitvec{e}_2 \cdot\unitvec{e}_2}_{=1}) \\
  =& 0
\end{align*}
$$
</details>

A field with a definite circular polarization can be written as

$$
\begin{align*}
  \mathbf{E}_{\pm} (\mathbf{r}, t) =& A_0 \unitvec{e}_\pm e^{i (\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r} - \omega t)} \\
  =& \frac{A_0}{\sqrt{2}} (\unitvec{e}_1 \pm i\unitvec{e}_2) e^{i\phi}
\end{align*}
$$

Taking the real part results in $\eqref{equation-36}$. Thus, $\unitvec{e}_+$ represents a pure left circular polarized wave, while $\unitvec{e}_-$ represents a pure right circular polarized wave.

#### Linear polarization as a superposition of circular polarizations

The inverse relations for the basis vectors are particularly useful. Adding and subtracting $\eqref{equation-7}$ gives

$$
\begin{align*}
  \unitvec{e}_1 =& \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} (\unitvec{e}_+ + \unitvec{e}_-) \\
  \unitvec{e}_2 =& \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} (\unitvec{e}_+ - \unitvec{e}_-)
\end{align*}
$$

This shows that linear polarization along $\unitvec{e}_1$ consists of equal-amplitude left- and right-circular components with the same phase. Conversely, linear polarization along $\unitvec{e}_2$ consists of equal-amplitude left- and right-circular components that are $\pi$ out of phase.

## Jones vector

In terms of the complex amplitudes $\eqref{equation-4}$, the electric field in $\eqref{equation-3}$ can be expressed as

$$
  \mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r}, t) = (A \unitvec{e}_1 e^{i\delta_1} + Be^{i\delta_2} \unitvec{e}_2) e^{i(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r} - \omega t)}
$$

Factoring out the common phase $e^{i\delta_1}$ and the total amplitude $E = \sqrt{A^2 + B^2}$, we get

$$
  \mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r}, t) = E e^{i\delta_1} \left(\frac{A}{E} \unitvec{e}_1 + \frac{B}{E} e^{i\delta} \unitvec{e}_2 \right) e^{i(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r} - \omega t)}
$$

where $\delta = \delta_2 - \delta_1$ is the relative phase. The factor $e^{i\delta_1}$ is a global phase and therefore has no effect on the polarization state. Similarly, the factor $e^{i(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r} - \omega t)}$ describes the common spatial and temporal phase evolution of the plane wave and does not distinguish between polarization states. Consequently, the polarization state is characterized by the complex amplitudes of the two orthogonal polarization components. In the bases $\set{\unitvec{e}_1, \unitvec{e}_2}$, these amplitudes form the normalized Jones vector

$$
  \mathbf{j} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{A}{E} \\ \frac{B}{E} e^{i\delta} \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{C}^2
$$

The normalization of $\mathbf{j}$ follows from the Hermitian inner product on $\mathbb{C}^2$

$$
\begin{align*}
  \norm{j}^2 =& \mathbf{j}^* \mathbf{j} \\
  =& \begin{bmatrix} \frac{A}{E} & \frac{B}{E} e^{-i\delta} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \frac{A}{E} \\ \frac{B}{E} e^{i\delta} \end{bmatrix} \\
  =& \frac{A^2}{E^2} + \frac{B^2}{E^2} = \frac{A^2 + B^2}{A^2 + B^2} = 1
\end{align*}
$$

Since $A, B \geq 0$ are non-negative, the relative magnitudes of the two electrical field components can be parametrized by an angle $\theta \in [0, \pi/2]$ according to $\theta = \arctan(B/A)$. Using $E = \sqrt{A^2 + B^2}$, this gives 

$$
\begin{align*}
  \frac{A}{E} =& \cos(\theta) \\
  \frac{B}{E} =& \sin(\theta)
\end{align*}
$$

Thus, the Jones vector can be expressed in the canonical form

$$
  \mathbf{j} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos(\theta) \\ e^{i\delta}\sin(\theta) \end{bmatrix},\, \theta \in \left[0, \frac{\pi}{2} \right],\, \delta\in [0,2\pi)
$$

Here, $\theta$ determines the relative amplitudes of the two orthogonal polarization components, while $\delta$ determines their relative phase.

If $A = 0$, then the phase $e^{i\phi_1}$ is indeterminant. In this case we let $B = 1$ and $\delta = 0$, corresponding to the $\unitvec{e}_2$-linearly polarized state with $\theta = \pi/2$. Likewise, for $B = 0$, we let $A = 1$, corresponding to the $\unitvec{e}_2$-linearly (horizontal) polarized state with $\theta = 0$.

When the orthogonal electrical field components are in phase quadrature $\delta = \pm \pi/2$, the polarization ellipse has its principal axes aligned with $\unitvec{e}_1$ and $\unitvec{e}_2$. The corresponding real field corresponding real field components can be written, up to an overall phase, as

$$
\begin{align*}
  E_{1r} (t) =& A \cos(\omega t) \\
  E_{2r} (t) =& \pm B \sin(\omega t)
\end{align*}
$$

and therefore satisfy

$$
  \frac{E_{1r}^2}{A^2} + \frac{E_{2r}^2}{B^2} = 1
$$


In this case $\theta$ coincides with the eccentricity $\varepsilon$. This requires $\delta = \pm \pi/2$ if $\theta \neq 0$, such that $e^{i\delta} = \pm i$. The sign can be absorbed into $\varepsilon$, where positive eccentricity corresponds to left elliptical polarization, while negative eccentricity corresponds to right elliptical polarization.

| Anti-diagonal (linearly polarized along $\unitvec{e}_1 - \unitvec{e}_2$)  | $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$ | $\ket{A} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{H} - \ket{V})$ |
| Right-hand circular (RCP) | $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -i \end{bmatrix}$ | $\ket{R} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{H} - i\ket{V})$ |
| Left-hand circular (LCP) | $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ i \end{bmatrix}$ | $\ket{R} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{H} + i\ket{V})$ |

## Linear polarizers and wave plates

A polarizer acts on a plane wave by absorbing its electric field along certain axis, while allowing transmission along another axis. The electric field of an monochromatic plane wave propagating in the $z$-direction is given by

$$
  \mathbf{E}(z, t) = (E_x \hat{\mathbf{x}} + E_y \hat{\mathbf{y}})e^{i(kz -\omega t)}
$$

Suppose a polarizer has a transmission axis along $\hat{\mathbf{e}}_1$ and absorption axis along $\hat{\mathbf{e}}_2$ in the $xy$-plane. If $\hat{\mathbf{e}}_1$ and $\hat{\mathbf{e}}_2$ are orthogonal, then they are related to $\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ and $\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ through a rotation by an angle $\theta$ with respect to the $x$-axis. This gives the basis transform

$$
\begin{align*}
  \hat{\mathbf{x}} =& \cos(\theta) \hat{\mathbf{e}}_1 - \sin(\theta) \hat{\mathbf{e}}_1 \\
  \hat{\mathbf{y}} =& \sin(\theta) \hat{\mathbf{e}}_1 + \cos(\theta) \hat{\mathbf{e}}_2
\end{align*}
$$

with inverse

$$
\begin{align*}
  \hat{\mathbf{e}}_1 =& \cos(\theta) \hat{\mathbf{x}} + \sin(\theta) \hat{\mathbf{y}} \\
  \hat{\mathbf{e}}_2 =& -\sin(\theta) \hat{\mathbf{x}} + \cos(\theta) \hat{\mathbf{y}}
\end{align*}
$$

In terms of the $(\hat{\mathbf{e}}_1, \hat{\mathbf{e}}_2)$-basis the electric field becomes

$$
  \mathbf{E}(z, t) = (E_1 \hat{\mathbf{e}}_1 + E_2 \hat{\mathbf{e}}_2)e^{i(kz -\omega t)}
$$

where

$$
\begin{align*}
  E_1 =& E_x \cos\theta + E_y \sin\theta \\
  E_2 =& -E_x \sin\theta + E_y \cos\theta
\end{align*}
$$

The polarizer allows transmission along $\hat{\mathbf{e}}_1$ and causes absorption along $\hat{\mathbf{e}}_2$. Thus, after traversing the polarizer $E_1$ gets unaffected while $E_2$ is changed by a factor $0 \leq \xi \leq 1$. If $\xi = 0$, then $E_2$ gets extinguished, which is the case of a linear polarizer. The polarized wave takes the form

$$
  \mathbf{E}'(z, t) = (E_1 \hat{\mathbf{e}}_1 + \xi E_2 \hat{\mathbf{e}}_2)e^{i(kz -\omega t)}
$$

In terms of the $(\hat{\mathbf{x}}, \hat{\mathbf{y}})$-basis the electric field takes the form

$$
\begin{align*}
  \mathbf{E}'(z, t) =& [(E_x \cos\theta + E_y \sin\theta)(\cos\theta\hat{\mathbf{x}} + \sin\theta\hat{\mathbf{y}}) \\
  &+ \xi(-E_x \sin\theta + E_y \cos\theta)(-\sin\theta \hat{\mathbf{x}} + \cos\theta\hat{\mathbf{y}})]e^{i(kz - \omega t)} \\
  =& [E_x (\cos^2\theta + \xi\sin^2 \theta) + E_y (1 - \xi) \sin\theta\cos\theta]\hat{\mathbf{x}}e^{i(kz - \omega t)} \\
  &+ [E_x (1 - \xi)\sin\theta\cos\theta + E_y(\sin^2 \theta + \xi\cos^2 \theta)]\hat{\mathbf{y}}e^{i(kz - \omega t)} \\
\end{align*}
$$

or in matrix form

$$
  \mathbf{E}'(z, t) = \begin{bmatrix} 
    \cos^2 \theta + \xi\sin^2 \theta & (1 - \xi)\sin\theta\cos\theta \\ 
    (1 - \xi)\sin\theta\cos\theta & \sin^2 \theta + \xi\cos^2\theta
  \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} E_x \hat{\mathbf{x}} \\ E_y \hat{\mathbf{y}} \end{bmatrix} e^{i(kz - \omega t)}
$$

The Jones matrix describing the polarization thus takes the form

$$
  \begin{bmatrix} 
    \cos^2 \theta + \xi\sin^2 \theta & (1 - \xi)\sin\theta\cos\theta \\ 
    (1 - \xi)\sin\theta\cos\theta & \sin^2 \theta + \xi\cos^2\theta
  \end{bmatrix}
$$

For a linear polarizer, $\xi = 0$, and the Jones matrix reduces to

$$
  \mathbf{J} = \begin{bmatrix} 
    \cos^2 \theta & \sin\theta\cos\theta  \\ 
    \sin\theta\cos\theta & \sin^2 \theta 
  \end{bmatrix}
$$

## Wave plates (phase retarder)

Wave plates are made out of birefringent (birefractive) crystals for which the index of refraction depends on the on the orientation of the electric field polarization. The crystal is cut such that the wave plate has a *fast* and *slow* axis, which are orthogonal. Along the fast axis, the elecromagnetic wave experiences index $n_{\text{fast}}$, while the orthogonal axis experiences higher index $n_{\text{slow}}$.

When a plane wave passes through a wave plate, the component of the electric field oriented along the fast axis travels faster than its orthogonal counterpart, which introduces a relative phase between the two polarization components. As light passes through a wave plate of thickness $d$, the phase difference that accumulates between the fast and the slow polarization component is

$$
  k_{\text{slow}}d - k_{\text{slow}}d = \frac{2\pi d}{\lambda_c} (n_{\text{slow}} - n_{\text{fast}})
$$

A desired phase shift can obtained by adjusting the thickness of the wave plate. The most common types of wave plates are the quarter-wave and half-wave plates. The quarter-wave plate induces a phase shift $\frac{\pi}{2} + 2\pi m$ for $m\in \N$ and has a form factor $\xi = e^{i\frac{\pi}{2}} = i$. The half-wave plate induces a phase shift $\pi + 2\pi m$ and has form factor $\xi = e^{i\pi} = -1$.

## Partial polarization

Consider a polychromatic beam of light propagating along the $z$-axis, with electric field components polarized along both the $x$- and $y$-direction

$$
\begin{equation*}
\begin{split}
  E_x (z, t) = \sum_n A_n \sqrt{\Delta f} \exp\left\{i \left[ 2\pi f_n \left(t - \frac{z}{c} \right) + \phi_n \right] \right) \\
  E_z (z, t) = \sum_n B_n \sqrt{\Delta f} \exp\left\{i \left[ 2\pi f_n \left(t - \frac{z}{c} \right) + \psi_n \right] \right) \\
\end{split}
\tag{\label{equation-9}}
\end{equation*}
$$

This beam's spectrum consists of individual frequencies $f_n = (N_0 + n) \Delta f$ within a finite bandwith around the central frequency $f_0 = N_0 \Delta f$. For each frequency $f_n$, here $A_n$ and $B_n$ are the amplitudes of the $x$- and $y$-polarized components, respectively, while $\phi_n$ and $\psi_n$ are their corresponding phases. Here $\sqrt{\Delta f}$ is a normalization factor for the discrete spectral representation.

Each frequency component in $\eqref{equation-9}$ is a fully polarized monochromatic plane wave. In general, it is elliptically polarized, with its polarization state determined by the amplitude ratio $A_n /B_n$ and the relative phase $\delta_n = \phi_n - \phi_n$. Although every individual spectral component is fully polarized, the beam as a whole can only be partially polarized. This occurs because different frequency components may have different polarization states. When the field is averaged over time, these different polarization states combine to produce a partially polarized beam.

### Ideal phase retarder and polarizer

To determine the degree of polarization of the beam described by $\eqref{equation-9}$, consider an ideal phase retarder followed by an ideal linear polarizer. Both optical elements are assumed to be perpendicular to the propagation direction.

The retarder introduces a relative phase shift $\chi \in [-\pi, \pi]$ between $E_x$ and $E_y$. A crucial assumption is that this phase shift is independent of optical frequency over the bandwidth of interest. Thus, every spectral component experiences the same phase shift $E_j \to e^{i\chi} E_j$. A frequency-independent phase shift changes the relative phase between $E_x$ and $E_y$, but does not introduce a temporal displacement between them. Consequently, it does not destroy their mutual coherence. It can, however, modify the cross-correlation function by changing the relative phase between the two polarization components.

A time delay $\tau$, by contrast, produces a frequency-dependenct phase shift $\chi(f) = 2\pi f\tau$. Such a frequency-dependent phase shift can reduce the observed mutual coherence after temporal averaging. A frequency-independent phase shift $\chi$, on the other hand, leaves the mutual coherence intact.

The beam emerging from the retarder is then passed through a linear polarizer whose transmission axis makes an angle $\theta$ with the $x$-axis. The polarizer is rotated about the $z$-axis until the transmitted optical power is minimized. The retardation $\chi$ is then varied, and the polarizer orientation is adjusted accordingly, until the absolute minimum of the transmitted power is obtained.

The values $\chi_0, \theta_0$ at which this minimum occurs characterize the fully polarized component of the beam. The corresponding minimum transmitted power provides a measure of the unpolarized component.

At $z = 0$, the amplitude of the light emerging from the polarizer can be expressed as

$$
\begin{align*}
  E(t; \chi, \theta) =& \sum_n \left[A_n \cos(\theta) e^{i(2\pi f_n t + \phi_n)} \right. \\
  &+ \left. B_n \sin(\theta) e^{i(2\pi f_n t + \psi_n + \chi)} \right] \sqrt{\Delta f}
\end{align*}
$$

Because all frequencies have the form $f_n = (N_0 + n) \Delta f$, their differences are integer multiples of $\Delta f$

$$
  f_n - f_m = (n - m) \Delta f
$$

Thus, the transmitted amplitude $E(z=0, t)$ is periodic with period $T = 1/\Delta f$. The time-averaged transmitted intensity as a function of $\chi$ and $\theta$ is therefore given by

$$
  I(\chi, \theta) = \frac{1}{T} \int_0^T \Re[E (t;\chi, \theta)]^2 \;\d t
$$

All terms involving different frequencies vanish, because

$$
  \frac{1}{T} \int_0^T e^{i2\pi (f_n - f_m)t} \;\d t = \delta_{nm}
$$

Thus, only terms associated with the same spectral frequency survive, resulting in

$$
\begin{equation*}
  I(\chi, \theta) = \frac{\Delta f}{2} \sum_n \left[A_n^2 \cos^2 (\theta) + B_n^2 \sin^2 (\theta) + A_n B_n \sin(2\theta) \cos(\phi_n - \psi_n - \chi) \right]
\tag{\label{equation-10}}
\end{equation*}
$$

The factor $1/2$ arises from averaging the square of the real electric field over a complete period. This shows that there are no interference terms between different frequencies in the time-averaged intensity. The surviving terms is only between the $x$- and $y$-polarized components belonging to the same frequency $f_n$.
 
### Stokes parameters

The transmitted intensity $\eqref{equation-10}$ can be expressed conveniently in terms of the Stokes parameters associated with each spectral component. For the component at frequency $f_n$, define

$$
\begin{equation*}
\begin{split}
  S_{0, n} =& A_n^2 + B_n^2 \\
  S_{1, n} =& A_n^2 - B_n^2 \\
  S_{2, n} =& 2A_n B_n \cos(\phi_n - \psi_n) \\
  S_{3, n} =& 2A_n B_n \sin(\phi_n - \psi_n)
\end{split}
\tag{\label{equation-16}}
\end{equation*}
$$

Since each individual spectral component is assumed to be fully polarized, its Stokes parameters satisfy

$$
  S_{0,n}^2 = S_{1,n}^2 + S_{2,n}^2 + S_{3,n}^2
$$

The Stokes parameters of the complete polychromatic beam are obtained by summing over all spectral components. Defining the total Stokes parameters

$$
\begin{equation*}
  S_j = \Delta f \sum_n S_{j,n},\, j = 0,1,2,3
\tag{\label{equation-17}}
\end{equation*}
$$

the intensity $\eqref{equation-10}$ can be written as

$$
\begin{equation*}
  I(\chi, \theta) = \frac{1}{4} \sum_n [S_{0, n} + S_{1, n} \cos(2\theta) + (S_{2,n} \cos(\chi) + S_{3,n} \sin(\chi)) \sin(2\theta)]
\tag{\label{equation-11}}
\end{equation*}
$$

The factor $1/4$ arises from the normalization adopted here: the Stokes parameters are defined in terms of the complex field amplitudes, while the physical intensity is obtained from the time average of the square of the real electric field. With this convention, $S_0$ is proportional to the total incident intensity.

This form makes the role of the retarder particularly transparent. The frequency-independent retarder leaves $S_0$ and  $S_1$ unchanged, while mixing the $S_2$ and $S_3$ components through a rotation in the $S_2$-$S_3$ plane by an angle $\chi$.

To find the absolute minimum of the transmitted intensity, we first minimize with respect to $\chi$. The quantity in $\eqref{equation-11}$ that depends on $\chi$ is $S_2 \cos(\chi) + S_3 \sin(\chi)$. Introducing $R = \sqrt{S_2^2 + S_3^2}$, this expression can be written as

$$
\begin{equation*}
  S_2 \cos(\chi) + S_3 \sin(\chi) = R \cos(\chi - \chi_c)
\tag{\label{equation-12}}
\end{equation*}
$$

where $\chi_c = \operatorname{atan2}(S_3, S_2)$. The minimum value of $\eqref{equation-12}$ is $-R$. Thus, the retardation corresponding to the minimum is

$$
  \chi_0 = \operatorname{atan2} (S_3, S_2) + \pi \pmod 2\pi
$$

Equivalently,

$$
\begin{align*}
  \cos(\chi_0) =& -\frac{S_2}{R} \\
  \sin(\chi_0) =& -\frac{S_3}{R}
\end{align*}
$$

At this value of the retardation, the intensity becomes

$$
  I(\chi_0, \theta) = \frac{1}{4} [S_0 + S_1 \cos(2\theta) - R\sin(2\theta)]
$$

We can now minimize this expression with respect to the polarizer angle $\theta$. Since the two terms depending on $\theta$ form the projection of a two-dimensional vector, their minimum is $-\sqrt{S_1^2 + R^2}$. Using $R^2 = S_2^2 + S_3^2$, we obtain

$$
\begin{equation*}
  I_\text{min} = \frac{1}{4} \left(S_0 - \sqrt{S_1^2 + S_2^2 + S_3^2} \right)
\tag{\label{equation-13}}
\end{equation*}
$$

The corresponding polarizer angle can be specified by

$$
\begin{align*}
  \cos(2\theta_0) =& -\frac{S_1}{\sqrt{S_1^2 + S_2^2 + S_3^2}} \\
  \sin(2\theta_0) =& \frac{\sqrt{S_2^2 + S_3^2}}{\sqrt{S_1^2 + S_2^2 + S_3^2}}
\end{align*}
$$

The orthogonal polarizer orientation $\theta_0 + \pi/2$, gives the maximum transmitted intensity for the same retardation

$$
\begin{equation*}
  I_\text{max} = \frac{1}{4} \left(S_0 + \sqrt{S_1^2 + S_2^2 + S_3^2} \right)
\tag{\label{equation-14}}
\end{equation*}
$$

It is convenient to define the polarization-dependent Stokes vector $\mathbf{S}_p = (S_1, S_2, S_3)$, with magnitude $S_p = \sqrt{S_1^2 + S_2^2 + S_3^2}$. Using this notation, the minimum and maximum transmitted intensities take the compact form

$$
\begin{align*}
  I_\text{min} =& \frac{S_0 - S_p}{4} \\
  I_\text{max} =& \frac{S_0 + S_p}{4}
\end{align*}
$$

Consequently

$$
\begin{align*}
  I_\text{min} + I_\text{max} =& \frac{S_0}{2} \\
  I_\text{max} - I_\text{min} =& \frac{S_p}{2}
\end{align*}
$$

These relations show that, up to normalization, $S_0$ represents the total incident intensity, whereas $S_p$ represents the intensity of polarized component. The beam decomposes uniquely into a completely polarized component with intensity $S_p$ and an unpolarized component with intensity $S_0 - S_p$. The unpolarized component is invariant under ideal retarders and contributes equally to all polarizer orientations. Consequently, $I_\text{min}$ is the transmitted contribution of the unpolarized part, while $I_\text{max} - I_\text{min}$ is the transmitted intensity of the fully polarized part. The degree of polarization $P$ of the beam can therefore be defined as

$$
\begin{equation*}
  P = \frac{S_p}{S_0} = \frac{\sqrt{S_1^2 + S_2^2 + S_3^2}}{S_0},\, 0 \leq P \leq 1
\tag{\label{equation-15}}
\end{equation*}
$$

or, equivalently

$$
\begin{equation*}
  P = \frac{I_\text{max} - I_\text{min}}{I_\text{max} + I_\text{min}}
\end{equation*}
$$

The limiting cases have a direct physical interpretation. For $P = 1$, the beam is completely polarized and $S_p = S_0$, so $I_\text{min} = 0$ and $I_\text{max} = S_0 /2$. For $P = 0$, the beam is completely unpolarized, so $S_p = 0$, and $I_\text{min} = I_\text{max} = S_0 /4$. Thus, a completely polarized beam can in principle be extinguished by a suitable combination of retarder and polarizer, whereas a completely unpolarized beam produces the same transmitted intensity for every choice of retardation and polarizer orientation.

For a completely unpolarized beam, no combination of an ideal retarder and a linear polarizer can produce a dependence of the transmitted power on either the retardation $\chi$ or the polarizer orientation $\theta$. The transmitted power is always one-half of the incident power. In terms of the Stokes parameters, a completely unpolarized beam is characterized by $S_1 = S_2 = S_3 = 0$.

In particular, $S_1 = 0$ implies

$$
  \sum_n A_n^2 \Delta f = \sum_n B_n^2 \Delta f
$$

Thus, the total powers carried by the $x$- and $y$-polarized components are equal. However, equality of these two powers alone is not sufficient to establish complete unpolarization; the condition $S_2 = S_3 = 0$ must also be satisfied.

For natural light, the $x$- and $y$-polarized components may be regarded as statistically independent. Consequently, their relative phases $\delta_n = \phi_n - \psi_n$ are random, and in the idealized model, uniformly distributed over $[0, 2\pi)$. The corresponding contributions to $S_2$ and $S_3$ therefore cancel upon averaging. In the continuum limit $\Delta f \to 0$, we obtain

$$
  S_2 = 2 \sum_n A_n B_n \cos(\delta_n) \Delta f \xrightarrow{\Delta f \to 0} 0
$$

and

$$
  S_3 = 2 \sum_n A_n B_n \sin(\delta_n) \Delta f \xrightarrow{\Delta f \to 0} 0
$$

Thus, for natural light, the condition $S_1 = S_2 = S_3 = 0$ is satisfied, and the beam is completely unpolarized.

It is important to note, however, that statistical randomness of the relative phases is not a necessary condition for complete unpolarization. Deterministic or highly structured combinations of polarized spectral components can also produce completely unpolarized light, as long as the ensemble-averaged Stokes parameters $S_1$, $S_2$ and $S_3$ vanish.

As a simple example, consider a beam composed of two spectral components at distinct frequencies $f_1$ and $f_2$, with equal powers, such that one component is right-circularly polarized and the other is left-circularly polarized. For the right- and left-circular components, the corresponding Stokes vectors have equal $S_0$ and opposite $S_3$, while $S_1 = S_2 = 0$. Hence, when the two spectral components are combined yields $S_1 = S_2 = S_3 = 0$, while $S_0 > 0$. The resulting beam is therefore completely unpolarized, even though each individual spectral component is itself fully polarized.

### The Poincaré sphere

From the definition of the Stokes parameters $\eqref{equation-16}$ and $\eqref{equation-17}$, a fully polarized beam satisfies

$$
\begin{equation*}
  S_0^2 = S_1^2 + S_2^2 + S_3^2
\tag{\label{equation-18}}
\end{equation*}
$$

This equation defines a sphere in the a three-dimensional Stokes space with coordinates $(S_1, S_2, S_3)$ and radius $S_0$. The sphere is known as the Poincaré sphere. More generally, the polarized Stokes vectors $\mathbf{S}_p = (S_1, S_2, S_3)$ satisfy

$$
  S_1^2 + S_2^2 + S_3^2 = S_p^2 \leq S_0^2
$$

From $\eqref{equation-15}$, the magnitude of the polarized Stokes vector $S_p$ is related to the degree of polarization $P$ via

$$
  S_p = PS_0
$$

Thus, $P$ determines the radial position of $\mathbf{S}_p$ within the Poincaré sphere:
- fully polarized states with $P = 1$ has $S_p = S_0$ and lie on the surface of the Poincaré sphere
- partially polarized states with $0 < P < 1$ has $0 < S_p < S_0$ lie inside the Poincaré sphere
- the fully unpolarized state with $P = 0$ has $S_p = 0$ and is represented by the origin 

For the polarized component, let $\eta$ denote the ellipticity angle and $\rho$ the orientation angle of the major axis of the polarization ellipse, measured with respect to the $x$-axis. The standard relationship between these angles and the Stokes parameters are

$$
\begin{align*}
  \sin(2\eta) =& \frac{S_3}{\sqrt{S_1^2 + S_2^2 + S_3^2}} \tag{\label{equation-20}} \\
  2\rho = \operatorname{atan2}(S_1, S_1)
\end{align*}
$$

Using $\eqref{equation-19}$, the relation $\eqref{equation-20}$ gives

$$
  S_3 = S_p \sin(2\eta)
$$

Substituting these identities into $\eqref{eqution-18}$ yields

$$
  S_0^2 = S_1^2 + S_1^2 \tan^2 (2\rho) + S_0^2 \sin^2 (2\eta)
$$

Factorizing gives

$$
  S_0^2 (1 - \sin^2 (2\eta)) = S_1^2 (1 + \tan^2 (2\rho))
$$

or

$$
  S_0^2 \cos^2 (2\eta) = \frac{S_1^2}{\cos^2 (2\rho)}
$$

Thus, the three Stokes parameters of the polarized component are given by

$$
\begin{align*}
  S_1 =& S_p \cos(2\eta) \cos(2\rho) \\
  S_2 =& S_p \cos(2\eta) \sin(2\rho) \\
  S_3 =& S_p \sin(2\eta)
\end{align*}
$$

The factor of two in the angles is a characteristic feature of the Poincaré-sphere representation: a physical rotation of the polarization ellipse by $\rho$ corresponds to an azimuthal rotation of $2\rho$ on the sphere, while the ellipticity angel $\eta$ corresponds to a latitude $2\eta$.

# Diffraction

## Huygens-Fresnel principle

The Huygens-Fresnel principle states that every point on a wavefront is itself the source of spherical wavelets, and the secondary wavelets emanating from different points mutually interfere. The propagation of each wavelet can be described by a complex amplitude $U: \R^3 \times \R \to \mathbb{C}$ given by

$$
  U(\mathbf{r}, t) = \frac{U_0}{r}e^{i\left(\omega t - \mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r} \right)}
$$

According to the Rayleigh-Sommerfeld diffraction theory, the Huygens-Fresnel principle can be formulated as the superposition of spherical wavelets emanating from within an aperture region $\Sigma$

$$
  U(P) = \frac{1}{i\lambda} \int_\Sigma U(\sigma) \frac{e^{ikr}}{r} \cos\theta \d s
$$

Applying Cartesian coordinates such that the aperture plane $\xi \eta$ and observation plane $xy$ are orthogonal to the $z$ axis, the obliquity angle $\theta$ is given by

$$
  \cos\theta = \frac{z}{r}
$$

and the distance $r$ is

$$
  r = \sqrt{z^2 + \left(x - \xi \right)^2 + (y - \eta)^2}
$$

## Kirchhoff's integral theorem

Kirchhoff's integral theorem provides a formulation for the diffraction problem in terms of a surface integral.

The field propagation monochromatic spherical scalar wave is of the form

$$
  A(\mathbf{r}, t) = U(\mathbf{r})e^{-i\omega t}
$$

Inserting into the wave equation $\nabla^2 A - \frac{1}{v^2}\frac{\partial^2 A}{\partial t^2} = 0$ yields the Helmholtz equation

$$
\begin{gather*}
  \nabla^2 \left[ U e^{-i\omega t} \right] = \frac{\left(-i\omega \right)^2}{v^2} U e^{-i\omega t} \\
  \nabla^2 U(\mathbf{r}) = -\frac{\omega^2}{v^2} U(\mathbf{r}) = -k^2 U(\mathbf{r})
\end{gather*}
$$

Considering a monochromatic scalar wave function $U$ and an impulse response function $G$ with continuous first and second order derivatives. Applying Green's second indentity gives

$$
  \iiint_\mathcal{V} \left( U \nabla^2 G - G \nabla^2 U  \right) \d V = -\oint_\mathcal{S} \left( U \frac{\partial G}{\partial \hat{\mathbf{n}}} - G \frac{\partial U}{\partial \hat{\mathbf{n}}} \right) \d S
$$

Since $\nabla^2 U = -k^2$ and $\nabla^2 G = -k^2$, the volume integral vanishes, leaving 

$$
  \oint_\mathcal{S} \left( U \frac{\partial G}{\partial \hat{\mathbf{n}}} - G \frac{\partial U}{\partial \hat{\mathbf{n}}} \right) \d S = \oint_\mathcal{S} \left( U \nabla_n G - G \nabla_n G \right) \d\mathbf{S} = 0
$$

The impulse response function $G$ can be choosen as the Green's function $G(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{e^{iks}}{s}$ where $s$ is the distance from an observation point $P$ in the closed volume $\mathcal{V}$. Since there is a singularity for $G$ at $P$, the integral surface must not include $P$. A suggested integral surface is an inner sphere $\mathcal{S}_\epsilon$ centered at $P$ with the radius of $\epsilon$ and an outer arbitrary closed surface $\mathcal{S}$. The surface integral becomes

$$
  \oint_\mathcal{S_\epsilon} \left( U \nabla_n \frac{e^{ik\epsilon}}{\epsilon} - \frac{e^{ik\epsilon}}{\epsilon} \nabla_n U \right) \d S + \oint_\mathcal{S} \left( U \nabla_n \frac{e^{iks}}{s} - \frac{e^{iks}}{s} \nabla_n U \right) \d S = 0 \tag{1}
$$

For the integral on the inner sphere $\mathcal{S}_\epsilon$

$$
  \frac{\partial}{\partial\hat{\mathbf{n}}}\frac{e^{ik\epsilon}}{\epsilon} = \nabla \frac{e^{ik\epsilon}}{\epsilon} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}} = \left( \frac{ik}{\epsilon} - \frac{1}{\epsilon} \right)e^{ik\epsilon}
$$

and by introducing the solid angle $\d\Omega$ given by $\d S = \epsilon^2 \d\Omega$

$$
\begin{align*}
  \oint_\mathcal{S_\epsilon} \left( U \nabla_n \frac{e^{ik\epsilon}}{\epsilon} - \frac{e^{ik\epsilon}}{\epsilon} \nabla_n U \right) \d S &= \oint_\mathcal{S_\epsilon} \left[ u \left( \frac{ik}{\epsilon} - \frac{1}{\epsilon} \right)e^{ik\epsilon} - \frac{e^{ik\epsilon}}{\epsilon} \frac{\partial}{\partial \hat{\mathbf{n}}}U \right] \epsilon^2 \d\Omega \\
  &= \oint_\mathcal{S_\epsilon} \left(ik\epsilon U - U - \epsilon\frac{\partial}{\partial \hat{\mathbf{n}}}U \right)e^{ik\epsilon} \d\Omega
\end{align*}
$$

Letting $\epsilon \to 0$ gives

$$
  \lim_{\epsilon \to 0} \oint_\mathcal{S_\epsilon} \left(ik\epsilon U - U - \epsilon\frac{\partial}{\partial \hat{\mathbf{n}}}U \right)e^{ik\epsilon} \d\Omega = -\oint_\mathcal{S_\epsilon} U \d\Omega = -4\pi U(\mathbf{r})
$$

Inserting back into eq. 1 gives Kirchoff's integral theorem

$$
\begin{align*}
  U(\mathbf{r}) &= \frac{1}{4\pi} \oint_\mathcal{S}\left( U \frac{\partial}{\partial \hat{\mathbf{n}}} \frac{e^{iks}}{s} - \frac{e^{iks}}{s} \frac{\partial}{\partial \hat{\mathbf{n}}} U \right) \d S \\
  &= \frac{1}{4\pi} \oint_\mathcal{S}\left[ U\nabla\left( \frac{e^{iks}}{s} \right) - \frac{e^{iks}}{s}\nabla U \right]\cdot \, \d\mathbf{S}
\end{align*}
$$

## Kirchhoff's diffraction formula

The field distribution of a monochromatic spherical wave emitted from a point source $P_0$ is given by

$$
  U(r) = \frac{ae^{ikr}}{r}
$$

Since the intensity of the wave falls of as the inverse square of the distance, the amplitude falls off as the inverse of the distance. 

The wave amplitude at an observation point $P$ can be found by applying Kirchhoff's integral theorem to the closed surface formed by the intersection of a sphere with the screen.

$$
  U(P) = \frac{1}{4\pi} \left[\int_\Sigma + \int_{S_1} + \int_{S_2} \right] \left( U \frac{\partial}{\partial n} \frac{e^{iks}}{s} - \frac{e^{iks}}{s}\frac{\partial U}{\partial n} \right)\d S
$$

To solve the equation, Kirchhoff's boundary conditions are applied, assuming
1. The values of $U$ and $\frac{\partial U_\Sigma}{\partial \hat{\mathbf{n}}}$ in the aperture area $\Sigma$ are the same as when the screen is not present
2. The values of $U$ and $\frac{\partial U_\Sigma}{\partial \hat{\mathbf{n}}}$ are zero on the screen $S_1$. This implies that $U$ is discontinuous at the edge of the aperture. A solution of the wave equation that vanishes on any finite surface element, must also vanish in all space. This boundary condition therefore implies that the fields are zero everywhere behind the aperture, which is contradicts reality.

The first assumption implies

$$
\begin{gather*}
  U_\Sigma = \frac{ae^{ikr}}{r} \\
  \frac{\partial U_\Sigma}{\partial \hat{\mathbf{n}}} = \nabla U_\Sigma \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}} = \frac{ae^{ikr}}{r}\left(ik - \frac{1}{r}\right) \cos(\hat{\mathbf{n}}, \mathbf{r})
\end{gather*}
$$

At $Q$, we also have

$$
  \frac{\partial}{\partial \hat{\mathbf{n}}} \left( \frac{e^{iks}}{s} \right) = \frac{e^{iks}}{s}\left(ik - \frac{1}{s}\right) \cos(\hat{\mathbf{n}}, \mathbf{s})
$$

It is assumed that the distance between observation point $P$ and the integral area, and the dimension of the aperture are much greater than the wavelength $\lambda$. Thus the inverse terms $\frac{1}{r}$ and $\frac{1}{s}$ are negligible compared with the wavenumber $k = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}$.

In the limit $R \to \infty$, it can be shown that the integral over the hemisphere $S_2$ vanishes. In this limit

$$
  \lim_{R \to \infty} \frac{\partial}{\partial \hat{\mathbf{n}}} \left( \frac{e^{ikR}}{R} \right) = \lim_{R \to \infty} \frac{e^{ikR}}{R}\left(ik - \frac{1}{R}\right) = \frac{ik}{R}e^{ikR}
$$

and the integral becomes

$$
  \iint_{S_2} \left(\frac{e^{ikR}}{R} \frac{\partial U}{\partial \hat{\mathbf{n}}} - U ik \frac{e^{ikR}}{R} \right) \d S_2 = \iint_{S_2} e^{ikr} \left( \frac{\partial U}{\partial \hat{\mathbf{n}}} - ikU \right) R \d\Omega = 0
$$

where $\d\Omega$ is the solid angle given by $\d S_2 = R^2 \d\Omega$. The quantity $|RG|$ is uniformly bounded on $S_2$ and the integral will vanish as $R$ becomes arbitrarily large, provided the amplitude has the property

$$
  \lim_{R\to\infty} R \left( \frac{\partial U}{\partial \hat{\mathbf{n}}} - ikU \right) = 0
$$

which is known as the Sommerfeld radiation condition.

Given that the Sommerfeld radiation condition is satisfied, the amplitude at $P$ becomes

$$
  U(P) = -\frac{ia}{2\lambda}\iint_{\Sigma} \frac{e^{ik(r+s)}}{rs} \left[ \cos(\hat{\mathbf{n}}, \mathbf{r}) - \cos(\hat{\mathbf{n}}, \mathbf{s}) \right] \d\sigma
$$

which is known as Kirchhoff's diffraction formula.

## Rayleigh-Sommerfeld diffraction formula

Kirchhoff's diffraction theory can be modified with alternative Green's functions that vanish in the aperture area, thus removing the necessity of the inconsistent Kirchhoff boundary conditions.

This can be accomplished by introducing a mirrored source point $P'_0$ on the opposite side of the screen. Suppose the two sources $P_0$ and $P'_0$ are emitting with the same wavelength, but oscillation out phase (phase difference of $\pi$). The Green's function in this case is

$$
  G_{-} (\mathbf{r}) = \frac{e^{ikr}}{r} - \frac{e^{ikr'}}{r'} 
$$

Clearly the function vanishes on the aperture $\Sigma$, leaving the following expression for the field distribution

$$
  U_{-}(P) = -\frac{1}{4\pi}\iint_{\Sigma} U \frac{\partial G_{-}}{\partial \hat{\mathbf{n}}} \d\sigma
$$

which is called the first Rayleigh-Sommerfeld solution. On the aperture $r = r'$ and $\cos(\hat{\mathbf{n}}, \mathbf{r}) - \cos(\hat{\mathbf{n}}, \mathbf{r}')$, so that

$$
\begin{align*}
  \left.\frac{\partial G_{-}}{\partial \hat{\mathbf{n}}}\right|_\Sigma &= \left(ik - \frac{1}{r} \right)\frac{e^{ikr}}{r}\cos(\hat{\mathbf{n}}, \mathbf{r}) - \left(ik - \frac{1}{r'} \right)\frac{e^{ikr'}}{r'}\cos(\hat{\mathbf{n}}, \mathbf{r}') \\
  &= 2 \left(ik - \frac{1}{r} \right)\frac{e^{ikr}}{r}\cos(\hat{\mathbf{n}}, \mathbf{r}) \\
  &= 2 \left.\frac{\partial G}{\partial \hat{\mathbf{n}}} \right|_\Sigma
\end{align*}
$$

where $G$ is the Green's function used in Kirchhoff's diffraction theory. The first Rayleigh-Sommerfeld solution can thus be rewritten as

$$
  U_{-}(P) = -\frac{1}{2\pi}\iint_\Sigma U\frac{\partial G}{\partial \hat{\mathbf{n}}} \d\sigma
$$

Applying Kirchhoff's integral theorem with the Green's function $G_-$ it follows that (assuming $r \gg \lambda$)

$$
  U_-(P) = \frac{1}{i\lambda} \iint_\Sigma U(\sigma) \frac{e^{ikr}}{r} \cos(\hat{\mathbf{n}}, \mathbf{r}) \;\d\sigma
$$

For a monochromatic spherical wave

$$
\begin{gather*}
  U(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{a}{r}e^{ikr} \\
  \frac{\partial U}{\partial \hat{\mathbf{n}}} = \left[ik - \frac{1}{r} \right]\frac{e^{ikr}}{r}\cos(\hat{\mathbf{n}}, \mathbf{r})
\end{gather*}
$$

we obtain

$$
  U_-(P) = \frac{a}{i\lambda}\iint_\Sigma \frac{e^{ik(r + s)}}{rs}\cos\left(\hat{\mathbf{n}}, \mathbf{s} \right)
$$

which is known as the Rayleigh-Sommerfeld diffraction formula. The formula can be interpreted as the superposition of spherical wavelets emanating from secondary sources within the aperture. This is consistent with the Huygens-Fresnel principle.

### Rayleigh-Sommerfeld's second solution

An alternative and equally valid Green's function is found by allowing the two point sources to oscillate in phase

$$
  G_+ (\mathbf{r}) = \frac{e^{ikr}}{r} + \frac{e^{ikr'}}{r'}
$$

The normal derivative of this function vanishes across the screen and aperture, leading to the second Rayleigh-Sommerfeld solution

$$
  U_{+}(P) = \frac{1}{4\pi}\iint_{\Sigma} G_+ \frac{\partial U}{\partial \hat{\mathbf{n}}} \d\sigma
$$

It can similarly be rewritten in terms of the Green's function used by Kirchhoff

$$
  U_{+}(P) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\iint_\Sigma G\frac{\partial U}{\partial \hat{\mathbf{n}}} \d\sigma
$$

Applying the Kirchhoff's integral theorem

$$
  U_-(P) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \iint_\Sigma \frac{U(\sigma)}{\partial \hat{\mathbf{n}}} \frac{e^{ikr}}{r} \;\d\sigma
$$

### Relation to Kirchhoff's diffraction theory

Kirchhoff's solution is the arithmetic average of the two Rayleigh-Sommerfeld solutions

$$
\begin{align*}
  U(P) &= \frac{1}{4\pi} \iint_\Sigma \left( \frac{\partial U}{\partial \hat{\mathbf{n}}}G - U\frac{\partial G}{\partial \hat{\mathbf{n}}} \right) \d\sigma \\
  &= \frac{1}{2}\left[ \frac{1}{2\pi}\iint_\Sigma G \frac{\partial U}{\partial \hat{\mathbf{n}}} \d\sigma - \frac{1}{2\pi}\iint_\Sigma U\frac{\partial G}{\partial \hat{\mathbf{n}}} \d\sigma \right] \\
  &= \frac{1}{2}\left[U_+(P) + U_-(P) \right]
\end{align*}
$$

The Rayleigh-Sommerfeld diffraction formula differs from Kirchhoff's through what is known as the obliquity factor $\psi$, which is the angular dependence introduced by the cosine terms. In the most general case

$$
  U(P) = \frac{a}{i\lambda}\iint_\Sigma \frac{e^{ik(r + s)}}{rs}\psi \;\d\sigma
$$

where

$$
  \psi = \begin{cases} \frac{1}{2}\left[ \cos\left(\hat{\mathbf{n}}, \mathbf{r}\right) - \cos\left(\hat{\mathbf{n}}, \mathbf{s}\right) \right] &\quad \textrm{Kirchhoff theory} \\ \cos\left(\hat{\mathbf{n}}, \mathbf{s} \right) &\quad \textrm{First Rayleigh-Sommerfeld solution} \\
  -\cos\left(\hat{\mathbf{n}}, \mathbf{r} \right) &\quad \textrm{Second Rayleigh-Sommerfeld solution}
  \end{cases}
$$

## Fresnel diffraction (near field)

The Fresnel approximation to the Huygens-Fresnel principle is derived by applying the binomial expansion of the aperture distance

$$
\begin{align*}
  r &= \sqrt{z^2 + \left(x - \xi \right)^2 + \left( y - \eta \right)^2} = \sqrt{z + \rho^2} \\
  &= z\sqrt{1 + \frac{\rho^2}{z^2} ^2} \\
  &= z\left[ 1 + \frac{\rho^2}{2z^2} - \frac{1}{8}\left(\frac{\rho^2}{z^2} \right)^2 + \dots \right]
\end{align*}
$$

The distance can be approximated to first order if the higher order terms have negligible contributions to the complex exponential factor $e^{ikr}$. In other words, the higher order terms have to be much smaller than the period of the complex exponential

$$
\begin{gather*}
  k\frac{\rho^4}{8z^3} \ll 2\pi \\
  \frac{\rho^4}{z^3 \lambda} \ll 8 \\
  \frac{\rho^4}{\lambda^4} = \frac{1}{\lambda^4} \left[ \left(x - \xi \right)^2 + \left( y - \eta \right)^2 \right]^2 \ll 8\frac{z^3}{\lambda^3}
\end{gather*}
$$

For applications involving optical wavelengths

$$
\begin{align*}
  \lambda &\ll z \\
  \lambda &\ll \rho
\end{align*}
$$

The Fresnel approximation will thus generally hold if $\rho \ll z$. The resulting expression for the field at the observation plane becomes

$$
    U(x, y) = \frac{e^{ikz}}{i\lambda z} \iint_{-\infty}^{\infty} U\left(\xi, \eta \right) e^{i\frac{k}{2z} \left[ \left(x - \xi \right)^2 + \left( y - \eta \right)^2  \right]} \d\xi \;\d\eta
$$

The integral can be expressed as a convolution

$$
  U(x, y) = \iint_{-\infty}^{\infty} U\left(\xi, \eta \right) h\left(x - \xi, y - \eta \right) \d\xi \;\d\eta
$$

where the convolution kernel is

$$
  h(x, y) = \frac{e^{ikz}}{i\lambda z} e^{i\frac{k}{2z}\left(x^2 + y^2 \right)}
$$

The integral can also be expressed as a Fourier transform

$$
\begin{align*}
  U(x, y) &= \frac{e^{ikz}}{i\lambda z} e^{i\frac{k}{2z} \left(x^2 + y^2 \right)} \iint_{-\infty}^{\infty} \left[ U\left(\xi, \eta \right) e^{i\frac{k}{2z} \left(\xi^2 + \eta^2 \right)} \right]e^{-i\frac{2\pi}{\lambda z}\left(x\xi + y\eta \right)} \d\xi \;\d\eta \\
  &= \frac{e^{ikz}}{i\lambda z} e^{i\frac{k}{2z} \left(x^2 + y^2 \right)} \mathcal{F}\left\{ U\left(\xi, \eta \right) e^{-i\frac{2\pi}{\lambda z}\left(x\xi + y\eta \right)} \right\}
\end{align*}
$$

## Fraunhofer diffraction (far field)

The Fraunhofer approximation is more stringent than the Fresnel approximation in that it also requires

$$
  z \gg \frac{k}{2}\max{\left(\xi^2 + \eta^2 \right)} = \frac{\pi}{\lambda} \max{\left(\xi^2 + \eta^2 \right)}
$$

If this is satisfied then the quadratic phase factor in the Fourier formulation of Fresnel diffraction is approximately unity over the entire aperture

$$
  U(x, y) = \frac{e^{ikz}}{i\lambda z} e^{i\frac{k}{2z} \left(x^2 + y^2 \right)} \iint_{-\infty}^\infty U(\xi, \eta) e^{-i\frac{2\pi}{\lambda z}\left(x\xi + y\eta \right)} \d\xi \;\d\eta
$$

The integral can be expressed as Fourier transform of the aperture distribution evaluated at frequencies

$$
\begin{align*}
  f_x = \frac{x}{\lambda z} &= \frac{l}{\lambda} \\
  f_y = \frac{y}{\lambda z} &= \frac{m}{\lambda} \\
\end{align*}
$$

The integral can be rewritten as

$$
  U(x, y) = \frac{e^{ikz}}{i\lambda z} e^{i\frac{k}{2z} \left(x^2 + y^2 \right)} \mathcal{F}\left\{ U(\xi, \eta) \right\}_{f_x f_y}
$$
